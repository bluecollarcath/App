## Integrating to infinity

Many engineering quantities involve an infinite range: the total energy of a decaying pulse, the total charge in a discharge that never quite finishes, the Laplace transform. An **improper integral** is defined as a limit:

$$
\int_a^\infty f(t)\,dt = \lim_{b\to\infty}\int_a^bf(t)\,dt
$$

If the limit exists, the integral **converges**; otherwise it **diverges**. The same idea handles integrands that blow up at a point, such as $\int_0^1 dx/\sqrt x$.

## The two key families

**Exponentials always converge** (for decaying ones):

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-at}\,dt = \frac1a \quad (a > 0)
$$

**Powers converge only if they fall fast enough:**

$$
\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^p} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{p - 1} & p > 1 \\ \text{diverges} & p \le 1 \end{cases}
$$

The borderline case $\int_1^\infty dx/x = \lim\ln b$ diverges, though very slowly. Physically: the electric potential of a point charge ($1/r$) needs a reference, but its field energy outside any radius ($\propto\int dr/r^2$) is finite.

## Engineering examples

> **Total charge.** A capacitor discharges with $i(t) = I_0e^{-t/\tau}$. Total charge delivered: $\int_0^\infty I_0e^{-t/\tau}\,dt = I_0\tau$. With $I_0 = 10$ mA and $\tau = 2$ ms: 20 µC, exactly $CV_0$ as it must be.

> **Energy in a pulse.** A voltage $v = V_0e^{-t/\tau}$ across a resistor $R$ delivers
> $$W = \int_0^\infty\frac{V_0^2e^{-2t/\tau}}{R}\,dt = \frac{V_0^2\tau}{2R}$$
> For $V_0 = 10$ V, $\tau = 1$ ms, $R = 100\ \Omega$: 0.5 mJ.

> **The Gaussian integral** $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt\pi$ normalizes the bell curve of probability and noise (Probability, Lesson 1.3).

## Preview: the Laplace transform

Multiply a signal by $e^{-st}$ and integrate to infinity:

$$
F(s) = \int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\,dt
$$

For each value of $s$, it's an improper integral that converges as long as $e^{-st}$ decays faster than $f(t)$ grows.

> $$\mathcal L\{1\} = \int_0^\infty e^{-st}\,dt = \frac1s, \qquad \mathcal L\{e^{-at}\} = \int_0^\infty e^{-(s+a)t}\,dt = \frac{1}{s + a}$$

These two results, and the partial fractions of Lesson 2.2, are most of what you need to solve circuit transients with Laplace transforms (Circuit Analysis II, Signals & Systems).

## Key takeaways

- Improper integrals are limits; they converge only if the integrand dies off fast enough.
- $\int_0^\infty e^{-at}dt = 1/a$; $\int_1^\infty x^{-p}dx$ converges only for $p > 1$.
- Total charge $I_0\tau$; pulse energy $V_0^2\tau/2R$.
- The Laplace transform is an improper integral: $\mathcal L\{e^{-at}\} = 1/(s + a)$.
