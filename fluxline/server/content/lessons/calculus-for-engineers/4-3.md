## Integrating along curves and across surfaces

Two more kinds of integral appear throughout electromagnetics: integrating a vector field **along a path** (work, voltage, circulation) and **through a surface** (flux).

## Line integrals

To integrate a field $\mathbf F$ along a curve $C$, **parametrize** the curve as $\mathbf r(t)$ for $a \le t \le b$. Then $d\mathbf r = \mathbf r'(t)\,dt$ and

$$
\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_a^b\mathbf F\big(\mathbf r(t)\big)\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt
$$

Only the field component **along** the path contributes.

- **Work:** a force field $\mathbf F$ does work $W = \int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$ on a particle moving along $C$.
- **Voltage:** $V_{ab} = -\int_b^a\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf r$.
- **Circulation** around a closed loop, $\oint\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$: Faraday's law (EMF) and Ampère's law.

> **Circulation.** $\mathbf F = -y\,\hat{\mathbf x} + x\,\hat{\mathbf y}$ around the unit circle, $\mathbf r(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$, $0 \le t \le 2\pi$: $\mathbf r' = (-\sin t, \cos t)$, so $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf r' = \sin^2t + \cos^2t = 1$, and $\oint = 2\pi$.

## Conservative fields

If $\mathbf F = \nabla\phi$ for some scalar **potential** $\phi$, the line integral depends only on the endpoints:

$$
\int_A^B\nabla\phi\cdot d\mathbf r = \phi(B) - \phi(A)
$$

so the circulation around any closed path is zero. Electrostatic fields are conservative, which is why voltage between two points is well defined and why KVL works. (Changing magnetic fields break this: Electromagnetics, Lesson 3.1.) A field is conservative if its curl is zero (next lesson).

> $\mathbf F = y\,\hat{\mathbf x} + x\,\hat{\mathbf y} = \nabla(xy)$. The work from $(0, 0)$ to $(2, 3)$ along **any** path is $xy\big|_{(0,0)}^{(2,3)} = 6$.

## Surface integrals and flux

The **flux** of a field through a surface $S$ counts how much of it passes through:

$$
\Phi = \iint_S\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf S = \iint_S\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dA
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is the unit normal (for a closed surface, pointing outward). Only the normal component counts.

> **Flat surface.** $\mathbf F = z\,\hat{\mathbf z}$ through the unit square at height $z = 2$ (normal $\hat{\mathbf z}$): $\Phi = 2 \times 1 = 2$.

> **Sphere.** The position field $\mathbf F = \mathbf r$ is everywhere normal to a sphere of radius $R$, with magnitude $R$, so $\Phi = R \times 4\pi R^2 = 4\pi R^3$.

> **Gauss's law** is a flux statement: the electric flux out of a closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by $\varepsilon_0$. For a point charge, $E \cdot 4\pi r^2 = Q/\varepsilon_0$ gives Coulomb's law immediately.

General surfaces are parametrized by two variables $(u, v)$, with $d\mathbf S = (\mathbf r_u \times \mathbf r_v)\,du\,dv$; symmetric cases like the ones above avoid that work entirely.

## Key takeaways

- Line integral: parametrize, then $\int\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))\cdot\mathbf r'(t)\,dt$; gives work, voltage and circulation.
- Conservative fields ($\mathbf F = \nabla\phi$): path-independent integrals, zero circulation.
- Flux $\iint\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dA$; use symmetry wherever possible (Gauss's law).
