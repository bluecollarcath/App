## Numbers with two parts

AC circuit analysis, signal processing, control and communications all run on **complex numbers**. Engineers write the imaginary unit as $j$ ($j^2 = -1$), since $i$ means current.

A complex number has two equivalent forms:

$$
z = x + jy = r\angle\theta = re^{j\theta}, \qquad r = |z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad \theta = \angle z = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x)
$$

and back: $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$. Plotted in the complex plane, $x$ is the horizontal coordinate, $y$ the vertical, $r$ the distance from the origin and $\theta$ the angle.

> $3 + j4$: $r = 5$, $\theta = \tan^{-1}(4/3) = 53.13°$, so $3 + j4 = 5\angle53.13°$.
>
> $-1 + j$: the second quadrant, so $\theta = 135°$ (a calculator's plain $\tan^{-1}(1/{-1}) = -45°$ is wrong here; use atan2 or check the quadrant).

## Arithmetic

- **Add and subtract** in rectangular form: $(a + jb) + (c + jd) = (a + c) + j(b + d)$.
- **Multiply and divide** in polar form: magnitudes multiply, angles add.
$$r_1\angle\theta_1 \times r_2\angle\theta_2 = r_1r_2\angle(\theta_1 + \theta_2), \qquad \frac{r_1\angle\theta_1}{r_2\angle\theta_2} = \frac{r_1}{r_2}\angle(\theta_1 - \theta_2)$$
- **Conjugate:** $z^* = x - jy = r\angle{-\theta}$; $zz^* = |z|^2$. Dividing in rectangular form uses it: $\dfrac{1}{1 + j} = \dfrac{1 - j}{(1 + j)(1 - j)} = \dfrac{1 - j}{2}$.

## Euler's formula

$$
e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta
$$

It follows from the Taylor series of Lesson 3.2: substitute $j\theta$ into the series for $e^x$ and separate the real and imaginary terms; they're exactly the series for cosine and sine. Its consequences:

- $e^{j\theta}$ is a unit-length arrow at angle $\theta$; **multiplying by $e^{j\theta}$ rotates** by $\theta$. Multiplying by $j = e^{j\pi/2}$ rotates by 90°.
- $\cos\theta = \dfrac{e^{j\theta} + e^{-j\theta}}{2}$, $\sin\theta = \dfrac{e^{j\theta} - e^{-j\theta}}{2j}$.
- $e^{j\pi} + 1 = 0$.

**Phasors.** A sinusoid $A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ is the real part of $Ae^{j\phi}e^{j\omega t}$. Since every voltage and current in a linear AC circuit shares the $e^{j\omega t}$ factor, it can be dropped, leaving the **phasor** $A\angle\phi$. Differentiation becomes multiplication by $j\omega$, so differential equations become algebra: the foundation of Circuit Analysis II.

## Powers and roots

De Moivre: $(re^{j\theta})^n = r^ne^{jn\theta}$. The equation $z^n = 1$ has $n$ solutions, the **roots of unity**, equally spaced around the unit circle:

$$
z_k = e^{j2\pi k/n}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, n - 1
$$

> The cube roots of 1 are $1$, $1\angle120°$ and $1\angle240°$: the operator $a$ of three-phase power (Power Systems, Lesson 3.3). The $N$th roots of unity are also the "twiddle factors" of the FFT (DSP, Lesson 2.2).

## Key takeaways

- $z = x + jy = re^{j\theta}$; add in rectangular form, multiply and divide in polar form; mind the quadrant.
- Euler: $e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta$; multiplying by $e^{j\theta}$ rotates.
- Phasors turn sinusoidal steady-state analysis into complex algebra ($d/dt \to j\omega$).
- Roots of unity are equally spaced on the unit circle: three-phase operators and FFT twiddle factors.
