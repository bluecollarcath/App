## One number for a whole waveform

A varying signal often needs to be summarized by a single number. Two summaries matter most, and both are integrals over one period $T$.

**Average (DC) value:**

$$
V_{avg} = \frac1T\int_0^Tv(t)\,dt
$$

It's what a DC voltmeter reads, and what remains after a lowpass filter. A pure sinusoid averages to zero.

**RMS (root-mean-square) value:**

$$
V_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac1T\int_0^Tv^2(t)\,dt}
$$

It's the DC voltage that would deliver the **same power** to a resistor, since power goes as $v^2/R$. "120 V" household power is an RMS value; its peak is 170 V.

## RMS of a sinusoid

For $v = V_p\sin\omega t$, use $\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 - \cos2\theta)$. The cosine term averages to zero over a period, leaving

$$
\frac1T\int_0^TV_p^2\sin^2\omega t\,dt = \frac{V_p^2}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_{rms} = \frac{V_p}{\sqrt2} = 0.707V_p
$$

## A table of common waveforms

| Waveform (peak $A$) | Average | RMS |
|---|---|---|
| Sine | 0 | $A/\sqrt2$ |
| Full-wave rectified sine | $2A/\pi$ | $A/\sqrt2$ |
| Half-wave rectified sine | $A/\pi$ | $A/2$ |
| Square wave, $\pm A$ | 0 | $A$ |
| Triangle or sawtooth, $\pm A$ | 0 | $A/\sqrt3$ |
| Pulse train, 0 to $A$, duty $D$ | $DA$ | $A\sqrt D$ |

Two derivations worth doing once:

> **Pulse train.** High at $A$ for a fraction $D$ of the period: $V_{avg} = DA$, $V_{rms} = \sqrt{DA^2} = A\sqrt D$. At 25% duty and 5 V: average 1.25 V, RMS 2.5 V. The two differ a lot, which matters for sizing fuses and resistors in PWM circuits (Embedded Systems, Lesson 2.3).

> **DC plus AC.** $v = V_0 + V_p\sin\omega t$: the cross term averages to zero, so $V_{rms} = \sqrt{V_0^2 + V_{ac,rms}^2}$. RMS values of uncorrelated components add **in quadrature**: 3 V DC with 4 V RMS of ripple is 5 V RMS.

## Why RMS matters

- **Heating:** resistor, wire and transistor losses depend on $I_{rms}^2R$.
- **Ratings:** fuses, transformers and cables are rated in RMS current.
- **Power:** average power into a resistor is $V_{rms}^2/R$; in AC circuits, $P = V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\theta$.

A cheap "average-responding" meter assumes a sine wave; on distorted waveforms (PWM, rectifier currents) it reads wrong. **True-RMS** meters compute the integral.

## Key takeaways

- Average $= \frac1T\int v\,dt$; RMS $= \sqrt{\frac1T\int v^2\,dt}$, the power-equivalent DC value.
- Sine: RMS $= V_p/\sqrt2$; pulse: $A\sqrt D$; triangle: $A/\sqrt3$.
- Uncorrelated components add in quadrature: $V_{rms}^2 = V_{dc}^2 + V_{ac}^2$.
- Use RMS for heating and ratings; use true-RMS meters on non-sinusoidal waveforms.
