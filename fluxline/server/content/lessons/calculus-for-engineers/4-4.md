## Two operators and three theorems

Vector calculus culminates in three theorems that relate integrals over a region to integrals over its boundary. They're the bridge between the integral and differential forms of Maxwell's equations (Electromagnetics, Lesson 3.1), and the reason fields can be described locally, point by point.

## Divergence and curl

For $\mathbf F = (F_x, F_y, F_z)$:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F = \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}
$$

the **divergence**: net outflow per unit volume, positive at sources and negative at sinks.

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F = \left(\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial z},\ \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}\right)
$$

the **curl**: circulation per unit area, the field's local "swirl."

> $\mathbf F = (x^2, xy, 0)$: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F = 2x + x = 3x$, and $\nabla\times\mathbf F = (0, 0, y)$.

Two identities matter everywhere: the curl of a gradient is zero (gradient fields don't swirl, which is why $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$ in electrostatics), and the divergence of a curl is zero (curl fields have no sources, which is why $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$).

## The divergence theorem (Gauss)

The total outflow through a closed surface equals the total divergence inside:

$$
\oiint_S\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf S = \iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV
$$

> Check with $\mathbf F = \mathbf r$ ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf r = 3$) and a sphere of radius $R$: the volume integral is $3 \cdot \tfrac43\pi R^3 = 4\pi R^3$, matching the direct flux of Lesson 4.3.

Applied to $\mathbf D$, it turns Gauss's law in integral form, $\oint\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf S = Q_{enc}$, into the point form $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D = \rho_v$.

## Stokes' theorem

The circulation around a closed loop equals the flux of the curl through any surface bounded by it:

$$
\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot d\mathbf S
$$

Applied to $\mathbf H$, it turns Ampère's law, $\oint\mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = I_{enc}$, into $\nabla\times\mathbf H = \mathbf J$. It also shows that a field with zero curl everywhere has zero circulation around every loop, so it's conservative.

## Green's theorem

Stokes' theorem in the plane: for a region $R$ bounded by a counterclockwise curve $C$,

$$
\oint_C(P\,dx + Q\,dy) = \iint_R\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dA
$$

> With $P = -y$ and $Q = x$, the integrand is 2, so $\oint(-y\,dx + x\,dy) = 2 \times \text{area}$. For the unit circle that's $2\pi$, matching the direct circulation in Lesson 4.3. This is also how a **planimeter** measures area by tracing a boundary, and how CAD software computes the area of a polygon.

## Key takeaways

- Divergence = outflow per volume (sources); curl = circulation per area (swirl). curl grad = 0, div curl = 0.
- Divergence theorem: closed-surface flux = volume integral of divergence (Gauss's law, point form).
- Stokes' theorem: loop circulation = flux of the curl (Ampère's and Faraday's laws, point form); Green's theorem is its planar case.
