## The derivative is the language of change

Electrical engineering is built on rates. Current is the rate at which charge moves. The voltage across an inductor is proportional to the rate at which its current changes. A capacitor's current is proportional to the rate at which its voltage changes. If you are fluent in derivatives, these laws read like plain English.

## Definition

The derivative of $f(x)$ is the limit of the slope of secant lines:

$$
f'(x) = \frac{df}{dx} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

Geometrically, $f'(x)$ is the slope of the tangent line at $x$. Physically, it is the *instantaneous* rate of change of $f$ with respect to $x$.

> **From the definition.** For $f(x) = x^2$:
> $$\frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h \;\xrightarrow{h\to0}\; 2x$$

## Essential derivatives

| $f(t)$ | $f'(t)$ |
|---|---|
| $t^n$ | $n t^{n-1}$ |
| $e^{at}$ | $a e^{at}$ |
| $\sin(\omega t)$ | $\omega\cos(\omega t)$ |
| $\cos(\omega t)$ | $-\omega\sin(\omega t)$ |
| $\ln t$ | $1/t$ |

Note the factor of $\omega$ that appears when you differentiate a sinusoid. This one factor is why inductors and capacitors behave so differently at different frequencies — the foundation of every filter you will design.

## Derivatives in circuits

$$
i = \frac{dq}{dt}, \qquad v_L = L\frac{di_L}{dt}, \qquad i_C = C\frac{dv_C}{dt}
$$

> **Worked example.** The current in a 2 mH inductor is $i(t) = 5\sin(1000t)$ A. Find the inductor voltage.
>
> $v = L\frac{di}{dt} = (2\times10^{-3})(5)(1000)\cos(1000t) = 10\cos(1000t)$ V.
>
> Notice two things: the voltage peak (10 V) depends on frequency, and the voltage *leads* the current by 90° (cosine vs. sine). Both facts reappear when you study phasors.

> **Worked example.** Charge on a capacitor plate is $q(t) = 3t^2$ µC. The current is $i = dq/dt = 6t$ µA — growing linearly with time.

## Interpreting signs and zeros

- $f'(x) > 0$: $f$ is increasing. $f'(x) < 0$: decreasing.
- $f'(x) = 0$: a critical point — possibly a maximum or minimum. This is the basis of optimization, e.g., finding the load that extracts the most power from a source.
- Second derivative $f''(x)$ describes curvature (concavity) and acceleration.

## Practice

```quiz
? The charge through a wire is $q(t) = 4t^3 - t$ C. What is the current (A) at $t = 2$ s?
= 47 | 0.01 | A
> $i = 12t^2 - 1 = 48 - 1 = 47$ A.
```

```quiz
? The voltage across a 10 µF capacitor is $v(t) = 50\sin(200t)$ V. What is the peak capacitor current, in mA?
= 100 | 0.01 | mA
> $i = C\,dv/dt = 10^{-5}\cdot 50 \cdot 200\cos(200t) = 0.1\cos(200t)$ A → 100 mA peak.
```

```quiz
? What is $\dfrac{d}{dt}\, e^{-t/\tau}$?
- $e^{-t/\tau}$
- $\tau e^{-t/\tau}$
+ $-\tfrac{1}{\tau} e^{-t/\tau}$
- $-t e^{-t/\tau}$
> Chain rule: the derivative of the exponent $-t/\tau$ is $-1/\tau$.
```

## Key takeaways

- $f'(x)$ is the instantaneous rate of change.
- Differentiating a sinusoid multiplies by $\omega$ and shifts phase by 90°.
- $i = dq/dt$, $v_L = L\,di/dt$, $i_C = C\,dv/dt$ — calculus *is* circuit theory.
