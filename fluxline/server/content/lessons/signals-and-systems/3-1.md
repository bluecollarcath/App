## Every periodic signal is a sum of sinusoids

Joseph Fourier's claim (1807) still surprises people: any reasonable periodic signal, even a square wave with sharp corners, can be built by adding sinusoids at multiples of its fundamental frequency. This is why engineers talk about a signal's **harmonics** and **spectrum**, and why LTI systems are so easy to analyze in frequency: a sinusoid goes in, the same-frequency sinusoid comes out, only scaled and shifted.

## The exponential Fourier series

A signal with period $T$ has fundamental frequency $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$ and can be written

$$
x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} c_k\,e^{jk\omega_0 t},
\qquad
c_k = \frac{1}{T}\int_{T} x(t)\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt
$$

The coefficient $c_k$ is the complex amplitude of the $k$th harmonic. For a real signal $c_{-k} = c_k^*$, and the pair combines into a real sinusoid of amplitude $2|c_k|$. $c_0$ is the average (DC) value.

The equivalent **trigonometric form** is $x(t) = a_0 + \sum_{k\ge1}\big[a_k\cos(k\omega_0t) + b_k\sin(k\omega_0t)\big]$.

## The square wave

A square wave switching between $+1$ and $-1$ is odd, with zero average, and half-wave symmetric, so only **odd sine harmonics** survive:

$$
x(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left[\sin\omega_0t + \frac{1}{3}\sin3\omega_0t + \frac{1}{5}\sin5\omega_0t + \cdots\right]
$$

The harmonics fall off as $1/k$. Sharp corners need high harmonics, which is why fast digital edges radiate interference at many times the clock frequency.

### The Gibbs phenomenon

Near a jump, the partial sums overshoot by about **9% of the jump**, no matter how many terms you add. The overshoot gets narrower but never shorter. For the ±1 square wave (jump of 2), the peak approaches 1.179. This is a real effect in filters that cut off sharply, and it's why audio and image filters often use smoother cutoffs.

## Parseval's theorem: power by harmonic

The average power equals the sum of the harmonic powers:

$$
P = \frac1T\int_T |x(t)|^2\,dt = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} |c_k|^2
$$

> For the ±1 square wave, $P = 1$. The fundamental, amplitude $4/\pi$, carries $\frac12(4/\pi)^2 = 8/\pi^2 = 81\%$ of the power. That's why a square wave sounds like a sine wave with extra "buzz."

## Key takeaways

- Periodic signals are sums of harmonics at $k\omega_0$: $c_k = \frac1T\int_T x\,e^{-jk\omega_0t}\,dt$.
- Square wave: odd harmonics with amplitude $4/(\pi k)$; sharp edges need high harmonics.
- Gibbs: about 9% overshoot at jumps. Parseval: power is the sum of $|c_k|^2$.
