## Convolution with samples

In discrete time, signals are sequences of numbers and the integral becomes a sum. The **convolution sum** is

$$
y[n] = x[n] * h[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k]\,h[n - k]
$$

This is exactly what a DSP chip, a microcontroller, or a line of Python computes when it filters data. A filter whose impulse response has finitely many nonzero samples is called **FIR** (finite impulse response).

## Computing it

Same recipe as continuous time (flip, shift, multiply, add), but for short sequences there's a quicker way that works like long multiplication without carries.

> **Worked example.** $x = [1, 2, 3]$ and $h = [1, 1]$, both starting at $n = 0$.
>
> |  | $n=0$ | $n=1$ | $n=2$ | $n=3$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | $x \times h[0] = 1$ | 1 | 2 | 3 |  |
> | $x \times h[1] = 1$, shifted 1 |  | 1 | 2 | 3 |
> | **sum** $y[n]$ | **1** | **3** | **5** | **3** |
>
> $y = [1, 3, 5, 3]$. Each output adds the current and previous input samples.

**Length rule:** if $x$ has $N$ samples and $h$ has $M$, then $y$ has $N + M - 1$ samples.

## The moving average

The simplest useful FIR filter averages the last $M$ samples, $h = [\tfrac1M, \tfrac1M, \ldots, \tfrac1M]$. It smooths noise, at the cost of blurring fast changes.

> Three-point average of $x = [3, 6, 9, 12]$: $y = [1, 3, 6, 9, 7, 4]$. The middle outputs, 6 and 9, are true 3-sample averages; the ends are partial because the window hangs off the data.

```plot
x: -0.5, 9.5, n
y: -1.5, 2.6
samples: sin(0.6*x) + 0.6*sin(2.9*x + 1) | 1 | noisy input x[n]
f: 0.8836*sin(0.6*x - 0.6) | slow part after a 3-point average | dash
caption: A moving average passes the slow trend and suppresses the fast wiggles: it's a crude low-pass filter.
```

## Recursive (IIR) systems

Filters can also feed back past *outputs*:

$$
y[n] = 0.5\,y[n-1] + x[n]
$$

Its impulse response is $h[n] = 0.5^n u[n] = 1, 0.5, 0.25, \ldots$, which never ends: an **IIR** (infinite impulse response) filter. IIR filters reach sharp responses with far fewer operations than FIR filters, but they can become unstable; you'll analyze them with the z-transform in Unit 4.


## Key takeaways

- $y[n] = \sum_k x[k]\,h[n-k]$: multiply-and-add, like long multiplication without carries.
- Output length is $N + M - 1$.
- FIR filters (like the moving average) use only inputs; IIR filters also feed back outputs.
