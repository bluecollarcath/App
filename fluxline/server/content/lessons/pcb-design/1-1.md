## The language of electronics

A **schematic** is the circuit's blueprint: it shows every component and every electrical connection, but not where anything physically sits. Engineers read schematics to understand a product, debug it, review a design, or copy a reference circuit from a datasheet. Being able to read one fluently is a core skill, whether you design boards or just repair them.

A schematic shows **connectivity**, not geometry. Two parts drawn side by side might be on opposite corners of the board; a wire drawn as one line might be a long trace on another layer. The physical arrangement is the PCB **layout** (Unit 2).

Two drawing standards coexist. The American (ANSI/IEEE) resistor is the zigzag above; the European (IEC) resistor is a plain rectangle. You'll see both, often mixed, and the rectangle is also used for "generic impedance" in textbooks. Integrated circuits are usually drawn as **boxes** with numbered, named pins.

## Reference designators

Every component has a unique **reference designator**: a letter code plus a number. It ties the schematic, the board's silkscreen and the bill of materials together.

| Prefix | Component | Prefix | Component |
|---|---|---|---|
| R | resistor | U (or IC) | integrated circuit |
| C | capacitor | J, P | connector (jack, plug) |
| L | inductor | SW, S | switch |
| D | diode, LED | F | fuse |
| Q | transistor | Y, X | crystal, oscillator |
| FB | ferrite bead | TP | test point |
| T | transformer | K | relay |

"Replace C17" means find C17 on the schematic to understand it, then find the "C17" printed next to it on the board.

## Value notation

To avoid misreading a tiny or smudged decimal point, schematics often put the **unit prefix in place of the decimal point**:

| Written | Means |
|---|---|
| 4k7 | 4.7 kΩ |
| 2R2 | 2.2 Ω (R marks the decimal point for plain ohms) |
| 0R | 0 Ω (a jumper) |
| 1M | 1 MΩ |
| 100n | 100 nF = 0.1 µF |
| 4u7 | 4.7 µF |
| 10p | 10 pF |

Parts often carry more information than the value: a capacitor might read **10u 25V X7R 0805** (value, voltage rating, dielectric type, package size), and a resistor **1k 1%**. Notes like **DNP** (do not populate) or **NC** (no connect) matter too.

## Wires, junctions and crossings

- Lines are **wires** (nets): everything connected by a line is at the same node.
- A **junction dot** marks where wires connect. Wires that cross **without** a dot are **not** connected.
- Good practice avoids four-way junctions entirely (a dot can be lost in printing), using two offset T-junctions instead.
- **Power and ground symbols** connect by name: every symbol labeled "+3V3" is the same net, even if no wire is drawn between them. The ground symbol works the same way.

Common rail names: VCC and VDD (positive supply for bipolar and CMOS parts), VEE and VSS (negative supply or ground), and explicit voltages like +5V, +3V3, −12V. Some designs distinguish **AGND** (analog ground), **DGND** (digital ground) and chassis/earth ground, each with its own symbol.

## Key takeaways

- Schematics show connectivity, not physical placement.
- Learn the core symbols, and remember that the diode bar is the cathode and + marks polarized parts.
- Reference designators (R, C, L, D, Q, U, J...) link schematic, board and BOM.
- Prefix-as-decimal notation: 4k7, 2R2, 100n, 4u7; read ratings and notes like DNP.
- A dot means connected; a crossing without one doesn't; power symbols connect by name.
