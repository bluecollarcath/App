## Every current comes back

Current flows in loops. Each signal that leaves a driver returns to it through ground, and **where the return current flows** decides how much noise the board makes and picks up. "Ground" isn't a magic zero-volt sink; it's a conductor with resistance and inductance like any other.

- At **DC and low frequencies**, return current takes the path of least **resistance**, spreading out across the plane.
- Above roughly **100 kHz** in a typical board, it takes the path of least **inductance**: directly **underneath the signal trace**, on the nearest plane, because that minimizes the loop area.

```circuit
caption: At high frequency the return current hugs the signal trace on the adjacent plane, so the loop encloses only the thin gap between the layers. A solid plane under every fast trace is the single most effective layout rule for low noise and low EMI.
```

## Solid ground planes

The rule that follows: give every signal a **continuous ground plane** on the adjacent layer, and **never route a fast signal across a gap, slot or split** in that plane.

- A trace crossing a split forces its return current to detour around the gap, creating a big loop: a radiating antenna and a source of crosstalk.
- If a signal must change layers, put a **ground via (stitching via)** next to the signal via so the return current can change reference planes too.
- Avoid chains of closely spaced vias or clearances that "Swiss-cheese" the plane into an accidental slot.
- Fill unused areas of outer layers with ground pour **stitched** to the plane with vias every few millimeters (or just leave them empty: an unconnected, floating copper island is worse than none).

## Split grounds: usually don't

Older guidance said to split "analog ground" and "digital ground" and join them at one point. On mixed-signal boards with a single ADC, this usually makes things **worse**, since any trace that crosses the split creates a large loop. Modern practice (supported by ADC manufacturers):

- use **one solid ground plane**;
- **partition by placement**: analog parts over one area, digital over another, the ADC straddling the boundary (Lesson 2.3);
- route analog signals only within the analog area and digital signals only within the digital area, so their return currents don't mix.

Separate grounds still make sense for **isolation** (an isolated DC-DC converter and digital isolator, as in safety-isolated or medical equipment), or for keeping large, low-frequency motor or power currents out of the signal ground.

## Decoupling

When a digital IC switches, it draws current spikes of nanoseconds' duration. The power supply is centimeters away, through inductive traces, and can't respond that fast. **Decoupling capacitors** (bypass capacitors) supply the spike locally.

A real capacitor is a series **RLC**: capacitance $C$, equivalent series resistance (ESR), and equivalent series inductance (ESL, from the package, pads and vias). Its impedance falls with frequency until **self-resonance**,

$$
f_{SRF} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{ESL}\,C}}
$$

above which it behaves as an **inductor**.

```plot

y: 0.001, 100, impedance (Ω), log
f: sqrt(0.005^2 + (2*pi*x*2e-9 - 1/(2*pi*x*1e-5))^2) | 10 µF, 2 nH | dash
f: sqrt(0.01^2 + (2*pi*x*1e-9 - 1/(2*pi*x*1e-7))^2) | 100 nF, 1 nH
mark: 1.59e7, 0.01, 15.9 MHz
caption: Impedance of two ceramic capacitors including ESR and mounted ESL. Each looks capacitive below self-resonance and inductive above it. Above about 20 MHz the 100 nF part and the 10 µF part are equally good (or bad): only lower inductance helps there.
```

> **Example.** A 100 nF capacitor with 1 nH of mounted inductance self-resonates at
> $$
> f = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{10^{-9}\times10^{-7}}} = 15.9\ \text{MHz}
> $$
> At 100 MHz its impedance is essentially $2\pi f L = 0.63$ Ω, set by inductance alone.

## Decoupling in practice

- Put a **small capacitor (100 nF, 0402 or 0603) at every power pin**, as close as physically possible, and connect it with **short, wide** traces and **vias straight to the planes**. The mounted loop inductance (pad, trace, via) usually matters more than the capacitor itself.
- Add **bulk capacitance** (10–100 µF) near each regulator and where power enters a region of the board.
- Follow the IC datasheet: processors and FPGAs specify exact capacitor counts and values.
- On 4+ layer boards, closely spaced **power and ground planes** form a low-inductance plane capacitor that helps above ~100 MHz.
- Mixing values (100 nF + 1 µF + 10 µF) spreads low impedance over a wider band, but watch for **anti-resonance** peaks between them; simulation tools or manufacturer models (KEMET K-SIM, Murata SimSurfing) help.

**Target impedance** is a useful way to size a power network: the impedance the rail may present so that a current step $\Delta I$ doesn't move the voltage more than the allowed ripple.

$$
Z_{target} = \frac{V_{rail} \times \text{ripple fraction}}{\Delta I}
$$

> A 3.3 V rail allowed 3% ripple, with a 1 A transient: $Z_{target} = 3.3 \times 0.03 / 1 = 0.099$ Ω. The decoupling network must stay below about 0.1 Ω from DC up to the frequencies in the load current.

## Key takeaways

- High-frequency return current flows directly under the signal; give every fast trace an unbroken adjacent plane.
- Use one solid ground and partition by placement; split grounds only for isolation.
- Decoupling capacitors are RLC: above self-resonance inductance dominates, so place them at the pins with short, low-inductance connections.
- Size the power network with $Z_{target} = V \times \text{ripple} / \Delta I$.
