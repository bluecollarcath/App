## How boards are assembled

Surface-mount assembly is a short, highly automated sequence:

1. **Solder paste printing:** a laser-cut steel **stencil** (typically 0.1–0.15 mm thick) is aligned over the board and a squeegee pushes paste (tiny solder spheres in flux) through its apertures onto the pads.
2. **Pick-and-place:** machines place parts from tape reels, using fiducials to align, at tens of thousands of parts per hour.
3. **Reflow:** the board passes through an oven following a **reflow profile**: preheat, soak (activates flux), reflow peak (about 240–250 °C for lead-free SAC305 solder, melting at 217 °C) and controlled cooling.
4. **Inspection:** automated optical inspection (**AOI**) checks placement and joints; **X-ray** inspects BGAs and QFN thermal pads, whose joints are hidden.
5. **Through-hole parts** are wave-soldered, selectively soldered, or hand-soldered.

Common defects, and their layout causes:

| Defect | Cause |
|---|---|
| **Tombstoning** (a chip stands on one end) | unequal pad sizes or unequal copper (one pad on a plane without thermal relief) |
| **Bridging** | too much paste, too little solder mask between fine-pitch pads |
| **Voids** under thermal pads | one large paste aperture; use a window-pane pattern of smaller apertures |
| **Solder wicking** into vias | open vias in pads |

## Hand assembly and rework

Prototypes are often built by hand: a fine-tip soldering iron, flux, tweezers and magnification handle 0603 parts and SOIC packages easily; hot air or a hot plate handles QFNs. Choosing 0603 or larger passives and leaded ICs makes a prototype far easier to build and rework.

## Bring-up: power first

A new board should be brought up **one step at a time**, never by plugging everything in at once:

1. **Inspect** the board: shorts, solder bridges, part orientation, missing parts.
2. **Measure resistance** from each power rail to ground with the board unpowered. A few ohms or less on a rail usually means a short (or a large load such as a processor core: compare with a known-good board or expectation).
3. **Power up current-limited.** Use a bench supply with the current limit set just above the expected draw. If it hits the limit immediately, stop and find out why.
4. **Check every rail's voltage**, then its ripple with an oscilloscope (short ground spring, not the long ground lead).
5. **Check clocks and reset**: the crystal oscillates, reset releases.
6. **Program and test the firmware** block by block: a blinking LED first, then each interface.

> **A first check.** A board drawing 300 mA on its 3.3 V rail when you expected 30 mA dissipates $3.3 \times 0.3 \approx 1$ W somewhere: touch-test carefully, or use a thermal camera, to find the hot part. A reversed polarized part or a solder bridge is the usual culprit.

## Debugging techniques

- **Divide and conquer:** remove 0 Ω jumpers or lift a regulator's enable pin to isolate sections.
- **Find shorts** by injecting a small current (current-limited supply at a fraction of a volt) and finding the warm spot, or by measuring millivolt drops along the plane: the voltage decreases toward the short.
- **Bodge it:** cut traces with a knife and add **bodge wires** (thin enameled wire) to fix routing mistakes; document every change.
- **Use the tools:** multimeter, oscilloscope (with probes grounded close to the test point), logic analyzer for buses, thermal camera, and the MCU's debug interface (SWD/JTAG).
- **Compare with the datasheet:** an I²C bus that doesn't work often has missing pull-ups or swapped SDA/SCL; a regulator that oscillates often has the wrong output capacitor ESR.

## Revisions and errata

Record every problem and fix in an **errata** list tied to the board revision (Lesson 2.2). Rev A is rarely perfect; the goal is that **rev B** fixes everything you found, and that you can rebuild exactly what you shipped. Production adds **design for test**: bed-of-nails fixtures probe the test points you placed, and functional tests run on every board.

## Key takeaways

- Assembly: stencil paste print, pick-and-place, reflow profile, AOI/X-ray; many defects trace back to layout (pad balance, mask, vias in pads).
- Bring up step by step: inspect, measure rail resistance, current-limited power, check rails, clocks and reset, then firmware block by block.
- Debug by isolating sections, finding hot spots, and bodging carefully; record errata and fix them in the next revision.
