## Design rules before you route

Before drawing a single trace, load your fabricator's **design rules** into the EDA tool: minimum trace width and spacing, via drill and pad size, annular ring, copper-to-edge clearance. Then define **net classes**: groups of nets that share rules. A typical small board has:

| Net class | Width | Clearance | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Default (signals) | 0.2 mm | 0.2 mm | most logic signals |
| Power | 0.5–2 mm | 0.25 mm | sized for current (below) |
| High voltage | per current | 1–3 mm+ | creepage/clearance by safety standard |
| USB / differential | set by impedance | — | Lesson 3.3 |

With net classes in place, the router (and the DRC) enforce the rules automatically.

## Routing practice

- **Route critical nets first:** clocks, high-speed pairs, sensitive analog, then power, then everything else.
- **Short and direct.** Every millimeter is resistance, inductance and a potential antenna.
- Use **45° corners** (or arcs). Right angles aren't an electrical problem below many GHz, but 45° routing is shorter, looks cleaner and avoids acute angles that can trap etchant.
- **Never leave acute angles** or stubs (dead-end branches) on fast signals.
- On a two-layer board, a common convention is **mostly horizontal on one side, mostly vertical on the other**, switching layers with vias.
- **Don't route signals across a gap** in the plane below them (Lesson 3.2).
- Keep traces away from the board edge and from mounting holes.
- **Teardrops** (tapered pad-to-trace joints) add mechanical strength and drilling tolerance.

## Sizing traces for current

A trace carrying current heats up. The classic sizing chart, from **IPC-2221**, relates current $I$ (A), temperature rise $\Delta T$ (°C) and cross-sectional area $A$ (mil²):

$$
I = k\,\Delta T^{0.44}\,A^{0.725}
$$

with $k = 0.048$ for **external** layers and $k = 0.024$ for **internal** layers (which can't shed heat to air as easily). One-ounce copper is 1.378 mil thick, so a trace's area is its width in mils times 1.378.

```plot
x: 0, 3, trace width (mm)
y: 0, 8, current (A)
f: 0.048*20^0.44*(1.378*39.37*x)^0.725 | ΔT = 20 °C | dash
f: 0.048*10^0.44*(1.378*39.37*x)^0.725 | ΔT = 10 °C
mark: 0.78, 2, 2 A at 0.78 mm
caption: IPC-2221 current capacity of an outer-layer, 1 oz trace. A 10 °C rise is a conservative target; doubling the copper weight to 2 oz halves the width needed.
```

Useful results for 1 oz, external, 10 °C rise:

| Current | Width |
|---|---|
| 1 A | 0.30 mm (12 mil) |
| 2 A | 0.78 mm (31 mil) |
| 3 A | 1.37 mm (54 mil) |
| 5 A | 2.77 mm (109 mil) |

Internal traces need about $2^{1/0.725} \approx 2.6$ times the area for the same current. The newer standard **IPC-2152** gives more accurate (and usually less conservative) curves that account for board thickness and nearby planes; online calculators implement both. For high currents, use **copper pours** instead of traces, 2 oz copper, or parallel traces on several layers joined by many vias.

## Trace resistance

Copper's resistivity is $1.72 \times 10^{-8}$ Ω·m, so 1 oz (35 µm) copper has a **sheet resistance** of about

$$
R_\square = \frac{\rho}{t} = \frac{1.72\times10^{-8}}{35\times10^{-6}} \approx 0.5\ \text{m}\Omega/\square
$$

A trace's resistance is $R_\square$ times its number of **squares**, length ÷ width.

> **Example.** A 0.25 mm wide, 50 mm long trace is $50/0.25 = 200$ squares: $R = 200 \times 0.5\ \text{m}\Omega = 0.1$ Ω. At 1 A it drops 0.1 V, a full 3% of a 3.3 V rail. Wider traces, pours, or remote sensing at the load fix this.

## Clearance and high voltage

Spacing between conductors must prevent arcing and surface leakage:

- **Clearance** is the shortest distance through air; **creepage** is the shortest distance along the board surface.
- Mains-connected boards follow safety standards (IEC 62368-1, IEC 60335, UL): several millimeters between primary and secondary circuits, often with **slots** milled in the board to lengthen creepage.
- For low voltages (under ~50 V), the fabricator's minimum spacing is enough electrically.

## Vias in routing

- A typical via (0.3 mm drill) carries roughly 1 A comfortably and adds about 1 nH of inductance and ~0.5 pF of capacitance.
- Use **several vias** for power connections and plane connections of high-current parts.
- Keep vias out of SMD pads unless they are **filled and capped** (via-in-pad): an open via wicks solder away from the joint.

## Key takeaways

- Load fab rules and define net classes before routing; route critical nets first, short and direct.
- Size power traces for current with IPC-2221/2152: about 0.3 mm per amp at low currents for 1 oz external, growing faster than linearly.
- 1 oz copper ≈ 0.5 mΩ per square; count squares to estimate drops.
- High voltage needs clearance and creepage per the safety standard; use multiple vias for power.
