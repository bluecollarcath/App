## The datasheet is the specification

A schematic says **what** is connected; the component's **datasheet** says how it behaves, what it needs, and what will destroy it. Every part on a board should be chosen from its datasheet, not from a guess or a forum post. Datasheets follow a predictable structure, so you can find what you need quickly.

## The sections, and what to use them for

| Section | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Features / description** | what the part is for; a quick fit check |
| **Pin configuration** | pinout for each package, with pin 1 marked |
| **Absolute maximum ratings** | limits that must **never** be exceeded, even briefly |
| **Recommended operating conditions** | the range where the part is guaranteed to work |
| **Electrical characteristics** | guaranteed min, typical and max performance, with test conditions |
| **Typical performance curves** | how parameters vary with temperature, load, frequency |
| **Application information** | the reference circuit, component values, **layout guidelines** |
| **Package / mechanical** | dimensions and the recommended PCB land pattern |
| **Ordering information** | exact part numbers for each package, temperature grade, tape-and-reel |

## Read the right columns

- **Absolute maximum ratings are not operating conditions.** A regulator rated "absolute max 20 V input" is designed for perhaps 15 V. Operating near the absolute maximum shortens life or destroys the part.
- **Design with min and max, not typical.** "Typical" describes the average part at 25 °C. Your 10,000 production boards will include parts at both extremes. If a sensor's offset is 0.5 mV typical and 3 mV max, design for 3 mV.
- **Check the test conditions.** A spec measured at 25 °C, a 1 mA load and a 5 V supply may not apply at 85 °C, 500 mA and 3.3 V. Footnotes and the performance curves fill the gaps.
- **Follow the application circuit and layout guidance.** Required capacitor values, ESR ranges and placement rules (such as "place C_IN within 2 mm of pin 3") are part of the spec. Ignoring them is the most common reason reference circuits misbehave.

## Thermal limits

Power dissipated in a part raises its junction temperature:

$$
T_J = T_A + P\,\theta_{JA}
$$

with $\theta_{JA}$ (junction to ambient, °C/W) from the datasheet. It depends heavily on the board: the datasheet value assumes a specific test PCB with a certain copper area.

> **Example.** A linear regulator drops 5 V to 3.3 V at 200 mA: $P = 1.7 \times 0.2 = 0.34$ W. In a SOT-23 package with $\theta_{JA} = 200$ °C/W and 40 °C ambient, $T_J = 40 + 68 = 108$ °C: under a 125 °C limit, but with little margin. At 400 mA it would overheat; a larger package, more copper, or a switching regulator would be needed.

## Derating

Parts are used well below their ratings to improve reliability, especially in harsh environments:

- **Resistors:** run at ≤ 50% of rated power. A 0603 resistor rated 0.1 W should dissipate no more than 50 mW.
- **Ceramic capacitors:** class II dielectrics (X5R, X7R) **lose capacitance with DC bias**. A 10 µF, 6.3 V, 0603 X5R capacitor may provide only 3–5 µF at 5 V. Check the manufacturer's DC-bias curve, and choose a higher voltage rating or a larger package. C0G/NP0 capacitors don't have this problem, but only come in small values.
- **Electrolytic capacitors:** voltage rating about 1.5–2× the working voltage; lifetime halves for every 10 °C of temperature.
- **Semiconductors:** keep junction temperatures well below maximum and voltages below about 80% of their ratings.

## Packages

| Package | Notes |
|---|---|
| 0402, 0603, 0805, 1206 | chip resistors/capacitors; imperial code = size in hundredths of an inch (0603 = 0.06 × 0.03 in = 1.6 × 0.8 mm) |
| SOT-23, SOT-223 | small transistors, regulators |
| SOIC, TSSOP | leaded ICs, 1.27 / 0.65 mm pitch; easy to hand-solder and inspect |
| QFN, DFN | leadless with a thermal pad underneath; compact, good thermal performance, harder to inspect |
| BGA | balls underneath; highest density; needs X-ray inspection and fine PCB features |
| Through-hole (DIP, TO-220) | connectors, large parts, prototypes |

Watch the **imperial vs. metric** trap: an imperial "0603" is a metric "1608," and a metric "0603" is a much smaller imperial "0201." Always check the dimensions.

Before committing to a part, check its **availability and lifecycle**: stock at distributors, multiple sources, and that it isn't marked "not recommended for new designs" (NRND) or end-of-life.



```quiz

```

```quiz
- It will fail immediately

```

## Key takeaways

- Choose every part from its datasheet: pinout, ratings, characteristics, application circuit, land pattern.
- Absolute maximum ratings are limits, not operating points; design with min/max, not typical; check test conditions.
- $T_J = T_A + P\theta_{JA}$; derate resistors, capacitors (DC bias!) and semiconductors.
- Know package sizes and the imperial/metric code trap; check availability and lifecycle.
