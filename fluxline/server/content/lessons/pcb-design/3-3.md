## When a trace becomes a transmission line

A signal travels along a trace at a finite speed: in FR-4, roughly half the speed of light. For a **microstrip** (an outer trace over a plane), the effective permittivity is about 3.3, giving a propagation delay of

$$
t_{pd} = \frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}}{c} = \frac{\sqrt{3.3}}{3\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}} \approx 6.1\ \text{ps/mm}
$$

A **stripline** (an inner trace between two planes) is surrounded entirely by FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r \approx 4.3$), so it is slower, about 6.9 ps/mm.

What matters is not the clock frequency but the **rise time** $t_r$. When the trace delay is a significant fraction of the edge, reflections from the far end arrive while the edge is still rising, and the trace must be treated as a **transmission line** with a defined **characteristic impedance** $Z_0$ and proper termination. A common rule of thumb is that this happens when the round-trip delay exceeds the rise time:

$$
\ell_{crit} \approx \frac{t_r}{2\,t_{pd}}
$$

> **Example.** A microcontroller GPIO with a 1 ns edge on an outer layer: $\ell_{crit} = 1000\ \text{ps} / (2 \times 6.06\ \text{ps/mm}) \approx 83$ mm. Short traces are fine; a 20 cm ribbon to another board isn't. Note that a "slow" 1 MHz SPI clock from a modern chip still has nanosecond edges.

The rise time also sets the signal's **bandwidth**, roughly $0.35/t_r$: 350 MHz for a 1 ns edge. That's the frequency range your layout must handle well, regardless of the clock rate.

## Characteristic impedance

$Z_0$ depends on the trace geometry and the dielectric: trace width $w$, copper thickness $t$, height $h$ above the plane, and $\varepsilon_r$. For a microstrip, the classic IPC-2141 approximation is

$$
Z_0 \approx \frac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r + 1.41}}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{5.98\,h}{0.8\,w + t}\right)
$$

> **Example.** On a 4-layer board with 0.2 mm prepreg ($\varepsilon_r = 4.3$) between L1 and L2, a 1 oz (0.035 mm) trace 0.33 mm wide gives $Z_0 \approx 50$ Ω. Wider traces or thinner dielectric lower the impedance.

Approximations like this are good to about ±10%. For real designs, use a **field-solver** calculator (KiCad's built-in calculator, Saturn PCB Toolkit, or the fabricator's own stackup tool) and order **controlled impedance** from the fab: they adjust the trace width for their actual materials and test coupons to ±10%.

Common targets:

| Interface | Impedance |
|---|---|
| general single-ended, RF | 50 Ω |
| USB 2.0 / 3.x | 90 Ω differential |
| Ethernet, HDMI, LVDS, PCIe | 85–100 Ω differential |
| DDR memory | 40–60 Ω single-ended |

## Reflections and termination

When a wave meets an impedance change, part of it reflects, with **reflection coefficient**

$$
\Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}
$$

An open end ($Z_L \to \infty$) reflects everything ($\Gamma = +1$); a matched load ($Z_L = Z_0$) reflects nothing. Unterminated fast lines ring, overshoot and can clock twice on one edge.

- **Series (source) termination:** a resistor at the driver so that driver output resistance plus the resistor equals $Z_0$. A driver with about 15 Ω output on a 50 Ω line takes a 33 Ω (≈ 35 Ω) series resistor. Cheap and low-power; ideal for point-to-point clocks and signals.
- **Parallel termination:** a resistor of $Z_0$ to ground (or to a termination voltage) at the receiver. Fully absorbs the wave but draws DC current.
- **Differential termination:** a single resistor of the differential impedance (100 Ω) across the pair at the receiver; often built into LVDS and SerDes receivers.

## Differential pairs

High-speed serial links (USB, Ethernet, HDMI, PCIe) use **differential pairs**: two traces carrying opposite signals. The receiver looks at the difference, rejecting common-mode noise, and the opposite currents produce little radiation. Layout rules:

- Route the two traces **together**, at constant spacing, same layer, same length.
- **Length-match** within the pair: skew converts differential signal into common-mode noise. At 6.06 ps/mm, a 10 ps budget allows about 1.65 mm of mismatch; tools add small "serpentine" bumps to the shorter trace.
- Keep vias and layer changes to a minimum, and do them symmetrically, with ground vias nearby.
- Keep a solid reference plane under the pair, and keep other signals away.

## Crosstalk

Fields from one trace couple into its neighbors. Crosstalk falls rapidly with spacing and rises with parallel length.

- The **3W rule:** keep center-to-center spacing between fast traces at least three times the trace width.
- Keep traces close to their reference plane (thin dielectric reduces fringing).
- Don't run long parallel segments on adjacent signal layers; route them orthogonally, or put a plane between them.
- Guard sensitive analog traces with ground pour stitched by vias.

## Key takeaways

- Rise time, not clock rate, decides when a trace is a transmission line: $\ell_{crit} \approx t_r / 2t_{pd}$; FR-4 microstrip is about 6 ps/mm.
- $Z_0$ depends on width, dielectric height and $\varepsilon_r$; use a field solver and order controlled impedance.
- Terminate fast lines (series, parallel or differential) to stop reflections, $\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)$.
- Route differential pairs together, length-matched, over solid planes; space traces (3W) to limit crosstalk.
