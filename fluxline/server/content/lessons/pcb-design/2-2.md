## The EDA workflow

Boards are designed in **EDA** (electronic design automation) tools: **KiCad** (free, open source, fully professional), Altium Designer, Cadence OrCAD/Allegro, Autodesk Fusion Electronics (formerly EAGLE) and others. The workflow is the same everywhere:

1. **Schematic capture:** place symbols, wire them, name nets, annotate.
2. **Electrical rule check (ERC).**
3. **Assign footprints** to every symbol.
4. **Update the PCB** from the schematic (forward annotation): the parts appear with **ratsnest** lines showing unrouted connections.
5. **Place, route** and run the **design rule check** (DRC; Unit 3, Lesson 4.1).
6. **Generate outputs** for fabrication and assembly.

The schematic is the **source of truth**: change connectivity in the schematic and push it to the PCB, never the other way around.

## Symbols and footprints are separate

- A **symbol** is the schematic drawing: pins with names and numbers.
- A **footprint** (land pattern) is the copper pads, silkscreen outline, courtyard and assembly data for one physical package.

They're linked by **pin numbers**: symbol pin 3 connects to footprint pad 3. The same op-amp symbol might map to an SOIC-8, a TSSOP-8 or a DFN-8 footprint. Getting this mapping wrong (a SOT-23 transistor whose pinout differs between manufacturers, a connector numbered differently than its symbol) is one of the most common reasons a first board doesn't work.

## Libraries and creating parts

Use verified libraries where possible (KiCad's official libraries, manufacturer-provided models, services like Ultra Librarian or SnapEDA), but **check every footprint against the datasheet** before ordering boards:

- pad size, pitch and spacing match the **recommended land pattern** (which follows IPC-7351 sizing);
- **pin 1** is in the right place and marked on silkscreen;
- the **exposed thermal pad** of a QFN is present and connected as the datasheet requires;
- the component outline and courtyard are correct;
- for connectors, check the mating side and mounting holes.

> A 32-pin QFN with 0.5 mm pitch has 8 pins per side; the centers of the first and last pins on a side are $7 \times 0.5 = 3.5$ mm apart. Measuring that in the footprint editor is a quick sanity check.

A good habit: print the PCB layout at 1:1 scale on paper and lay the real parts on it.

## Annotation and fields

**Annotation** assigns reference designators (R1, R2...). Each symbol also carries fields that flow into the bill of materials: value, footprint, **manufacturer part number (MPN)**, supplier part number, voltage rating, tolerance, and "DNP" flags. A complete BOM, with an exact MPN for every line, is what an assembler needs to build your board.

## Electrical rule check

ERC scans the schematic for mistakes that a human misses on page 7 of 12:

- unconnected pins (mark intentional ones with a no-connect flag);
- outputs driving outputs;
- power input pins with no power source (EDA tools need a "power flag" to know a net is driven from a connector or regulator);
- nets with only one connection (often a misspelled net label: "SDA" vs. "SDA1");
- duplicate reference designators.

Treat every ERC warning as a bug until you've understood and resolved it.

## Version control

Keep design files in Git (Programming for Engineers, Lesson 3.2), tag each board revision ("rev A," "rev B") and record changes in an errata file. When a board comes back from the fab, you must be able to open exactly the design it was made from.

## Key takeaways

- Workflow: schematic → ERC → footprints → PCB update → place and route → DRC → outputs.
- Symbols and footprints are linked by pin number; verify every footprint against the datasheet land pattern and pin 1.
- Annotate, and give every part an exact MPN for the BOM.
- Resolve every ERC warning; keep designs in version control with tagged revisions.
