## Turning on a second-order circuit

Lesson 1.1 let a charged RLC circuit discharge on its own. Now apply a DC source at $t = 0$ and ask how the circuit settles to its new steady state. This is the **step response**, and it's how engineers characterize everything from power supplies to feedback controllers.

```circuit
V 0,0 0,2 V_s side=-1
SW 0,0 1.6,0 "t = 0"
R 1.6,0 3.2,0 R i=i
L 3.2,0 4.8,0 L
C 4.8,0 4.8,2 C v=v_C
W 4.8,2 0,2
caption: A series RLC circuit switched onto a DC source.
```

## Forced plus natural

KVL gives the same differential equation as before, now with a source term:

$$
\frac{d^2 v_C}{dt^2} + 2\alpha\frac{dv_C}{dt} + \omega_0^2\,v_C = \omega_0^2\,V_s,
\qquad \alpha = \frac{R}{2L},\ \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}
$$

The solution is a **forced response** (the final value) plus the familiar **natural response**:

$$
v_C(t) = \underbrace{V_s}_{v_C(\infty)} + \underbrace{(\text{natural response})}_{\text{decays}}
$$

The final value comes from DC steady state: the capacitor is open, so no current flows and $v_C(\infty) = V_s$. The natural response has the same three forms as Lesson 1.1. For the underdamped case:

$$
v_C(t) = V_s + e^{-\alpha t}\big(B_1\cos\omega_d t + B_2\sin\omega_d t\big)
$$

The constants come from **two** initial conditions. If the circuit starts at rest, $v_C(0^+) = 0$ and $i(0^+) = 0$; since $i = C\,dv_C/dt$, that means $dv_C/dt(0^+) = 0$. Then

$$
B_1 = -V_s, \qquad B_2 = -\frac{\alpha}{\omega_d}V_s
$$

## Worked example

> $V_s = 10$ V, $R = 40\ \Omega$, $L = 0.1$ H, $C = 10\ \mu$F, starting at rest.
>
> $\omega_0 = 1000$ rad/s, $\alpha = 200$ Np/s, so it's underdamped with $\omega_d = \sqrt{1000^2 - 200^2} = 979.8$ rad/s and $\zeta = \alpha/\omega_0 = 0.2$.
>
> $$v_C(t) = 10 - e^{-200t}\big(10\cos 979.8t + 2.04\sin 979.8t\big)\ \text{V}$$

## Reading a step response

Four numbers describe an underdamped step response, and they are the specifications you'll design to in Control Systems:

| Measure | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Peak time** $t_p$ | $\pi/\omega_d$ | 3.21 ms |
| **Overshoot** | $e^{-\alpha\pi/\omega_d} = e^{-\zeta\pi/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$ | 52.7% (peaks at 15.27 V) |
| **Settling time** (2%) | $\approx 4/\alpha$ | 20 ms |
| **Ringing frequency** | $\omega_d$ | 156 Hz |

```plot
x: 0, 30, t (ms)
y: 0, 16, v_C (V)
f: 10 - exp(-0.2*x)*(10*cos(0.9798*x) + 2.0412*sin(0.9798*x)) | R = 40 Ω (ζ = 0.2)
f: 10 - 10*(1 + x)*exp(-x) | R = 200 Ω (critically damped) | dash
hline: 10, final value
mark: 3.206, 15.27, peak 15.27 V at 3.21 ms
caption: The same circuit with two resistances. Less damping means a faster rise but large overshoot and ringing.
```

Overshoot can be destructive. A 10 V supply that briefly hits 15 V can exceed a chip's absolute-maximum rating. Designers add damping (more resistance, or a snubber) to trade speed for safety.




```quiz

+ Because the inductor current is continuous and starts at zero, and $i = C\,dv_C/dt$
- Because the source is DC
- Because the circuit is underdamped
> The series current equals the inductor current, which can't jump from its initial value of zero.
```

```quiz
- increase the source voltage

```

## Key takeaways

- Step response = final value + decaying natural response.
- Two initial conditions: $v_C(0^+)$ and $dv_C/dt(0^+) = i_L(0^+)/C$.
- Underdamped specs: $t_p = \pi/\omega_d$, overshoot $e^{-\alpha\pi/\omega_d}$, $t_s \approx 4/\alpha$.
