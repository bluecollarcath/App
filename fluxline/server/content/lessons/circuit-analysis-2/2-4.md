## The problem with a lagging power factor

Industrial loads are dominated by induction motors, which draw reactive power: their current lags the voltage. That reactive current does no useful work, but it still flows through every wire, transformer, and generator between the power plant and the load. For the same real power, a low power factor means:

- **more current**, so more $I^2R$ loss in the wiring and transformers,
- **less spare capacity**, since equipment is rated in VA (apparent power), not watts,
- **larger voltage drop** along feeders.

Utilities therefore bill industrial customers for reactive power or apply penalties below a power factor of about 0.9–0.95.

## The fix: supply VARs locally

A capacitor draws **negative** reactive power (it *supplies* VARs). Placing a capacitor in **parallel** with an inductive load cancels part of the load's reactive power, so less of it has to come through the supply. The real power, and the load itself, are unaffected.

## Sizing the capacitor

To raise the power factor from $\text{pf}_1 = \cos\theta_1$ to $\text{pf}_2 = \cos\theta_2$ at constant real power $P$:

$$
Q_1 = P\tan\theta_1, \qquad Q_2 = P\tan\theta_2, \qquad Q_C = Q_1 - Q_2 = P(\tan\theta_1 - \tan\theta_2)
$$

A capacitor across voltage $V_{rms}$ supplies $|Q_C| = \omega C V_{rms}^2$, so

$$
\boxed{C = \frac{P(\tan\theta_1 - \tan\theta_2)}{\omega V_{rms}^2}}
$$

## Worked example

> A 50 kW load runs at 0.8 lagging pf from a 480 V rms, 60 Hz single-phase supply. Correct it to 0.95 lagging.
>
> $\theta_1 = \cos^{-1}0.8 = 36.87^\circ$, $\tan\theta_1 = 0.75$. $\quad\theta_2 = \cos^{-1}0.95 = 18.19^\circ$, $\tan\theta_2 = 0.329$.
>
> $Q_C = 50\,000(0.75 - 0.329) = 21.07$ kVAR.
>
> $C = \dfrac{21\,066}{(2\pi\cdot60)(480^2)} = 243\ \mu$F.
>
> **The payoff:** supply current falls from $\frac{62.5\text{ kVA}}{480} = 130$ A to $\frac{52.6\text{ kVA}}{480} = 110$ A, a 16% reduction, which cuts wiring losses by about 29% (they scale with $I^2$).

## Practical notes

- **Don't overcorrect.** Correcting past unity makes the load capacitive (leading), which also raises current and can push voltage too high at light load. Most targets are 0.95–0.98 lagging.
- **Correct close to the load** so the reduced current benefits as much wiring as possible.
- **Watch for resonance.** Correction capacitors and the system's inductance form a resonant circuit; with nonlinear loads (variable-frequency drives, rectifiers) harmonics can excite it. Large installations use detuned (inductor-in-series) capacitor banks.

## Key takeaways

- Lagging loads draw reactive current that wastes capacity and causes losses.
- A parallel capacitor supplies VARs locally: $Q_C = P(\tan\theta_1 - \tan\theta_2)$.
- $C = Q_C/(\omega V_{rms}^2)$. Target about 0.95 lagging, not leading.
