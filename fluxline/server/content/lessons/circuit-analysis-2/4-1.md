## Why use the Laplace transform for circuits?

Phasors handle sinusoidal steady state. Time-domain methods handle first- and second-order transients, painfully. The **Laplace transform** handles **everything at once**: any input, any order, initial conditions included, using only algebra. It is the standard tool for analyzing circuits and control systems.

Recall $\mathcal L\{f(t)\} = F(s) = \int_{0^-}^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\,dt$, and the key property:

$$
\mathcal L\left\{\frac{df}{dt}\right\} = sF(s) - f(0^-)
$$

## Element models in the s-domain

Transform each element's $v$–$i$ law. Initial conditions turn into ordinary sources:

| Element | Time domain | s-domain (series form) |
|---|---|---|
| Resistor | $v = Ri$ | $V = RI$ |
| Inductor | $v = L\,di/dt$ | $V = sL\,I - L\,i(0^-)$: impedance $sL$ in series with a source $L\,i(0^-)$ |
| Capacitor | $i = C\,dv/dt$ | $V = \dfrac{I}{sC} + \dfrac{v(0^-)}{s}$: impedance $\dfrac{1}{sC}$ in series with a source $\dfrac{v(0^-)}{s}$ |

With zero initial conditions, impedances are simply $R$, $sL$, $1/(sC)$. Setting $s = j\omega$ recovers the phasor impedances, so the s-domain is a generalization of everything you've done in AC.

## The procedure

1. Find initial conditions $i_L(0^-)$, $v_C(0^-)$.
2. Draw the s-domain circuit, with initial-condition sources.
3. Solve for the desired $V(s)$ or $I(s)$ with any DC method (nodal, mesh, Thévenin, ...).
4. Invert with partial fractions.

## Example 1: RC with an initial charge

> A 0.5 F capacitor is charged to 4 V. At $t = 0$ it is connected in series with a 2 Ω resistor and a 10 V source. Find $i(t)$ and $v_C(t)$.
>
> ```circuit
> V 0,0 0,2 10/s side=-1
> R 0,0 2.4,0 "2" i=I(s)
> C 2.4,0 2.4,1 "1/(sC) = 2/s"
> V 2.4,1 2.4,2 4/s
> W 2.4,2 0,2
> caption: The s-domain circuit. The initial capacitor voltage becomes a source $4/s$ in series.
> ```
>
> KVL: $\dfrac{10}{s} = 2I + \dfrac{2}{s}I + \dfrac4s \;\Rightarrow\; I(s) = \dfrac{6/s}{2 + 2/s} = \dfrac{3}{s + 1}$
>
> So $i(t) = 3e^{-t}$ A, and $v_C(t) = 10 - 2i(t) = 10 - 6e^{-t}$ V. Check: $v_C(0) = 4$ V ✓, $v_C(\infty) = 10$ V ✓.

## Example 2: a second-order circuit with no guesswork

> A series RLC ($R = 5\ \Omega$, $L = 1$ H, $C = 0.25$ F), initially at rest, is driven by a 10 V step. Find $i(t)$.
>
> $$I(s) = \frac{10/s}{R + sL + \frac{1}{sC}} = \frac{10/s}{5 + s + 4/s} = \frac{10}{s^2 + 5s + 4} = \frac{10}{(s+1)(s+4)}$$
>
> Partial fractions: $\dfrac{10}{(s+1)(s+4)} = \dfrac{10/3}{s+1} - \dfrac{10/3}{s+4}$, so
>
> $$i(t) = \tfrac{10}{3}\left(e^{-t} - e^{-4t}\right)\ \text{A}$$
>
> No need to decide in advance whether the circuit is over- or underdamped: the roots of the denominator tell you.

## Initial and final value theorems

Check answers (or skip the inversion) with:

$$
f(0^+) = \lim_{s\to\infty} sF(s), \qquad f(\infty) = \lim_{s\to0} sF(s)
$$

The final value theorem is valid only if $f(t)$ actually settles, meaning all poles of $sF(s)$ are in the left half-plane.

> In Example 2: $f(0^+) = \lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{10s}{s^2+5s+4} = 0$ (the inductor current starts at zero ✓) and $f(\infty) = 0$ (the capacitor blocks DC ✓).

## Key takeaways

- s-domain impedances: $R$, $sL$, $1/(sC)$; initial conditions become sources.
- Solve algebraically with any DC method, then invert with partial fractions.
- Initial/final value theorems give quick checks.
