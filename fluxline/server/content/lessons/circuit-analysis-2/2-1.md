## The problem phasors solve

In the sinusoidal steady state, every voltage and current in a linear circuit is a sinusoid at the **same frequency** as the source. Only amplitude and phase differ. Solving differential equations to find those two numbers is wasteful — phasors turn calculus into complex-number algebra.

## Sinusoids

$$
v(t) = V_m\cos(\omega t + \phi)
$$

$V_m$ is the amplitude, $\omega = 2\pi f$ the angular frequency (rad/s), and $\phi$ the phase. Convention in this course: write everything as **cosines** (convert sines with $\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - 90^\circ)$).

## The phasor transform

Euler's formula gives $V_m\cos(\omega t + \phi) = \text{Re}\{V_m e^{j\phi} e^{j\omega t}\}$. Since every signal shares the $e^{j\omega t}$ factor, drop it and keep the complex amplitude:

$$
v(t) = V_m\cos(\omega t + \phi) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \mathbf{V} = V_m\angle\phi = V_m e^{j\phi}
$$

The key property: **differentiation becomes multiplication by $j\omega$.**

$$
\frac{dv}{dt} \longleftrightarrow j\omega\mathbf{V}
$$

## Impedance

Applying that to each element's $v$–$i$ law gives Ohm's law in phasor form, $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{Z}\mathbf{I}$:

| Element | Time domain | Impedance $\mathbf{Z}$ | Phase of $v$ vs $i$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resistor | $v = Ri$ | $R$ | in phase |
| Inductor | $v = L\,di/dt$ | $j\omega L$ | $v$ leads by 90° |
| Capacitor | $i = C\,dv/dt$ | $\dfrac{1}{j\omega C} = -\dfrac{j}{\omega C}$ | $v$ lags by 90° |

Memory aid: **ELI the ICE man** — in an inductor (L), E (voltage) leads I; in a capacitor (C), I leads E.

Impedance $\mathbf{Z} = R + jX$ has **resistance** $R$ and **reactance** $X$ ($X_L = \omega L > 0$, $X_C = -1/\omega C < 0$). Its reciprocal is **admittance** $\mathbf{Y} = 1/\mathbf{Z}$.

**Every DC technique now works for AC**: series/parallel impedances, dividers, nodal, mesh, Thévenin — just use complex arithmetic.

## Worked example

> A source $v_s = 100\cos(1000t)$ V drives $R = 30\ \Omega$ in series with $L = 40$ mH.
>
> $\mathbf{Z} = 30 + j(1000)(0.04) = 30 + j40 = 50\angle53.13^\circ\ \Omega$
>
> $\mathbf{I} = \dfrac{100\angle0^\circ}{50\angle53.13^\circ} = 2\angle{-53.13^\circ}$ A
>
> ```circuit
> VAC 0,0 0,2 "100 cos(1000t) V" side=-1
> R 0,0 2.4,0 "30 Ω" i=i
> L 2.4,0 2.4,2 "40 mH"
> W 2.4,2 0,2
> ```
>
> Back to the time domain: $i(t) = 2\cos(1000t - 53.13^\circ)$ A. The current lags the voltage — the circuit is inductive.
>
> ```plot
> x: 0, 12.57, t (ms)
> y: -1.15, 1.15, normalized
> f: cos(x) | v_s(t) / 100 V
> f: cos(x - 0.9273) | i(t) / 2 A | dash
> caption: The current peaks 53.13° (0.93 ms) after the voltage: the circuit is inductive.
> ```
>
> Voltages: $\mathbf{V}_R = 60\angle{-53.13^\circ}$ V, $\mathbf{V}_L = j40 \cdot \mathbf{I} = 80\angle36.87^\circ$ V. Note $60 + 80 \ne 100$ — magnitudes do not add; phasors do.

## Key takeaways

- $V_m\cos(\omega t + \phi) \leftrightarrow V_m\angle\phi$; $d/dt \leftrightarrow j\omega$.
- $Z_R = R$, $Z_L = j\omega L$, $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$.
- All DC analysis methods carry over with complex numbers.
