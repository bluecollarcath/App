## Every DC method, now with complex numbers

In sinusoidal steady state, phasors turn each element into an impedance and each sinusoid into a complex number. KCL and KVL still hold for phasors, so **nodal analysis, mesh analysis, superposition, source transformation, and Thévenin/Norton all work unchanged**. Only the arithmetic is complex.

### The workflow

1. Convert sources to phasors (same $\omega$ for all!) and elements to impedances: $R$, $j\omega L$, $1/(j\omega C)$.
2. Solve with any method, using complex arithmetic.
3. Convert the answer back to the time domain.

## Nodal analysis with phasors

> **Worked example.** $v_s = 10\cos(1000t)$ V. Find the voltage $v_A(t)$ and the current in the RL branch.
>
> ```circuit
> VAC 0,0 0,2 "10∠0° V" side=-1
> R 0,0 2.4,0 "10 Ω"
> C 2.4,0 2.4,2 "100 µF"
> W 2.4,0 4.2,0
> R 4.2,0 4.2,1 "10 Ω"
> L 4.2,1 4.2,2 "10 mH"
> W 0,2 2.4,2
> W 2.4,2 4.2,2
> TEXT 2.4,-0.4 A
> ```
>
> Impedances at $\omega = 1000$: $\mathbf Z_C = 1/(j\cdot1000\cdot10^{-4}) = -j10\ \Omega$, $\mathbf Z_{RL} = 10 + j10\ \Omega$.
>
> KCL at A:
> $$
> \frac{\mathbf V_A - 10}{10} + \frac{\mathbf V_A}{-j10} + \frac{\mathbf V_A}{10 + j10} = 0
> $$
>
> Multiply through and solve: $\mathbf V_A = 6 - j2 = 6.32\angle{-18.43^\circ}$ V.
>
> Branch current: $\mathbf I = \dfrac{\mathbf V_A}{10 + j10} = 0.447\angle{-63.43^\circ}$ A.
>
> Back to time: $v_A(t) = 6.32\cos(1000t - 18.43^\circ)$ V and $i(t) = 0.447\cos(1000t - 63.43^\circ)$ A.

## Thévenin equivalents in AC

Same definitions, complex values: $\mathbf V_{th}$ is the open-circuit phasor voltage and $\mathbf Z_{th}$ the impedance seen with sources deactivated.

> For the example above, looking into the terminals where the RL branch connects:
>
> $\mathbf V_{th} = 10\cdot\dfrac{-j10}{10 - j10} = 7.07\angle{-45^\circ}$ V, $\quad\mathbf Z_{th} = 10\parallel(-j10) = 5 - j5\ \Omega$
>
> Then $\mathbf I = \dfrac{\mathbf V_{th}}{\mathbf Z_{th} + 10 + j10} = \dfrac{7.07\angle{-45^\circ}}{15 + j5} = 0.447\angle{-63.43^\circ}$ A. ✓

An AC Thévenin equivalent is only valid **at the frequency it was computed for**, because the impedances change with $\omega$.

## Different frequencies: superposition is required

Phasors assume one frequency. If sources have different frequencies (including DC, which is $\omega = 0$), you **must** use superposition: solve once per frequency with its own impedances, convert each result to the time domain, and add the **time functions**. Never add phasors from different frequencies.

> **Example.** $v_s = 5 + 10\cos(1000t)$ V drives a 1 kΩ resistor in series with a 1 µF capacitor. Find the capacitor voltage.
>
> **DC part** ($\omega = 0$): the capacitor is open, so $v_{C,DC} = 5$ V.
>
> **AC part** ($\omega = 1000$): $\mathbf Z_C = -j1000\ \Omega$, so $\mathbf V_C = 10\cdot\dfrac{-j1000}{1000 - j1000} = 7.07\angle{-45^\circ}$ V.
>
> **Total:** $v_C(t) = 5 + 7.07\cos(1000t - 45^\circ)$ V.

## Key takeaways

- Every DC method works for phasors; just use complex impedances.
- AC Thévenin equivalents are valid only at one frequency.
- Multiple frequencies (including DC): superpose time-domain results, never phasors.
