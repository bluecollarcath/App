## The machine that generates the world's power

Nearly all grid electricity comes from **synchronous generators**: in coal, gas, nuclear and hydro plants. Their rotor carries a DC **field winding** (or permanent magnets) and turns at exactly synchronous speed, locked to the grid frequency:

$$
n_s = \frac{120f}{p}
$$

Steam turbines spin 2-pole rotors at 3600 rpm (3000 at 50 Hz); slow hydro turbines use many poles: a 72-pole generator turns at 100 rpm on 60 Hz.

## The per-phase model

The spinning field induces an internal EMF $E_f$ in the stator, proportional to the field current (and speed). The stator winding adds its **synchronous reactance** $X_s$ (armature reaction plus leakage; resistance is usually negligible):

$$
\mathbf E_f = \mathbf V + jX_s\mathbf I \quad\text{(generator)}
$$

The angle between $\mathbf E_f$ and $\mathbf V$ is the **power angle** $\delta$. Working out $S = VI^*$ from the circuit gives the two key equations (per phase, or in per-unit):

$$
P = \frac{E_fV}{X_s}\sin\delta, \qquad Q = \frac{E_fV\cos\delta - V^2}{X_s}
$$

- **Real power** is set by the mechanical input: open the turbine valve, the rotor advances, $\delta$ grows, more power flows. The maximum, $E_fV/X_s$ at 90°, is the steady-state stability limit (Power Systems, Lesson 4.1).
- **Reactive power** is set mainly by the **field current** (through $E_f$).

> **Example (per-unit).** $E_f = 1.5$, $V = 1.0$, $X_s = 1.2$, $\delta = 30°$:
> $P = 1.5(0.5)/1.2 = 0.625$ pu and $Q = (1.5 \times 0.866 - 1)/1.2 = 0.249$ pu. The machine delivers both real and reactive power. Its maximum power at this excitation is $1.5/1.2 = 1.25$ pu.

## Excitation and reactive power

At a given real power output, adjusting the field current changes $E_f$:

- **Over-excited** ($E_f\cos\delta > V$): the machine **supplies** reactive power, like a capacitor. Generators normally run this way, supporting grid voltage.
- **Under-excited**: it **absorbs** reactive power, like an inductor.

Plotting armature current against field current at constant power gives the characteristic **V-curves**, with the minimum current at unity power factor. A synchronous machine running with no mechanical load, used only for its reactive power, is a **synchronous condenser**; old generators are often converted into them as grids add inverter-based plants (they also add inertia and fault current).

## As a motor

Run as a motor, the same machine turns at exactly synchronous speed regardless of load (until it pulls out of step), with $\delta$ now negative. Large industrial synchronous motors (compressors, mills) are run over-excited to correct the plant's power factor. They can't start on their own (the rotor can't jump to synchronous speed), so they use damper bars to start as induction motors, or a variable-frequency drive.

## Key takeaways

- Synchronous machines turn at exactly $120f/p$; the field sets $E_f$.
- $P = (E_fV/X_s)\sin\delta$ (set by mechanical input), $Q = (E_fV\cos\delta - V^2)/X_s$ (set by excitation).
- Over-excited: supplies vars (capacitive); under-excited: absorbs vars. V-curves show this.
- As motors they run at constant speed and can correct power factor; synchronous condensers support grids.
