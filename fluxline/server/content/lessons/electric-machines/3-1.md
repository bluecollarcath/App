## Why vary the speed?

A motor connected straight to the grid runs at essentially one speed. Most loads don't need that: a fan or pump at 80% flow could run slower, yet traditionally it ran flat out while a valve or damper threw away the excess. A **variable-frequency drive** (VFD) rectifies the grid to a DC bus and uses an inverter (Power Electronics, Lesson 2.2) to feed the motor with whatever frequency and voltage it needs.

**The savings can be huge.** For centrifugal fans and pumps, the **affinity laws** say flow $\propto n$, pressure $\propto n^2$ and power $\propto n^3$. Running at 80% speed needs only $0.8^3 = 51\%$ of full power. Since fans and pumps are a large share of industrial electricity, VFDs are one of the most cost-effective efficiency measures there is.

## Scalar control: constant V/f

An induction motor's air-gap flux is proportional to $V/f$ (Faraday's law: $V \approx 4.44fN\phi$). To change speed while keeping the flux, and therefore the torque capability, at its rated value, vary the voltage **in proportion to frequency**:

$$
\frac{V}{f} = \text{constant} = \frac{V_{rated}}{f_{rated}}
$$

> A 460 V, 60 Hz motor at 30 Hz gets 230 V.

The whole torque–speed curve then slides left along the speed axis: the motor runs at the new synchronous speed minus roughly the same slip (in rpm) as before. Two refinements:

- **Low-speed boost:** at low frequency the stator resistance drop becomes significant, so drives add extra voltage to keep the flux up.
- **Above base speed** (above 60 Hz), the voltage can't rise further (the inverter's DC bus limits it), so the flux falls as $1/f$: **field weakening**. Torque capability falls, giving a constant-power region, just like a DC motor's field weakening.

V/f control is simple and robust, and it's ideal for fans, pumps and conveyors, but it doesn't control torque directly and responds sluggishly.

## Field-oriented (vector) control

For high-performance applications (servos, cranes, elevators, electric vehicles), **field-oriented control** makes an AC motor behave like a separately excited DC motor (Lesson 2.1), with independent control of flux and torque.

1. Measure the three phase currents and convert them to a two-axis stationary frame (**Clarke transform**).
2. Rotate that frame to align with the rotor flux (**Park transform**), using the rotor position (from an encoder or estimated sensorlessly). In this rotating frame the currents are DC quantities:
   - $i_d$, aligned with the flux, controls the **flux** (like a DC motor's field current);
   - $i_q$, at 90°, controls the **torque** (like the armature current).
3. Regulate $i_d$ and $i_q$ with two PI controllers (Control Systems, Lesson 3.2), then transform the controller outputs back to three-phase voltage references for the PWM inverter.

$$
T \propto \lambda_d\,i_q
$$

The result is full torque from standstill, torque response in a millisecond, and precise speed and position control. It runs on a microcontroller doing a few dozen multiplications every PWM period.

## Regeneration and braking

When a drive slows a high-inertia load or lowers a crane's load, the motor acts as a **generator** and pushes energy back into the DC bus, raising its voltage. The drive must dump it in a **braking resistor**, or, with an active front end, return it to the grid. Electric vehicles do the latter with their battery: regenerative braking.

## Key takeaways

- VFDs rectify, then invert to variable frequency and voltage; fans and pumps save energy as $n^3$.
- V/f control keeps flux constant below base speed; field weakening above it gives constant power.
- Field-oriented control transforms currents to a rotating frame: $i_d$ for flux, $i_q$ for torque, like a DC motor.
- Regenerated energy goes to a braking resistor or back to the grid or battery.
