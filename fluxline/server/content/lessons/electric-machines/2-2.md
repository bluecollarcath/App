## The motor that runs the world

Induction motors drive most of the world's pumps, fans, compressors and conveyors, and use roughly a third of all electricity generated. They're rugged, cheap and need no brushes or magnets: the rotor is just a cage of aluminum or copper bars, shorted at the ends (the "squirrel cage"), and its currents are **induced** by the stator.

## The rotating field

Three stator windings, 120° apart in space and fed with three-phase currents 120° apart in time, produce a magnetic field of constant strength that **rotates** around the air gap at the **synchronous speed**:

$$
n_s = \frac{120f}{p} \ \text{rpm}
$$

where $p$ is the number of poles. At 60 Hz, a 2-pole machine's field turns at 3600 rpm, a 4-pole at 1800, a 6-pole at 1200.

## Slip

If the rotor turned at synchronous speed, its bars would see no changing flux, induce no current, and produce no torque. So an induction motor always runs slightly **slower** than the field. The **slip** is

$$
s = \frac{n_s - n}{n_s}
$$

typically 1–5% at full load. The rotor bars see the field sweep past at the slip speed, so the rotor currents have frequency $sf$, a few hertz.

> A 4-pole, 60 Hz motor running at 1746 rpm: $s = 54/1800 = 0.03$, and the rotor current frequency is $0.03 \times 60 = 1.8$ Hz.

## Power flow

The per-phase equivalent circuit looks like a transformer whose secondary (rotor) resistance appears as $R_2/s$. The power crossing the air gap splits in a fixed ratio:

$$
P_{ag} = 3I_2^2\frac{R_2}{s}, \qquad P_{rotor\ Cu} = sP_{ag}, \qquad P_{mech} = (1 - s)P_{ag}
$$

and the torque is

$$
T = \frac{P_{ag}}{\omega_s}
$$

> With $P_{ag} = 10$ kW at $s = 0.03$: rotor copper loss is 300 W, mechanical power 9.7 kW, and torque $= 10\,000/188.5 = 53$ N·m ($\omega_s = 1800$ rpm $= 188.5$ rad/s).

Running at high slip wastes energy: at $s = 0.5$, half the air-gap power becomes rotor heat. That's why efficient operation means low slip, and why controlling speed by increasing slip is wasteful.

## The torque–speed curve

From standstill, torque rises to a maximum (the **breakdown** or pull-out torque, typically 2–3 times rated) at a slip $s_{max} \approx R_2/X$, then falls steeply to zero at synchronous speed. A handy approximation (Kloss's formula):

$$
\frac{T}{T_{max}} = \frac{2}{s/s_{max} + s_{max}/s}
$$

In the steep region near synchronous speed, the motor behaves like a stiff speed source: more load, slightly more slip, much more torque. Overload past the breakdown torque and it **stalls**. Design classes (NEMA A–D) trade starting torque against running efficiency by shaping the rotor bars.

## Starting

Starting at full voltage draws 5–8 times rated current (the rotor at $s = 1$ looks almost like a short). Large motors use soft starters (thyristor voltage control), wye–delta starters, or variable-frequency drives (Lesson 3.1).

## Key takeaways

- Three-phase windings make a field rotating at $n_s = 120f/p$; the rotor lags by slip $s = (n_s - n)/n_s$.
- $P_{ag}$ splits into rotor loss $sP_{ag}$ and mechanical power $(1 - s)P_{ag}$; $T = P_{ag}/\omega_s$.
- Torque peaks at breakdown (2–3 pu); normal operation is at a few percent slip.
- Direct starting draws 5–8× rated current.
