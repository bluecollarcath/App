## Magnets instead of brushes

Replace a DC motor's wound field with **permanent magnets on the rotor**, put the windings on the stator, and replace the mechanical commutator with transistors switched according to the rotor's position. The result is a **brushless permanent-magnet machine**: no brushes to wear or spark, no rotor copper loss, and the highest efficiency and power density of any motor type. They run drones, hard drives, fans, power tools, robots, and the traction motors of most electric vehicles.

Rare-earth (neodymium-iron-boron) magnets made this possible: they produce about 1.2 T and resist demagnetization, though they lose strength when hot, so motor cooling and magnet grade matter.

## Two flavors

| | BLDC (brushless DC) | PMSM (permanent-magnet synchronous) |
|---|---|---|
| Back-EMF shape | trapezoidal | sinusoidal |
| Drive | six-step: two phases on at a time | sinusoidal currents, field-oriented control |
| Position sensing | 3 Hall sensors (60° resolution), or sensorless | encoder/resolver, or sensorless observer |
| Torque ripple | noticeable at each commutation | very smooth |
| Typical use | fans, pumps, drones, appliances | servos, EVs, precision drives |

Both are synchronous machines (Lesson 2.3) driven by an inverter that always keeps the stator field ahead of the rotor magnets.

## The motor constants

A permanent-magnet machine behaves like a DC motor with a fixed field:

$$
e = K_e\,\omega, \qquad T = K_t\,I
$$

In SI units, $K_e$ (V·s/rad) and $K_t$ (N·m/A) are the **same number**, again by energy conservation.

> **Example.** A drone motor with $K_t = K_e = 0.05$: at 20 A it makes 1 N·m. At 3000 rpm (314 rad/s), its back-EMF is 15.7 V. On a 24 V supply, its no-load speed is $24/0.05 = 480$ rad/s, or 4580 rpm.

Hobby motors are often rated by $K_v$, the no-load rpm per volt: $K_v = 60/(2\pi K_e)$, about 191 rpm/V here. A high-$K_v$ motor spins fast with little torque per amp; a low-$K_v$ motor the reverse.

## Six-step commutation

A BLDC controller reads the three Hall sensors, which give one of six states per electrical revolution, and switches the inverter so that current flows into one phase and out of another, choosing the pair whose back-EMF is flat at its peak. Every 60 electrical degrees, it advances to the next pair. Speed is controlled by PWM on the active switches. **Sensorless** controllers instead watch the back-EMF zero crossing on the floating third phase, which works once the motor is turning.

## PMSM with field-oriented control

For a sinusoidal machine with $p$ pole pairs and magnet flux linkage $\lambda_m$, field-oriented control (Lesson 3.1) aligns the $d$ axis with the magnets. With $i_d = 0$, all the current produces torque:

$$
T = \frac32\,p\,\lambda_m\,i_q
$$

> With $p = 4$, $\lambda_m = 0.1$ Wb and $i_q = 100$ A: $T = 1.5 \times 4 \times 0.1 \times 100 = 60$ N·m. At 6000 rpm (100 rev/s), the electrical frequency is $4 \times 100 = 400$ Hz.

**Interior permanent-magnet (IPM)** machines bury the magnets inside the rotor iron, so the inductance differs along the $d$ and $q$ axes. A negative $i_d$ then adds **reluctance torque** and also weakens the field, extending the speed range at constant power. That's why IPM machines dominate EV traction: high torque for acceleration, and a wide constant-power range for highway speed, at efficiencies above 95%.

## Key takeaways

- Brushless PM machines put magnets on the rotor and commutate electronically: efficient, compact, durable.
- $e = K_e\omega$, $T = K_tI$, with $K_e = K_t$ in SI; $K_v = 60/(2\pi K_e)$ rpm/V.
- BLDC: trapezoidal EMF, six-step Hall commutation. PMSM: sinusoidal, FOC, $T = \tfrac32p\lambda_mi_q$.
- IPM machines add reluctance torque and field weakening, ideal for EVs.
