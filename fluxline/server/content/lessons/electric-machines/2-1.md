## The first practical motor

The DC machine is the easiest rotating machine to understand and control: its torque is proportional to its current, and its speed to its voltage. Though brushless motors have replaced it in most new designs, its equations are the model every motor drive tries to imitate.

## Construction and commutation

The **field** (stator magnets or field windings) produces a fixed flux $\phi$. The **armature** (rotor) carries conductors connected through a **commutator**, a segmented copper ring with brushes, which reverses each conductor's current as it passes from one pole to the next. The armature's field therefore stays fixed at 90° to the field flux, the angle of maximum torque, whatever the rotor position.

## The two equations

With a machine constant $K$ (set by the number of poles and conductors) and speed $\omega$ (rad/s):

$$
E_a = K\phi\,\omega \quad\text{(back-EMF)}, \qquad T = K\phi\,I_a \quad\text{(torque)}
$$

The **same** constant appears in both (in SI units), which is energy conservation: $E_aI_a = T\omega$. The armature circuit adds its resistance:

$$
V_t = E_a + I_aR_a \quad\text{(motor)}
$$

Combining them gives the speed:

$$
\omega = \frac{V_t - I_aR_a}{K\phi} = \frac{V_t}{K\phi} - \frac{R_a}{(K\phi)^2}T
$$

a straight line drooping slightly with load.

> **Example.** A separately excited motor: $V_t = 240$ V, $R_a = 0.5\ \Omega$, $K\phi = 2$ V·s/rad.
>
> No load: $\omega = 120$ rad/s (1146 rpm).
>
> At $I_a = 20$ A: $E_a = 240 - 10 = 230$ V, $\omega = 115$ rad/s, $T = 40$ N·m, and the converted power is $230 \times 20 = 4.6$ kW.
>
> **Starting:** at standstill $E_a = 0$, so $I_a = 240/0.5 = 480$ A, 24 times the running current. DC motors need a starting resistor or a current-limited drive.

## Speed control

- **Armature voltage control** (below base speed): vary $V_t$ with a chopper or controlled rectifier. Speed is proportional to voltage at constant torque capability.
- **Field weakening** (above base speed): reduce $\phi$; the motor speeds up ($\omega \propto 1/\phi$) but its torque per amp falls, giving a constant-power region.

## Field connections

- **Separately excited / permanent magnet:** constant $\phi$; nearly constant speed.
- **Shunt:** field across the supply; similar to separately excited.
- **Series:** field in series with the armature, so $\phi \propto I_a$ and $T \propto I_a^2$: huge starting torque, but the speed runs away at no load. Used in old traction motors and still in universal motors (power tools, vacuum cleaners), which run on AC because both fields reverse together.

## Key takeaways

- $E_a = K\phi\omega$, $T = K\phi I_a$, $V_t = E_a + I_aR_a$ (the same $K\phi$ in both).
- Speed falls slightly with load: $\omega = V_t/K\phi - R_aT/(K\phi)^2$; starting current $V_t/R_a$ is huge.
- Armature voltage controls speed below base; field weakening extends it above.
- Series machines give high starting torque; universal motors run on AC.
