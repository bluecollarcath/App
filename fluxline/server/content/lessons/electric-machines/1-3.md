## Where does the force come from?

Motors, generators, relays, solenoids, loudspeakers and MEMS actuators all turn electrical energy into motion (or the reverse) through a magnetic field. Two complementary views explain the force.

## The Lorentz force view

A conductor of length $\ell$ carrying current $I$ across a field $B$ feels a force, and a conductor moving at speed $v$ through a field has an EMF induced along it:

$$
F = BI\ell, \qquad e = B\ell v
$$

> A 0.2 m conductor carrying 10 A in a 1 T field feels 2 N.

These two equations are the whole story of a DC motor or a loudspeaker voice coil: current produces force, and motion produces a back-EMF that opposes the current. Multiplying them shows that electrical power in equals mechanical power out: $eI = (B\ell v)I = Fv$.

## The energy view

In most machines the current-carrying conductors sit in slots in iron, where the field is weak; the force actually acts on the iron. A more general method uses energy.

A magnetic system with flux linkage $\lambda$ and current $i$ stores **field energy** and has a complementary **co-energy**:

$$
W_f = \int_0^\lambda i\,d\lambda', \qquad W_f' = \int_0^i \lambda\,di'
$$

For a linear system ($\lambda = Li$), both equal $\tfrac12Li^2$. If a part moves by $dx$ while the current is held constant, energy balance gives the force as the derivative of co-energy:

$$
F = \frac{\partial W_f'(i, x)}{\partial x}\bigg|_{i} = \frac12i^2\frac{dL}{dx}, \qquad T = \frac12i^2\frac{dL}{d\theta}
$$

**Forces act to increase inductance**: to shorten air gaps and align iron with the field. That's the **reluctance force** behind solenoids, relays, stepper motors and switched-reluctance motors, and it's why a piece of iron is pulled into a coil.

## Magnetic pressure

The force on an iron surface across an air gap with flux density $B$ acts like a pressure:

$$
\frac{F}{A} = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}
$$

At 1 T, that's 398 kPa, about 4 atmospheres.

> **Electromagnet.** A U-shaped lifting magnet with two pole faces of 10 cm² each, at 1 T in the gaps: $F = 2 \times (1^2/2\mu_0)(10^{-3}) = 796$ N, enough to hold about 80 kg. Doubling $B$ quadruples the force, until the iron saturates near 1.5–2 T.

## Torque in rotating machines

Every rotating machine produces torque from the interaction of two magnetic fields, one on the stator and one on the rotor, which try to align, like two magnets. In terms of the stator and rotor MMFs and the angle $\delta$ between them,

$$
T \propto F_sF_r\sin\delta
$$

Maximum torque occurs at 90°. Each machine type maintains that angle differently: the DC machine's commutator keeps the rotor field fixed at 90° to the stator field (Lesson 2.1); the induction and synchronous machines use a **rotating** stator field that the rotor field follows (Lessons 2.2 and 2.3). Machine design is largely about getting the most $B$ in the gap and the most current in the conductors, limited by saturation and heating; that's why torque scales with **rotor volume**.

## Key takeaways

- $F = BI\ell$ and $e = B\ell v$; together they conserve power.
- Force from co-energy: $F = \tfrac12i^2\,dL/dx$, $T = \tfrac12i^2\,dL/d\theta$; reluctance forces increase inductance.
- Magnetic pressure $B^2/2\mu_0$ (398 kPa at 1 T); force grows with $B^2$ until saturation.
- Machines make torque from two fields, maximum at 90°.
