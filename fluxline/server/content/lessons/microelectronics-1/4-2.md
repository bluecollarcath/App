## Why gain falls at low frequencies

Coupling capacitors (between stages) and bypass capacitors (across emitter or source resistors) are open circuits at DC, which is exactly why they're used: they keep stages from disturbing each other's bias. But that also means they're **not** perfect shorts at low signal frequencies. As frequency falls, their reactance $1/(2\pi fC)$ grows, and the gain rolls off. Every capacitor-coupled amplifier is a **high-pass** filter.

## Each capacitor sees a resistance

Each capacitor forms a high-pass corner at

$$
f = \frac{1}{2\pi R_{eq}C}
$$

where $R_{eq}$ is the resistance seen by that capacitor with the signal source shorted (the other capacitors treated as shorts).

**Coupling capacitor** between a source $R_s$ and an amplifier input $R_{in}$: $R_{eq} = R_s + R_{in}$.

> For $f = 20$ Hz with $R_s = 1$ kΩ and $R_{in} = 4$ kΩ: $C = \dfrac{1}{2\pi(5\ \text{k}\Omega)(20)} = 1.6\ \mu$F.

**Emitter bypass capacitor** (CE stage): it sees $R_E$ in parallel with the resistance looking back into the emitter, which is tiny because of the $(\beta + 1)$ division:

$$
R_{eq} = R_E \parallel \left(r_e + \frac{R_s \parallel R_{Th}}{\beta + 1}\right)
$$

> Using the Lesson 3.2 amplifier ($R_E = 1$ kΩ, $r_e = 12\ \Omega$, $R_{Th} = 7.5$ kΩ, $\beta = 100$) with a 1 kΩ source:
> $R_{eq} = 1\ \text{k} \parallel (12 + 882/101) = 1\ \text{k} \parallel 20.8 = 20.4\ \Omega$.
> For 20 Hz: $C_E = \dfrac{1}{2\pi(20.4)(20)} = 390\ \mu$F.

That's why bypass capacitors are the big electrolytic parts on amplifier boards: they see a very small resistance.

## Designing for a target $f_L$

With several capacitors, the corners combine. The practical approach is to make **one capacitor dominant**: put the bypass capacitor's corner at the target $f_L$, and the coupling capacitors' corners about a decade lower, where they cost little because they see larger resistances. The overall cutoff then lands close to the dominant corner.

High-frequency rolloff, caused by the transistor's internal capacitances and the Miller effect, is the subject of Microelectronics II. Between $f_L$ and $f_H$ lies the **midband**, where the gain is flat and equal to the value from the small-signal analysis.

```quiz
? Why is the emitter bypass capacitor usually much larger than the coupling capacitors?
+ It sees a very small resistance, because the resistance looking into the emitter is divided by $\beta + 1$
- It carries the DC bias current
- It must block a larger DC voltage
- It sets the high-frequency cutoff
> For the same corner frequency, a smaller resistance needs a proportionally larger capacitor.
```

## Key takeaways

- Coupling and bypass capacitors make amplifiers high-pass; each gives a corner at $1/(2\pi R_{eq}C)$.
- Coupling: $R_{eq} = R_s + R_{in}$. Bypass: $R_E \parallel (r_e + R_s'/(\beta + 1))$, which is tiny.
- Make one corner dominant at $f_L$ and place the others about a decade lower.
