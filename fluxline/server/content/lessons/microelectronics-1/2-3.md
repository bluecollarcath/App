## Small signals on a DC bias

An amplifier's transistor sits at a DC operating point, and the signal is a small wiggle around it: $v_{GS} = V_{GS} + v_{gs}$. For small enough wiggles, the curved square law looks like a straight line (a first-order Taylor expansion), and the transistor can be replaced by a simple **linear** circuit. That's the **small-signal model**, and it's how every amplifier gain in this course is calculated.

## Transconductance

The slope of $i_D$ versus $v_{GS}$ at the operating point is the **transconductance**:

$$
g_m = \frac{\partial i_D}{\partial v_{GS}}\bigg|_{Q} = k_nV_{ov} = \frac{2I_D}{V_{ov}} = \sqrt{2k_nI_D}
$$

All three forms are equivalent; use whichever matches the numbers you have. The small-signal drain current is $i_d = g_mv_{gs}$.

## Output resistance

Channel-length modulation makes $i_D$ rise slightly with $v_{DS}$, which looks like a resistor from drain to source:

$$
r_o = \frac{1}{\lambda I_D}
$$

To analyze an amplifier: find the DC bias, compute $g_m$ and $r_o$, then **short DC voltage sources to ground and large capacitors to wires**, replace the transistor with this model, and solve the linear circuit.

## Intrinsic gain

The highest voltage gain one transistor can give is when it drives only its own $r_o$:

$$
A_0 = g_mr_o
$$

> **Worked example.** $I_D = 1$ mA, $V_{ov} = 0.2$ V, $\lambda = 0.1\ \text{V}^{-1}$.
>
> $g_m = 2I_D/V_{ov} = 10$ mA/V $= 10$ mS. $\quad r_o = 1/(0.1 \times 1\ \text{mA}) = 10$ kΩ. $\quad A_0 = 100$ (40 dB).

Designers trade these against each other: a small $V_{ov}$ gives high $g_m$ per milliamp (efficient), while a large $V_{ov}$ gives speed and linearity.

## Key takeaways

- Small-signal = linearize around the bias point.
- $g_m = k_nV_{ov} = 2I_D/V_{ov} = \sqrt{2k_nI_D}$; $r_o = 1/(\lambda I_D)$.
- Hybrid-π: open gate, $g_mv_{gs}$ current source, $r_o$. Intrinsic gain $g_mr_o$.
