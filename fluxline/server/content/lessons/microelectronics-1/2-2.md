## Setting the operating point

An amplifier must sit at a stable DC **operating point** (Q-point) in saturation, with enough room for the signal to swing. **Biasing** is the DC design that puts it there. The challenge: $V_t$ and $k_n$ vary widely from part to part and with temperature, so a good bias network must keep $I_D$ steady anyway.

## Voltage-divider bias with source degeneration

```circuit
W 1.2,-0.4 3,-0.4
TEXT 2.1,-0.75 "V_{DD} = 12 V"
R 1.2,-0.4 1.2,2 R_1
R 1.2,2 1.2,4.4 R_2
W 1.2,2 2,2
NMOS 3,2 ""
R 3,-0.4 3,1 "R_D = 3 kΩ"
R 3,3 3,4.4 "R_S = 1 kΩ"
W 1.2,4.4 3,4.4
GND 2.1,4.4
TEXT 0.9,2 "V_G = 4 V" anchor=end color=anno size=13
caption: Voltage-divider bias. $R_1$ and $R_2$ set the gate voltage; $R_S$ provides negative feedback that stabilizes $I_D$.
```

Since the gate draws no current, the divider sets $V_G$ exactly. Then $V_{GS} = V_G - I_DR_S$, and in saturation

$$
I_D = \tfrac12k_n\big(V_G - I_DR_S - V_t\big)^2
$$

a quadratic in $I_D$. Take the root that keeps $V_{GS} > V_t$.

> **Worked example.** $V_G = 4$ V, $R_S = 1$ kΩ, $R_D = 3$ kΩ, $V_{DD} = 12$ V, $k_n = 2$ mA/V², $V_t = 1$ V.
>
> With $I_D$ in mA: $I_D = (3 - I_D)^2$, so $I_D^2 - 7I_D + 9 = 0$, giving $I_D = 1.70$ mA or $5.30$ mA. The second root makes $V_{GS} = 4 - 5.3 < V_t$, which is impossible, so $I_D = 1.70$ mA.
>
> $V_{GS} = 2.30$ V, $V_{ov} = 1.30$ V. $V_D = 12 - 3(1.70) = 6.91$ V, $V_S = 1.70$ V, $V_{DS} = 5.21$ V.
>
> **Check saturation:** $V_{DS} = 5.21 \ge V_{ov} = 1.30$ ✓.

## Why the source resistor matters

$R_S$ is **negative feedback**: if $I_D$ tries to rise, $V_S$ rises, $V_{GS}$ falls, and $I_D$ is pulled back down.

> Suppose a different part has $V_t = 1.2$ V instead of 1 V.
> - **With** $R_S$: solving again gives $I_D = 1.55$ mA, an **8%** change.
> - **Without** $R_S$ (gate fixed at $V_{GS} = 2.30$ V): $I_D$ falls from 1.70 to 1.22 mA, a **28%** change.

The price is gain. $R_S$ also degenerates the AC signal, unless it's bypassed with a capacitor (Lesson 4.2).

## Design guidelines

- Choose the divider resistors large (hundreds of kΩ) to keep the input resistance high. The gate doesn't load them.
- Put roughly a third of $V_{DD}$ across $R_D$, a third across the transistor, and the rest across $R_S$. That leaves room for the drain voltage to swing both ways.
- Always verify the saturation condition at the end.

```quiz
? With $V_G = 3$ V, $R_S = 1$ kΩ, $k_n = 2$ mA/V², and $V_t = 1$ V, the bias equation is $I_D = (2 - I_D)^2$ (mA). What is $I_D$, in mA?
= 1 | 0.01 | mA
> $I_D^2 - 5I_D + 4 = 0$ gives 1 or 4 mA; 4 mA would make $V_{GS} = -1$ V, so $I_D = 1$ mA.
```

```quiz

= 5 | 0.01 | V
> $V_D = 10 - 4 = 6$ V, $V_S = 1$ V, so $V_{DS} = 5$ V (and $V_{ov} = 1$ V, so it's in saturation).
```

```quiz
? $V_{DD} = 15$ V, $R_1 = 200$ kΩ (top), $R_2 = 100$ kΩ (bottom). What is $V_G$, in V?
= 5 | 0.01 | V
> $15 \cdot \frac{100}{300} = 5$ V. No gate current flows, so the divider is unloaded.
```

```quiz
? Why does adding a source resistor $R_S$ make the bias point more stable?
+ It creates negative feedback: a rise in $I_D$ raises $V_S$, lowering $V_{GS}$
- It increases $V_t$
- It increases the gate current
- It moves the transistor into triode
> Feedback trades gain for predictability, a theme you'll see throughout electronics.
```

## Key takeaways

- Bias = choose the DC operating point; it must stay in saturation with room to swing.
- Divider plus source resistor: solve $I_D = \tfrac12k_n(V_G - I_DR_S - V_t)^2$ and keep the valid root.
- $R_S$ is negative feedback that stabilizes $I_D$ against device variation.
