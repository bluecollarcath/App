## Turning AC into DC

Almost every electronic device runs on DC, but the wall supplies AC. The first stage of a linear power supply is a **rectifier**, which uses diodes to let current flow one way only, followed by a **filter capacitor** that smooths the result.

## The half-wave rectifier

One diode passes the positive half-cycles and blocks the negative ones. The output peaks at $V_p = V_{s,peak} - 0.7$ V and is zero half the time.

```circuit
VAC 0,0 0,2 v_s side=-1
D 0,0 2,0 D
W 2,0 3,0
C 3,0 3,2 C side=-1
W 3,0 4.4,0
R 4.4,0 4.4,2 R_L v=v_o
W 0,2 3,2
W 3,2 4.4,2
caption: A half-wave rectifier with a filter capacitor across the load.
```

## The full-wave bridge

Four diodes in a **bridge** steer both half-cycles through the load in the same direction. The output pulses twice per input cycle (120 Hz from a 60 Hz line), so it's much easier to filter. The cost: current passes through **two** diodes, so the peak is $V_{s,peak} - 1.4$ V.

| Rectifier | Output peak | Ripple frequency | Peak inverse voltage per diode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Half-wave | $V_s - 0.7$ | $f$ | $V_s$ |
| Center-tapped full-wave | $V_s - 0.7$ (per half-winding) | $2f$ | $2V_s - 0.7$ |
| Bridge | $V_s - 1.4$ | $2f$ | $V_s - 0.7$ |

(Here $V_s$ is the peak secondary voltage.) The **peak inverse voltage (PIV)** is the largest reverse voltage a diode must block; choose diodes rated well above it.

## The filter capacitor and ripple

The capacitor charges to the peak, then discharges through the load until the next peak tops it up. If the discharge is small, it's nearly linear, and the **peak-to-peak ripple** is

$$
V_r \approx \frac{V_p}{f_{ripple}\,R_L\,C}
$$

where $f_{ripple}$ is $f$ for half-wave and $2f$ for full-wave. The DC output is about $V_p - V_r/2$.

> **Worked example.** A transformer gives 12 V rms at 60 Hz into a bridge rectifier, with $C = 2200\ \mu$F and $R_L = 100\ \Omega$.
>
> $V_{s,peak} = 12\sqrt2 = 16.97$ V, so $V_p = 16.97 - 1.4 = 15.57$ V.
>
> $V_r = \dfrac{15.57}{(120)(100)(2200\times10^{-6})} = 0.59$ V peak-to-peak, so $V_{DC} \approx 15.28$ V.
>
> A half-wave rectifier with the same capacitor would have twice the ripple: 1.18 V.

```plot
x: 0, 25, t (ms)
y: 0, 17.5, voltage (V)
f: 15.57*abs(sin(2*pi*0.06*x)) | rectified (no capacitor) | dot | 4
f: 15.57 - 0.59*mod(x - 4.1667, 8.3333)/8.3333 | output with C (approx.) | solid | 1
caption: With the filter capacitor, the output sags only 0.59 V between the 120 Hz charging peaks (the recharge is drawn as instantaneous).
```

A **voltage regulator** after the filter removes the remaining ripple. That's the next lesson.

## Practice

```quiz
? A bridge rectifier is fed 10 V peak. What is the peak output voltage, in V?
= 8.6 | 0.01 | V
> Two diode drops: $10 - 1.4 = 8.6$ V.
```

```quiz
? A full-wave rectifier (60 Hz line) has a 10 V peak, a 500 Ω load, and should have 0.5 V of ripple. What capacitance is needed, in µF?
= 333.3 | 0.01 | µF
> $C = \frac{V_p}{2fR_LV_r} = \frac{10}{120 \cdot 500 \cdot 0.5} = 333\ \mu$F.
```

```quiz
? In a center-tapped full-wave rectifier where each half of the secondary peaks at 17 V, what PIV must each diode withstand, in V?
= 33.3 | 0.01 | V
> $2V_s - 0.7 = 34 - 0.7 = 33.3$ V.
```

```quiz
? Doubling the load current (halving $R_L$) with the same capacitor makes the ripple:
- half as large
+ twice as large
- unchanged
- four times as large
> $V_r \propto 1/R_L$: more current drains the capacitor faster between peaks.
```

## Key takeaways

- Half-wave: one diode, ripple at $f$. Bridge: four diodes, two drops, ripple at $2f$.
- $V_r \approx V_p/(f_{ripple}R_LC)$; more capacitance or less load current means less ripple.
- Size diodes for the peak inverse voltage.
