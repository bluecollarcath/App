## An amplifier as a black box

To combine stages, we don't need the transistor details, just three numbers per stage. The **voltage-amplifier model**:

```circuit
V 0,0 0,2 v_s side=-1
R 0,0 2,0 R_s
W 2,0 2.6,0
R 2.6,0 2.6,2 R_{in} v=v_i side=-1
W 2.6,2 4.3,2
VD 4.3,2 4.3,0 "A_{vo} v_i" side=-1
R 4.3,0 6,0 R_{out}
W 6,0 6.8,0
R 6.8,0 6.8,2 R_L v=v_o
W 0,2 2.6,2
W 4.3,2 6.8,2
LINE 2.25,-0.75 6.35,-0.75 dash
LINE 6.35,-0.75 6.35,2.45 dash
LINE 6.35,2.45 2.25,2.45 dash
LINE 2.25,2.45 2.25,-0.75 dash
TEXT 4.3,-1 amplifier color=muted size=13
caption: The voltage-amplifier model: input resistance $R_{in}$, open-circuit gain $A_{vo}$, and output resistance $R_{out}$.
```

- **$R_{in}$**: what the source sees.
- **$A_{vo}$**: the gain with no load connected (open circuit).
- **$R_{out}$**: the Thévenin resistance at the output.

## Loading: where gain gets lost

Two voltage dividers sit on either side of the ideal gain:

$$
A_v = \frac{v_o}{v_s} = \underbrace{\frac{R_{in}}{R_s + R_{in}}}_{\text{input divider}} \cdot A_{vo} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{R_L}{R_L + R_{out}}}_{\text{output divider}}
$$

> **Worked example.** $R_s = 10$ kΩ, $R_{in} = 100$ kΩ, $A_{vo} = 50$, $R_{out} = 1$ kΩ, $R_L = 4$ kΩ.
> $$A_v = \frac{100}{110}(50)\frac{4}{5} = 36.4\ \ (31.2\ \text{dB})$$
> More than a quarter of the ideal gain is lost to loading.

**An ideal voltage amplifier** has $R_{in} \to \infty$ and $R_{out} \to 0$. Real designs approach that by combining stages.

## Cascading stages

The overall gain of a cascade is the product of each stage's *loaded* gain, where each stage's $R_{in}$ is the load for the stage before it. In decibels, the gains add.

> **Why buffers matter.** A gain stage with $R_{in} = 1$ MΩ, $A_{vo} = -20$, $R_{out} = 10$ kΩ must drive a 1 kΩ load from a 50 kΩ source.
>
> **Directly:** $A_v = \frac{1000}{1050}(-20)\frac{1}{11} = -1.73$. The heavy load wastes 91% of the gain.
>
> **With a follower in between** ($R_{in} = 100$ kΩ, $A_{vo} = 0.95$, $R_{out} = 100\ \Omega$):
> $$A_v = \frac{1000}{1050}(-20)\frac{100}{110}(0.95)\frac{1000}{1100} = -15.0$$
> Almost 9× more gain, from a stage whose own gain is less than 1.

That's the classic structure: **input stage** (high $R_{in}$) → **gain stage(s)** → **output buffer** (low $R_{out}$). An op-amp is built exactly this way.

## Other amplifier types

Depending on whether the input and output are best treated as voltages or currents, there are four models: **voltage** (V→V, gain in V/V), **current** (I→I, A/A), **transconductance** (V→I, A/V, like a single transistor), and **transresistance** (I→V, V/A, used for photodiode receivers).

## Key takeaways

- Model each stage by $R_{in}$, $A_{vo}$, $R_{out}$.
- $A_v = \frac{R_{in}}{R_s + R_{in}}A_{vo}\frac{R_L}{R_L + R_{out}}$; loading always costs gain.
- Cascade: multiply loaded gains (add dB). Buffers rescue gain into heavy loads.
