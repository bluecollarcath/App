## The power wall

Around 2005, chips hit a **power wall**: clock frequencies stopped rising, not because transistors couldn't switch faster, but because chips couldn't be cooled. A desktop processor dissipates 100–250 W in a few square centimeters, a power density higher than a hot plate. A phone chip has only 3–5 W to spend. Power is now the first constraint in almost every design.

## Where the power goes

$$
P = \underbrace{\alpha C V_{DD}^2 f}_{\text{dynamic}} + \underbrace{P_{sc}}_{\text{short-circuit}} + \underbrace{V_{DD}I_{leak}}_{\text{leakage}}
$$

**Dynamic power** (Lesson 1.1) charges and discharges capacitance: each 0→1 transition draws $CV_{DD}^2$ from the supply, half stored on the capacitor and half burned in the PMOS, and the stored half is burned in the NMOS on the way down. The **activity factor** $\alpha$ is the probability a node switches 0→1 per cycle: about 0.1 for typical logic, 1 for the clock.

> **Example.** A chip with 50 nF of total switched capacitance, $\alpha = 0.1$, 0.8 V and 2 GHz: $P = 0.1 \times 50\times10^{-9} \times 0.64 \times 2\times10^9 = 6.4$ W.

**Short-circuit power** flows briefly while a gate's input is mid-transition and both networks conduct. With crisp input edges it's typically under 10% of dynamic power.

**Leakage** flows even when nothing switches: subthreshold conduction (Semiconductor Devices, Lesson 3.3), gate tunneling and junction leakage. It grows exponentially as $V_T$ falls and roughly doubles every 10–20 °C, and in modern processes it can be a third of the total.

## Reducing power

| Technique | Attacks | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Clock gating** | $\alpha$ | stop the clock to idle blocks; the single most common technique |
| **Voltage scaling (DVFS)** | $V^2$, $f$ | lower $V_{DD}$ and $f$ together when full speed isn't needed |
| **Multiple $V_T$** | leakage | high-$V_T$ cells off the critical path, fast low-$V_T$ cells only where needed |
| **Power gating** | leakage | header/footer switches cut the supply to sleeping blocks |
| **Multiple voltage domains** | $V^2$ | run slow blocks at lower voltage, with level shifters between |
| **Architecture** | everything | parallelism at lower voltage, accelerators, data locality |

**Why DVFS is so powerful:** gate delay rises as voltage falls, so frequency must drop roughly in proportion to voltage, giving $P \propto V^2f \propto V^3$. Running 20% slower at 20% lower voltage cuts dynamic power to $0.8^3 = 51\%$, and the energy per operation ($\propto V^2$) to 64%.

**Parallelism trades area for power.** Two cores at half the frequency (and lower voltage) can match one fast core's throughput with much less power. That's the reason for multicore processors.

## Energy vs. power

Battery life depends on **energy per task**, not power. Running fast and then sleeping deeply ("race to halt", Embedded Systems Lesson 3.3) often wins when leakage is high; running slowly at low voltage wins when dynamic energy dominates. Mobile chips constantly choose between them.

## Key takeaways

- $P = \alpha CV^2f + P_{sc} + VI_{leak}$; dynamic power dominates when active, leakage when idle.
- Clock gating reduces $\alpha$; DVFS gives roughly $P \propto V^3$; multi-$V_T$ and power gating fight leakage.
- Parallelism at lower voltage saves power; battery life depends on energy per task.
