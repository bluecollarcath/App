## How a chip is made

A modern chip is built on a polished silicon **wafer** (300 mm across) in hundreds of steps, mostly repetitions of a few basic operations:

1. **Oxidation / deposition:** grow or deposit thin films (oxides, nitrides, polysilicon, metals).
2. **Photolithography:** coat with light-sensitive **photoresist**, expose through a **mask** (reticle) carrying one layer's pattern, and develop. Leading-edge processes use 13.5 nm **extreme ultraviolet** (EUV) light; earlier layers use 193 nm deep UV, often patterned several times to draw features smaller than the wavelength.
3. **Etching:** remove material where the resist doesn't protect it (plasma etching for sharp, vertical walls).
4. **Ion implantation:** fire dopant ions into the silicon to form wells, sources and drains, then anneal to repair the crystal and activate the dopants.
5. **Chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP):** grind each layer flat so the next can be patterned precisely.

The **front end of line** (FEOL) builds the transistors: wells, isolation trenches, gate stack, source/drain. The **back end of line** (BEOL) builds the wiring: 10 to 15+ layers of copper interconnect separated by low-k dielectrics, connected by **vias**, with thin, dense wires at the bottom and thick, fast ones at the top (Lesson 2.3).

## Layout and design rules

A chip's **layout** is the set of polygons on each mask layer: n-well, active (diffusion), polysilicon (gate), contacts, metal 1, via 1, metal 2, and so on. A transistor exists wherever polysilicon crosses active.

**Design rules** guarantee manufacturability despite misalignment and process variation: minimum widths, spacings, enclosures and overlaps for every layer. Classic teaching rules express them in units of **λ** (half the minimum feature size), for example: minimum poly width 2λ, poly-to-poly spacing 3λ, contacts 2λ × 2λ. Real advanced-node rule decks run to thousands of rules, checked automatically by **DRC** (design-rule checking) software.

## Stick diagrams and Euler paths

A **stick diagram** sketches a cell's topology (lines for diffusion, poly and metal, without dimensions) to plan the layout. A key trick: if the transistors of the pull-up and pull-down networks can be ordered along a single unbroken strip of diffusion, adjacent transistors share source/drain regions, saving area and capacitance. That requires a common **Euler path** (a path through the network's graph that uses each edge once) with the same input order in both networks.

> For $\overline{AB + C}$, the order A–B–C works for both networks, so each fits on one diffusion strip.

**Standard cells** (inverters, NANDs, flip-flops...) are laid out at a common height, with power and ground rails along the top and bottom, so that placement tools can line them up in rows (Lesson 3.3).

## Yield and cost

Random defects kill some chips. With defect density $D_0$ (defects per unit area) and die area $A$, the simple Poisson model gives the fraction of good dies:

$$
Y = e^{-D_0A}
$$

> A 300 mm wafer holds about 640 dies of 100 mm². With $D_0 = 0.1$ cm⁻² and $A = 1$ cm², $Y = e^{-0.1} = 90.5\%$. At \$10,000 per processed wafer, each good die costs $10\,000/(640 \times 0.905) \approx 17$ dollars.

Cost grows **faster than linearly** with die area (fewer dies per wafer, and lower yield), which is why big chips are expensive, and why large designs are increasingly split into **chiplets** joined in one package.

## Key takeaways

- Chips are built by repeated deposition, lithography, etching, implantation and polishing; FEOL makes transistors, BEOL makes wiring.
- Layout = polygons per mask layer; design rules (checked by DRC) ensure manufacturability.
- Stick diagrams and Euler paths minimize cell area; standard cells share a common height.
- Yield $Y = e^{-D_0A}$; cost per good die rises steeply with area.
