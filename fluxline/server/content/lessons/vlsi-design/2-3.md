## Wires are now the bottleneck

Transistors get faster as they shrink; wires get **slower**. A wire's resistance per unit length rises as its cross-section shrinks, while its capacitance per unit length stays roughly constant (about 0.2 fF/µm, because width and spacing shrink together). In modern chips, long wires, not gates, set the speed of many paths, and they burn a large share of the power.

## Wire resistance and capacitance

Wire resistance is quoted per square: $R = R_\square L/W$, with $R_\square = \rho/t$. Thin lower-level wires might be 0.5–5 Ω/µm; thick top-level wires much less. Copper replaced aluminum around 2000 for its lower resistivity, and at the narrowest dimensions even copper suffers from surface scattering, prompting moves to cobalt and ruthenium.

Capacitance comes from the wire's area and fringing fields to the layers above and below and to its **neighbors**. Side-wall coupling to neighbors is often the largest part.

## Distributed RC delay

A wire is a distributed RC line. With resistance $r$ and capacitance $c$ per unit length, the **Elmore delay** of a wire of length $L$ is

$$
t \approx \frac{rcL^2}{2}
$$

**Quadratic in length**: double the wire, four times the delay.

> A 1 mm wire with $r = 0.5\ \Omega$/µm and $c = 0.2$ fF/µm has $R = 500\ \Omega$, $C = 200$ fF, and delay $\approx 50$ ps. A 5 mm wire takes 1.25 ns, longer than a whole clock cycle at 1 GHz.

## Repeaters

Breaking a long wire into $k$ segments with an inverter (**repeater**) between each makes the total delay **linear** in length: each segment's delay falls as $1/k^2$, while the repeaters add a fixed delay each. There's an optimal spacing and repeater size, typically every few hundred microns to a millimeter. Modern chips contain hundreds of thousands of repeaters.

```plot
caption: Unrepeated wire delay grows with the square of length; repeaters make it linear, a large win for wires longer than about a millimeter.
```

The other fix is to route long, critical wires on the **thick upper metal layers**, which have much lower resistance.

## Crosstalk

Coupling capacitance between neighboring wires causes **crosstalk**:

- **Noise:** a switching **aggressor** injects a glitch onto a quiet **victim** wire, which can propagate as a logic error or false clock edge.
- **Delay variation:** if the neighbor switches in the **opposite** direction, the coupling capacitance sees twice the voltage swing and effectively doubles (a Miller factor of 2); in the **same** direction, it effectively disappears (factor 0). A wire's delay can vary by tens of percent depending on what its neighbors do.

Defenses: wider spacing, **shielding** with grounded wires (standard for clocks), alternating routing directions on adjacent layers, and timing analysis that accounts for worst-case switching windows (Lesson 3.3).

## Key takeaways

- Wire resistance rises as wires shrink; capacitance per length stays near 0.2 fF/µm.
- Distributed RC delay $\approx rcL^2/2$: quadratic in length.
- Repeaters make delay linear; thick top metal helps long routes.
- Crosstalk causes glitches and delay variation (Miller factor 0–2); shield and space critical nets.
