## From an idea to a tapeout

A modern chip has billions of transistors; nobody draws them by hand. An **ASIC** (application-specific integrated circuit) is designed through a largely automated flow, driven by **EDA** (electronic design automation) tools, with engineers writing code, setting constraints and fixing what the tools can't.

## The flow

1. **Specification and architecture:** what the chip does, its performance, power and area targets, and how it's divided into blocks.
2. **RTL design:** describe each block's behavior at the register-transfer level in Verilog, SystemVerilog or VHDL (Digital Logic, Unit 4).
3. **Verification:** prove the RTL does what the spec says, using simulation with self-checking testbenches, coverage metrics, formal verification and emulation. Verification typically takes more effort than design itself, because a bug found after manufacturing costs millions.
4. **Logic synthesis:** the tool maps RTL to a netlist of **standard cells** (Lesson 1.3) from the foundry's library, optimizing for timing, area and power under constraints (clock periods, input/output delays).
5. **Physical design:**
   - **floorplanning:** place large blocks, memories and I/O; plan the power grid;
   - **placement:** put millions of standard cells in rows;
   - **clock-tree synthesis:** build and balance the clock network (Lesson 3.1);
   - **routing:** connect everything on the metal layers.
6. **Sign-off:** **static timing analysis** across all process corners and conditions, power and IR-drop analysis, **DRC** (design rules) and **LVS** (layout versus schematic: the layout matches the netlist).
7. **Tapeout:** send the final layout database (GDSII or OASIS) to the foundry to make masks. Then come wafer fabrication (about 3 months), packaging, testing and bring-up.

## Static timing analysis

STA checks every timing path without simulation: for each path it adds up cell and wire delays to find the **arrival time**, compares it with the **required time** from the clock constraints, and reports the **slack**:

$$
\text{slack} = t_{required} - t_{arrival}
$$

Positive slack passes; negative slack is a violation to fix (by resizing cells, restructuring logic, adding pipeline stages or rerouting). STA runs at multiple **corners**: slow transistors, low voltage and high temperature for setup; fast corners for hold.

> A path must arrive by 1000 ps; it arrives at 930 ps. Slack $= +70$ ps. A hold check on the same path requires arrival after 40 ps; the shortest path arrives at 25 ps: slack $= -15$ ps, so the tool adds delay buffers.

## Design for test

Every manufactured chip must be tested. **Scan chains** connect the flip-flops into long shift registers in test mode, so that automatic test-pattern generation (ATPG) can load any state, clock once and read out the result, detecting manufacturing faults with high coverage. **Built-in self-test** (BIST) tests memories on-chip.

## ASIC, FPGA or off-the-shelf?

An ASIC has a large **non-recurring engineering** (NRE) cost (design teams, tools, and masks costing millions at advanced nodes) but a low unit cost and the best performance and power. An FPGA has almost no NRE but a high unit cost and less efficiency. The break-even volume:

$$
N_{break-even} = \frac{\text{NRE}}{c_{FPGA} - c_{ASIC}}
$$

> With \$2M of NRE, \$5 per ASIC and \$50 per FPGA: break-even at about 44,000 units. Below that, use the FPGA.

## Key takeaways

- Flow: spec → RTL → verification → synthesis → floorplan, placement, CTS, routing → sign-off (STA, DRC, LVS) → tapeout.
- Verification dominates effort; STA checks every path for setup and hold slack across corners.
- Scan chains and BIST make chips testable.
- ASICs win at high volume (NRE / unit-cost difference sets the break-even); FPGAs below it.
