## The most important circuit in the world

Billions of CMOS inverters operate in every modern processor. Every other static CMOS gate is a generalization of it, so understanding its static and dynamic behavior is understanding digital IC design.

## Structure and operation

A PMOS transistor connects the output to $V_{DD}$; an NMOS connects it to ground. Both gates connect to the input.

- Input low: PMOS on, NMOS off → output pulled to $V_{DD}$.
- Input high: NMOS on, PMOS off → output pulled to 0.

```circuit
LINE 1.6,0 2.4,0
TEXT 2,-0.3 V_{DD}
PMOS 2,1 M_p
NMOS 2,3 M_n
W 1,1 1,2
W 1,2 1,3
T 0,2 V_{in}
W 0,2 1,2
W 2,2 3.6,2
T 3.6,2 V_{out}
GND 2,4
caption: The CMOS inverter: a PMOS pull-up and an NMOS pull-down share one input. Exactly one of them conducts in each steady state.
```

In either steady state there is **no path from $V_{DD}$ to ground**, so static power is (ideally) zero, and the output swings rail to rail. This is why CMOS replaced every earlier logic family.

## The voltage transfer characteristic

Plotting $V_{out}$ vs. $V_{in}$ gives a steep curve with high gain in the transition region. Key points:

- **Switching threshold $V_M$**: where $V_{in} = V_{out}$. Equating NMOS and PMOS saturation currents (long-channel, equal thresholds):
$$ V_M = \frac{V_{tn} + r(V_{DD} - |V_{tp}|)}{1 + r}, \qquad r = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_p (W/L)_p}{\mu_n (W/L)_n}} $$
Since $\mu_n \approx 2$–$3\mu_p$, the PMOS is usually made wider to center $V_M$ near $V_{DD}/2$.
```plot
x: 0, 1, V_{in} (V)
y: 0, 1.05, V_{out} (V)
f: 0.5*(1 - tanh(9*(x - 0.5))) | V_{out}, V_{DD} = 1 V
f: x | V_{out} = V_{in} | dot | 4
mark: 0.5, 0.5, V_M
caption: The inverter's voltage transfer characteristic (illustrative shape). The steep middle gives high gain; the flat ends give noise margins.
```

- **Noise margins**: $NM_H = V_{OH} - V_{IH}$, $NM_L = V_{IL} - V_{OL}$, where $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ are the unity-gain points. Large noise margins make digital logic **regenerative**: noise is removed at every stage.

## Dynamic power

Each time the output charges a load capacitance $C$ from 0 to $V_{DD}$, energy $CV_{DD}^2$ is drawn from the supply (half stored, half dissipated in the PMOS; the stored half is dissipated in the NMOS on discharge). With activity factor $\alpha$ (the probability of a 0→1 transition per cycle):

$$
P_{dyn} = \alpha\,C\,V_{DD}^2\,f
$$

The **quadratic** dependence on $V_{DD}$ is why supply voltages fell from 5 V to under 1 V, and why dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS) is so effective.

## Practice

```quiz
? A node with α = 0.2, C = 10 fF, $V_{DD}$ = 0.8 V switches at 1 GHz. What is its dynamic power, in µW?
= 1.28 | 0.01 | µW
> $0.2 \times 10^{-14} \times 0.64 \times 10^9 = 1.28$ µW.
```

```quiz
? If $V_{DD}$ is reduced by 30% (frequency unchanged), dynamic power falls to what fraction of the original?
= 0.49 | 0.01 |
> $(0.7)^2 = 0.49$.
```

```quiz
? Why is the PMOS in a typical inverter made wider than the NMOS?
+ Hole mobility is lower than electron mobility
- PMOS has a higher threshold voltage by definition
- To reduce input capacitance
- To lower static power
> Widening the PMOS equalizes drive strength, centering $V_M$ and balancing rise/fall times.
```

## Key takeaways

- CMOS: complementary pull-up/pull-down, zero ideal static power, rail-to-rail swing.
- $V_M$ is set by the PMOS/NMOS strength ratio.
- $P_{dyn} = \alpha CV_{DD}^2 f$.
