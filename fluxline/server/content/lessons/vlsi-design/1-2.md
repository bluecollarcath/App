## Beyond the inverter

Every static CMOS gate generalizes the inverter (Lesson 1.1): a **pull-down network** (PDN) of NMOS transistors connects the output to ground when the function should be 0, and a complementary **pull-up network** (PUN) of PMOS transistors connects it to $V_{DD}$ when it should be 1. Exactly one network conducts for any input, so there's no static current, and the output swings fully from rail to rail.

The two networks are **duals**: series NMOS transistors become parallel PMOS transistors, and vice versa.

```circuit
caption: A two-input CMOS NAND gate: parallel PMOS pull-ups, series NMOS pull-downs. The output is low only when both A and B are high.
```

- **NAND:** NMOS in **series** (the output pulls low only if A **and** B are 1), PMOS in **parallel**. 4 transistors.
- **NOR:** NMOS in **parallel**, PMOS in **series**. 4 transistors.

Because static CMOS gates are inherently **inverting** (a high input turns on an NMOS that pulls low), the natural gates are NAND, NOR and their complex cousins; AND and OR need an extra inverter.

## Sizing for equal drive

A gate's worst-case drive should match a reference inverter's. Transistors in series add resistance, so they must be widened. Taking a unit NMOS as width 1 and assuming PMOS needs twice the width for equal resistance (hole mobility is about half, Semiconductor Devices Lesson 1.3), a reference inverter is NMOS 1, PMOS 2:

| Gate | NMOS width | PMOS width | Input capacitance (per input) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inverter | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| NAND2 | 2 (two in series) | 2 | 4 |
| NOR2 | 1 | 4 (two in series) | 5 |

So a NOR2 presents more capacitance than a NAND2 for the same drive, because its slow PMOS transistors are the ones in series. That's why **NAND-based logic is preferred** in CMOS, and why gates with more than about four series transistors are avoided (they get big and slow).

## Complex gates

One gate can implement any inverting sum-of-products function. For $Y = \overline{AB + C}$ (an AOI21, "AND-OR-INVERT"):

- PDN: (A series B) in parallel with C;
- PUN: the dual, (A parallel B) in series with C.

That's 6 transistors, versus 10 for a separate AND2, OR2 and inverter: smaller, faster and lower power. Standard-cell libraries include dozens of these AOI and OAI gates.

## Other logic styles

- **Pass-transistor and transmission-gate logic**: efficient for multiplexers and XORs.
- **Dynamic (domino) logic**: precharge, then evaluate through an NMOS network only; faster, but needs a clock and is sensitive to noise and leakage.
- **Static CMOS** remains the default because it's robust: full swing, no static power, high noise margins.

## Key takeaways

- Static CMOS: NMOS pull-down and dual PMOS pull-up networks; inherently inverting; no static current.
- NAND: series NMOS, parallel PMOS. NOR: the reverse.
- Size series transistors wider; NOR's series PMOS make it costlier than NAND.
- Complex AOI/OAI gates implement inverting sum-of-products functions compactly.
