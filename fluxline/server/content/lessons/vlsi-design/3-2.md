## Most of the chip is memory

Caches, register files and buffers often occupy more than half of a processor's area, and memory chips are the largest market in semiconductors. Memories are built as dense **arrays** of tiny cells with shared periphery: decoders that select a row, **bitlines** that carry data, and **sense amplifiers** that detect small signals quickly.

## Array organization

A memory of $2^n$ words is arranged as a roughly square array, with $2^r$ rows and $2^c$ columns:

- the **row decoder** turns $r$ address bits into one active **wordline**;
- every cell on that row connects to its **bitline**;
- **column multiplexers** pick the bits of the addressed word, using the remaining $c$ address bits.

> A 1 Mb array organized as 1024 × 1024 needs 10 row-address bits and 10 column-address bits. Square arrays keep wordlines and bitlines short, both of which set the speed.

## SRAM: the 6T cell

A static RAM cell stores a bit in two **cross-coupled inverters** (a latch), with two **access transistors** connecting the storage nodes to a complementary pair of bitlines when the wordline is high. Six transistors per bit; it holds its value as long as power is on, with no refresh.

- **Read:** precharge both bitlines high, raise the wordline; the side storing 0 slowly discharges its bitline. A sense amplifier detects a difference of only 50–100 mV, long before the bitline swings fully, which is the key to speed.
- **Read stability:** the cell's pull-down NMOS must be stronger than its access transistor (the **cell ratio**, typically 1.5–2), or reading flips the bit.
- **Write:** drive one bitline low; the access transistor must overpower the cell's PMOS pull-up (the **pull-up ratio**), so the PMOS is made weak.

These conflicting sizing constraints, plus variation in tiny transistors, make SRAM cells hard to shrink and limit how low their supply voltage can go.

## DRAM: one transistor, one capacitor

A DRAM cell stores a bit as charge on a capacitor (about 10–25 fF) through one access transistor: far denser than SRAM. But reading is destructive and the signal is tiny. When the wordline opens, the cell **shares charge** with a bitline precharged to $V_{DD}/2$, whose capacitance is several times larger:

$$
\Delta V = \frac{V_{DD}}{2}\cdot\frac{C_s}{C_s + C_{BL}}
$$

> With $V_{DD} = 1.1$ V, $C_s = 25$ fF and $C_{BL} = 100$ fF: $\Delta V = 0.55 \times 25/125 = 110$ mV. The sense amplifier amplifies that to a full level and writes it back, restoring the cell.

Because the capacitor leaks, every cell must be **refreshed** (read and rewritten) every 64 ms or so. DRAM is built on a specialized process with tall capacitors, and supplies main memory; SRAM, built in the logic process, supplies caches.

## Flash and beyond

**Flash memory** stores charge on a floating gate or in a charge-trap layer, shifting the transistor's threshold voltage; it's non-volatile but slow to write and wears out. NAND flash stacks over 200 layers of cells vertically and stores 3–4 bits per cell by distinguishing 8–16 threshold levels. Emerging memories (MRAM, ReRAM, phase-change) aim to combine non-volatility with speed.

## Key takeaways

- Arrays: row decoder → wordline, bitlines → column mux and sense amplifiers; keep arrays roughly square.
- SRAM 6T: latch plus two access transistors; sizing balances read stability and writability.
- DRAM 1T1C: charge sharing gives $\Delta V = (V_{DD}/2)C_s/(C_s + C_{BL})$; destructive reads, refresh every ~64 ms.
- Flash stores charge to shift $V_T$; multi-level, 3-D stacked.
