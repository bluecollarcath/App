## Keeping everything in step

Almost all digital chips are **synchronous**: flip-flops capture new values on each clock edge, and the combinational logic between them must finish within one cycle. Digital Logic (Unit 3) introduced setup and hold times; at the chip level, the questions are how fast the clock can run and how to deliver it to millions of flip-flops at once.

## The two timing constraints

For a path from one flip-flop, through logic, to the next:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $t_{pcq}$ / $t_{ccq}$ | clock-to-Q propagation (max) / contamination (min) delay |
| $t_{pd}$ / $t_{cd}$ | logic propagation (longest) / contamination (shortest) delay |
| $t_{setup}$, $t_{hold}$ | flip-flop setup and hold requirements |
| $t_{skew}$ | difference in clock arrival times between the two flip-flops |

**Setup (max-delay) constraint:** data launched at one edge must arrive before the next edge, minus setup time:

$$
T_c \ge t_{pcq} + t_{pd} + t_{setup} + t_{skew}
$$

**Hold (min-delay) constraint:** new data must not race through and overwrite the value being captured at the **same** edge:

$$
t_{ccq} + t_{cd} \ge t_{hold} + t_{skew}
$$

> **Example.** At 1 GHz ($T_c = 1000$ ps) with $t_{pcq} = 50$ ps, $t_{setup} = 30$ ps and 20 ps of skew, the logic may take at most $1000 - 100 = 900$ ps.

A setup violation is fixed by slowing the clock or speeding up the logic. A **hold violation can't be fixed by changing the clock frequency**: the chip fails at any speed. Tools fix it by inserting buffers on short paths, which is why hold checks are critical.

## Skew and jitter

- **Skew** is a spatial difference: the same edge reaches different flip-flops at different times, because of wire lengths, buffer variation and process, voltage and temperature differences across the die. It eats into both setup and hold margins.
- **Jitter** is a temporal variation: the period itself fluctuates from cycle to cycle, from PLL noise and supply noise. It reduces the usable cycle time.

## Clock distribution

The clock is the largest, most heavily loaded net on the chip, often 20–40% of the power. Distribution schemes:

- **H-trees:** recursively symmetric wiring so every leaf is equidistant from the source.
- **Clock meshes (grids):** a metal grid driven at many points, shorting out local skew differences; robust but power-hungry, used in high-performance processors.
- **Clock-tree synthesis** (Lesson 3.3): tools build buffered trees and balance them automatically.

Large designs also use **multiple clock domains**, and signals crossing between them need **synchronizers**.

## Metastability

If a flip-flop's input changes too close to the clock edge (as happens with any asynchronous input), its output can hang between 0 and 1 for an unpredictable time. The probability of still being unresolved after waiting a time $t_r$ falls exponentially, with a time constant $\tau$ set by the flip-flop's internal gain-bandwidth. The mean time between failures of a synchronizer is

$$
\text{MTBF} = \frac{e^{t_r/\tau}}{T_0\,f_{clk}\,f_{data}}
$$

> With $\tau = 25$ ps, $T_0 = 100$ ps, a 1 GHz clock and 100 MHz of data transitions, waiting one full cycle ($t_r = 1$ ns) gives $\text{MTBF} = e^{40}/10^7 \approx 2.4\times10^{10}$ s, about 750 years. Halving the wait to 0.5 ns gives about 50 seconds.

That's why every asynchronous signal passes through a **two-flip-flop synchronizer**: the second stage gives the first a full cycle to resolve. Multi-bit values cross domains through handshakes or asynchronous FIFOs with Gray-coded pointers, never as raw parallel bits.

## Key takeaways

- Setup: $T_c \ge t_{pcq} + t_{pd} + t_{setup} + t_{skew}$. Hold: $t_{ccq} + t_{cd} \ge t_{hold} + t_{skew}$; hold failures don't depend on frequency.
- Skew (spatial) and jitter (temporal) reduce margins; clock trees and meshes distribute the clock.
- Metastability MTBF $= e^{t_r/\tau}/(T_0f_{clk}f_{data})$; synchronize asynchronous signals with two flip-flops.
