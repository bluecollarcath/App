## Breakdown

Under large enough reverse bias, current suddenly increases. This **breakdown** isn't damage if the current is limited; Zener diodes and transient-voltage suppressors operate there deliberately. Two mechanisms:

- **Avalanche multiplication.** In a strong field, a carrier gains enough energy between collisions to knock an electron out of a silicon bond, creating a new pair. Those carriers do the same, in a chain reaction. Avalanche dominates in lightly doped junctions with wide depletion regions, at breakdown voltages above about 7 V. Higher doping means a higher peak field for a given voltage, so the breakdown voltage falls roughly as $N^{-3/4}$ of the lightly doped side's doping.
- **Zener (tunneling) breakdown.** In very heavily doped junctions, the depletion region is so thin (under about 10 nm) that electrons **tunnel** straight through the bandgap from the p-side valence band to the n-side conduction band. It dominates below about 5 V.

Between 5 and 7 V both act. They have opposite temperature coefficients (avalanche voltage rises with temperature because more lattice vibration means more scattering; tunneling voltage falls because the bandgap shrinks), which is why 5.6 V Zener diodes are the most temperature-stable. Power devices that must block hundreds or thousands of volts use very lightly doped, thick drift regions to keep the field below the avalanche limit.

## Junction capacitance

A reverse-biased junction stores charge in its depletion region, like a parallel-plate capacitor whose plate spacing is the depletion width:

$$
C_j = \frac{\varepsilon_sA}{W} = \frac{C_{j0}}{\sqrt{1 + V_R/V_{bi}}}
$$

Increasing reverse bias widens $W$ and reduces $C_j$. **Varactor** diodes exploit this as voltage-tuned capacitors in VCOs and TV tuners.

> **Example.** The junction of Lesson 2.1 ($W = 0.33\ \mu$m) with $A = 10^{-4}$ cm² has $C_{j0} = 1.04\times10^{-12}\times10^{-4}/3.3\times10^{-5} = 3.1$ pF. At 5 V reverse bias, $W = 0.91\ \mu$m and $C_j = 1.1$ pF.

Under forward bias, the injected minority charge adds a much larger **diffusion capacitance** $C_d = g_d\tau$ (with $g_d = I/V_T$). Removing that stored charge is what slows a diode's turn-off (**reverse recovery**), a central issue in power electronics; fast-recovery diodes use short lifetimes.

## Metal–semiconductor contacts

Every device needs metal connections, and a metal–semiconductor junction can behave in two very different ways.

**Schottky contact (rectifying).** On lightly doped silicon, the difference between the metal's work function and the semiconductor's electron affinity forms a barrier $\phi_B$. The contact rectifies like a diode, but:

- current is carried by **majority** carriers flowing over the barrier, so there's no stored minority charge and no reverse recovery: Schottky diodes switch extremely fast;
- the barrier is lower than a p–n junction's, so the forward drop is only about 0.2–0.4 V.

The cost is higher reverse leakage. Schottky diodes are the standard choice in switching power supplies and RF detectors, and SiC Schottky diodes handle kilovolts.

**Ohmic contact.** On very heavily doped (degenerate) semiconductor, the barrier still exists but the depletion layer is only a few nanometers thick, so carriers **tunnel** through freely in both directions. The contact acts as a small linear resistance. That's why every source, drain and emitter contact in an IC sits on a heavily doped region.

## Key takeaways

- Breakdown: tunneling (heavy doping, < 5 V) or avalanche (lighter doping, > 7 V), with opposite temperature coefficients.
- Junction capacitance $C_j = \varepsilon_sA/W \propto 1/\sqrt{1 + V_R/V_{bi}}$; forward bias adds diffusion capacitance $g_d\tau$.
- Schottky contacts rectify with majority carriers: low drop, fast, leakier. Ohmic contacts use heavy doping so carriers tunnel through.
