## How carriers move

Current in a semiconductor flows by two quite different mechanisms. **Drift** is motion pushed by an electric field, as in a metal. **Diffusion** is motion from regions of high concentration to low, driven only by random thermal motion. Most interesting device behavior, including the diode and the bipolar transistor, comes from diffusion, which barely matters in metals.

## Drift and mobility

An electron in a crystal is accelerated by a field, then scattered by lattice vibrations and impurities every fraction of a picosecond. On average it settles at a **drift velocity** proportional to the field:

$$
v_d = \mu E
$$

The proportionality constant is the **mobility** $\mu$, in cm²/V·s. In lightly doped silicon at 300 K, $\mu_n \approx 1350$ and $\mu_p \approx 480$ cm²/V·s. Electrons are about three times more mobile than holes, which is why NMOS transistors are faster than PMOS of the same size, and why PMOS devices are drawn wider in CMOS logic. Mobility falls with heavy doping (more impurity scattering) and with temperature (more lattice scattering).

The resulting current density and conductivity are

$$
J_{drift} = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)E = \sigma E, \qquad \rho = \frac{1}{\sigma}
$$

> **Example.** n-type silicon with $N_D = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³: $\sigma = 1.6\times10^{-19} \times 10^{16} \times 1350 = 2.16$ S/cm, so $\rho = 0.46\ \Omega\cdot$cm. Intrinsic silicon is about 300,000 Ω·cm, so a part-per-million of doping changed the resistivity by a factor of 600,000.

**Velocity saturation.** The linear relation fails at high fields: above about $10^4$ V/cm, carriers lose energy to the lattice as fast as they gain it, and the velocity saturates near $10^7$ cm/s in silicon. In a modern transistor with a channel tens of nanometers long, even 1 V produces fields of $10^5$ V/cm, so carriers travel at saturated velocity. That changes the MOSFET's current law (Lesson 3.3).

## Diffusion

Carriers wander randomly. Where their concentration is higher, more wander away than wander in, so there's a net flow down the concentration gradient. For electrons and holes:

$$
J_{n,diff} = qD_n\frac{dn}{dx}, \qquad J_{p,diff} = -qD_p\frac{dp}{dx}
$$

with **diffusion coefficients** $D$ in cm²/s. (The sign differs because holes carry positive charge: holes diffusing in $+x$ make current in $+x$; electrons diffusing in $+x$ make current in $-x$.) The total current is drift plus diffusion for each carrier type.

## The Einstein relation

Drift and diffusion both come from the same random thermal motion, so their coefficients are linked:

$$
\frac{D}{\mu} = \frac{kT}{q}
$$

With $kT/q = 25.9$ mV at 300 K: $D_n = 1350 \times 0.0259 = 35$ cm²/s and $D_p = 12.4$ cm²/s.

## Equilibrium: a balance of drift and diffusion

In equilibrium no net current flows, but a doping gradient still creates a concentration gradient and hence a diffusion current. It's exactly cancelled by a drift current in a **built-in electric field**. That field is why a p–n junction has a built-in voltage (Lesson 2.1). In band-diagram terms: in equilibrium the Fermi level is **flat** everywhere, and bands bend wherever the doping changes.

## Key takeaways

- Drift: $v_d = \mu E$, $\sigma = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$; mobility falls with doping and temperature; velocity saturates near $10^7$ cm/s.
- Diffusion: $J = qD_n\,dn/dx$ (electrons), $-qD_p\,dp/dx$ (holes).
- Einstein relation $D/\mu = kT/q$.
- In equilibrium, drift and diffusion cancel and $E_F$ is flat.
