## Joining p and n

Put p-type and n-type silicon in contact (in practice, by doping one region of a single crystal differently). Near the boundary, electrons diffuse from the n side, where they're plentiful, into the p side, and holes diffuse the other way. They recombine, leaving behind a region stripped of free carriers: the **depletion region** (or space-charge region). It contains only the fixed, ionized dopant atoms: positive donors on the n side, negative acceptors on the p side.

That charge creates an electric field pointing from n to p, which pushes carriers back against diffusion. Equilibrium is reached when the drift caused by the field exactly cancels the diffusion (Lesson 1.3), and the Fermi level is flat across the junction.

## Built-in potential

The field corresponds to a **built-in potential** $V_{bi}$ across the depletion region. Requiring the Fermi level to be flat gives

$$
V_{bi} = \frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}
$$

> **Example.** $N_A = 10^{17}$, $N_D = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³: $V_{bi} = 0.0259\ln(10^{33}/10^{20}) = 0.0259\ln(10^{13}) = 0.775$ V.

You can't measure $V_{bi}$ with a voltmeter: the contact potentials at the metal wires cancel it exactly. But it's the barrier that forward bias must overcome, which is why silicon diodes turn on near 0.6–0.7 V.

## The depletion approximation

Treat the depletion region as fully depleted with sharp edges at $-x_p$ and $x_n$. Charge neutrality requires equal and opposite charges on each side:

$$
N_Ax_p = N_Dx_n
$$

so the depletion region extends mostly into the **lightly doped** side. Solving Poisson's equation (Electromagnetics, Lesson 2.2) across it gives a triangular field and a total width

$$
W = x_n + x_p = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s V_{bi}}{q}\left(\frac{1}{N_A} + \frac{1}{N_D}\right)}, \qquad
E_{max} = \frac{qN_Dx_n}{\varepsilon_s}
$$

with $\varepsilon_s = 11.7\varepsilon_0 = 1.04\times10^{-12}$ F/cm for silicon.

> **Example, continued.** $W = 0.332\ \mu$m, of which $x_n = 0.302\ \mu$m lies in the lightly doped n side and only $x_p = 0.030\ \mu$m in the p side. The peak field is $E_{max} = 4.7\times10^4$ V/cm.

## Applying a bias

With an applied voltage $V$ (positive on the p side = forward bias), the barrier becomes $V_{bi} - V$, and the width changes accordingly:

$$
W(V) = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s(V_{bi} - V)}{q}\left(\frac{1}{N_A} + \frac{1}{N_D}\right)}
$$

Forward bias shrinks the barrier and the depletion region, letting carriers flood across (next lesson). Reverse bias widens both. With a 5 V reverse bias, the example junction's width grows from 0.33 to 0.91 µm.

## Key takeaways

- Diffusion across a p–n junction leaves a depletion region of fixed dopant charge and a built-in field.
- $V_{bi} = (kT/q)\ln(N_AN_D/n_i^2)$, about 0.7–0.9 V in silicon.
- $N_Ax_p = N_Dx_n$: depletion extends into the lightly doped side.
- $W \propto \sqrt{V_{bi} - V}$: forward bias narrows it, reverse bias widens it.
