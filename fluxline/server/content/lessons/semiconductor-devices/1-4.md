## Disturbing equilibrium

Shine light on a semiconductor, or forward-bias a junction, and you create **excess carriers** beyond the equilibrium values: $n = n_0 + \Delta n$, $p = p_0 + \Delta p$. Nature then works to restore $np = n_i^2$ by **recombination**. How long excess carriers survive, and how far they travel before recombining, controls diodes, bipolar transistors, solar cells, photodetectors and LEDs.

## Generation and recombination

- **Generation** creates electron–hole pairs: thermally, or by absorbing a photon with energy above $E_g$.
- **Recombination** destroys pairs. In direct-gap materials (GaAs, GaN) an electron can drop straight into a hole and emit a photon: **radiative** recombination, the basis of LEDs and lasers. In indirect-gap silicon, recombination happens mostly through defect states in the gap (**Shockley–Read–Hall** recombination), releasing heat.

## Minority-carrier lifetime

Under **low-level injection** (excess carriers much fewer than the majority carriers), the recombination rate is proportional to the excess **minority** carrier density. In n-type material:

$$
R = \frac{\Delta p}{\tau_p}
$$

where $\tau_p$ is the **minority-carrier lifetime**. Turn off the generation, and the excess decays exponentially:

$$
\Delta p(t) = \Delta p(0)\,e^{-t/\tau_p}
$$

Lifetimes range from milliseconds in ultra-pure silicon (used for solar cells and power devices) to nanoseconds where gold or platinum is added deliberately to make fast-switching diodes. Under steady generation rate $G$, the excess settles at $\Delta p = G\tau_p$.

> **Example.** Light generates $G = 10^{20}$ pairs/cm³·s in n-type silicon with $\tau_p = 1\ \mu$s: $\Delta p = 10^{14}$ cm⁻³. That's 10 billion times the equilibrium hole density of $10^4$ cm⁻³ (for $N_D = 10^{16}$), but still only 1% of the electron density: low-level injection. The **minority** carrier density changes enormously while the majority barely notices; that asymmetry is the key to devices.

## The continuity equation

Carriers in a small volume change because of current flowing in or out, generation and recombination. For holes in n-type material:

$$
\frac{\partial\Delta p}{\partial t} = D_p\frac{\partial^2\Delta p}{\partial x^2} - \mu_pE\frac{\partial\Delta p}{\partial x} + G - \frac{\Delta p}{\tau_p}
$$

## Diffusion length

The most important special case: steady state, no field, no generation away from the injection point. Then

$$
D_p\frac{d^2\Delta p}{dx^2} = \frac{\Delta p}{\tau_p} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta p(x) = \Delta p(0)\,e^{-x/L_p}, \qquad L_p = \sqrt{D_p\tau_p}
$$

Excess minority carriers injected at one point diffuse inward and die out over the **diffusion length** $L_p$.

> With $D_p = 12.4$ cm²/s and $\tau_p = 1\ \mu$s: $L_p = \sqrt{12.4\times10^{-6}} = 3.5\times10^{-3}$ cm $= 35\ \mu$m.

Diffusion length sets device dimensions: a bipolar transistor's base must be much thinner than $L$ so injected carriers cross it before recombining (Lesson 3.1), and a solar cell collects carriers generated within about $L$ of its junction.

## Key takeaways

- Excess minority carriers recombine with lifetime $\tau$: $\Delta p(t) = \Delta p(0)e^{-t/\tau}$; steady state $\Delta p = G\tau$.
- Low-level injection changes the minority density enormously and the majority density hardly at all.
- Steady-state diffusion gives $\Delta p(x) = \Delta p(0)e^{-x/L}$ with $L = \sqrt{D\tau}$.
