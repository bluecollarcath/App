## Two junctions, one thin base

A bipolar transistor is two p–n junctions sharing a very thin middle region. In an npn transistor in the forward-active mode:

- the **emitter–base** junction is forward biased, so the heavily doped emitter injects electrons into the base;
- the **base** is much thinner than the electron diffusion length, so almost all of those electrons diffuse across it before recombining;
- the **collector–base** junction is reverse biased, and its field sweeps the arriving electrons into the collector.

The collector current is set by the emitter–base voltage (through the law of the junction, Lesson 2.2) but hardly depends on the collector voltage. A small base current controls a large collector current: that's the transistor action used in Microelectronics I, now explained from the inside.

## Where the base current goes

The base current is the small leftover that keeps the device from being perfect. It has two parts:

1. **Holes injected back** from the base into the emitter. The forward-biased emitter junction injects both ways, and only the electron current is useful.
2. **Recombination in the base**: a few injected electrons meet holes before reaching the collector, and each one needs a replacement hole from the base contact.

Two figures of merit measure these losses.

**Emitter injection efficiency** (the fraction of emitter current that is electrons):

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{N_BD_EW_B}{N_ED_BW_E}}
$$

So the emitter must be doped **much more heavily** than the base ($N_E \gg N_B$).

**Base transport factor** (the fraction of injected electrons that survive the trip across the base):

$$
\alpha_T \approx 1 - \frac{W_B^2}{2L_n^2}
$$

So the base must be **much thinner** than the diffusion length.

The common-base current gain and the familiar $\beta$ follow:

$$
\alpha = \gamma\,\alpha_T, \qquad \beta = \frac{\alpha}{1 - \alpha}
$$

> **Example.** $W_B = 0.5\ \mu$m and $L_n = 20\ \mu$m give $\alpha_T = 1 - 0.25/800 = 0.99969$. With $\gamma = 0.995$, $\alpha = 0.9947$ and $\beta \approx 187$. Even a 99.5% emitter efficiency limits $\beta$ far more than base recombination does, which is why modern BJTs focus on the emitter, using polysilicon emitters or wide-bandgap SiGe bases (heterojunction bipolar transistors) to block the back-injection.

## Second-order effects

- **Early effect.** Raising $V_{CE}$ widens the collector depletion region, thinning the effective base, so $I_C$ rises slightly with $V_{CE}$. That's the output resistance $r_o = V_A/I_C$ (Microelectronics I). A lightly doped collector keeps most of the depletion out of the base and raises $V_A$.
- **Punch-through.** If the collector depletion reaches the emitter, the base disappears and current flows uncontrolled.
- **High-level injection and the Kirk effect.** At high currents, injected electrons rival the base doping, and the base effectively widens into the collector. $\beta$ and $f_T$ fall.
- **Speed.** The time for electrons to diffuse across the base, $\tau_B = W_B^2/2D_n$, limits $f_T \approx 1/(2\pi\tau_B)$ (plus junction charging times). Thin bases make fast transistors: SiGe HBTs with bases tens of nanometers thick reach $f_T$ above 300 GHz.

## Key takeaways

- Forward-biased emitter injects carriers across a thin base into a reverse-biased collector.
- $\alpha = \gamma\alpha_T$, $\beta = \alpha/(1 - \alpha)$; heavy emitter doping raises $\gamma$, a thin base raises $\alpha_T$.
- Early effect, punch-through and the Kirk effect limit real devices; base transit time limits $f_T$.
