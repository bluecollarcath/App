## How many carriers are there?

A semiconductor's usefulness comes from being able to set its number of free electrons and holes over many orders of magnitude. This lesson answers how many carriers there are at a given temperature and doping, and where the **Fermi level** sits.

## The Fermi–Dirac distribution

Electrons are fermions: no two can occupy the same state. At temperature $T$, the probability that a state at energy $E$ is occupied is

$$
f(E) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(E - E_F)/kT}}
$$

where $E_F$ is the **Fermi level**, the energy at which a state would be exactly half full. At room temperature $kT = 0.0259$ eV. A few $kT$ above $E_F$, states are almost empty, and $f(E) \approx e^{-(E - E_F)/kT}$ (the Boltzmann approximation); a few $kT$ below, almost full.

Multiplying the occupancy by the density of available states in each band and integrating gives the carrier concentrations (in the Boltzmann approximation):

$$
n = N_c\,e^{-(E_c - E_F)/kT}, \qquad p = N_v\,e^{-(E_F - E_v)/kT}
$$

where $N_c$ and $N_v$ are the **effective densities of states**: $2.8\times10^{19}$ and $1.04\times10^{19}$ cm⁻³ in silicon at 300 K.

## Intrinsic silicon and the mass-action law

In pure (**intrinsic**) silicon, every electron excited into the conduction band leaves a hole behind, so $n = p = n_i$. Multiplying the two expressions above, $E_F$ cancels:

$$
np = n_i^2 = N_cN_v\,e^{-E_g/kT}
$$

This **mass-action law** holds in equilibrium **whatever the doping**. For silicon at 300 K, $n_i \approx 10^{10}$ cm⁻³: one free electron per $5\times10^{12}$ atoms. Because of the exponential, $n_i$ roughly doubles every 8 °C near room temperature, which is why semiconductor devices leak much more when hot. Wide-bandgap materials (SiC, GaN) have tiny $n_i$ and keep working at temperatures where silicon fails.

## Doping

Replace a few silicon atoms with impurities from the neighboring columns of the periodic table:

- **Donors** (group V: phosphorus, arsenic) have a fifth valence electron, bound so weakly (about 0.045 eV) that at room temperature essentially every donor gives it to the conduction band: **n-type**, with $n \approx N_D$.
- **Acceptors** (group III: boron) have one electron too few; they grab one from the valence band, creating a hole: **p-type**, with $p \approx N_A$.

The **majority** carrier concentration is set by the doping; the **minority** concentration then follows from the mass-action law.

> **Example.** Silicon doped with $N_D = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³ (about one donor per 5 million atoms):
>
> $n = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³, $p = n_i^2/n = 10^{20}/10^{16} = 10^4$ cm⁻³. One part per million of doping raised the electron density a millionfold and cut the hole density a millionfold.
>
> The Fermi level moves up toward the conduction band: $E_F - E_i = kT\ln(n/n_i) = 0.0259\ln(10^6) = 0.358$ eV above the intrinsic level, or $E_c - E_F = kT\ln(N_c/n) = 0.21$ eV.

In general,

$$
E_F - E_i = kT\ln\frac{n}{n_i} \quad\text{(n-type)}, \qquad E_i - E_F = kT\ln\frac{p}{n_i} \quad\text{(p-type)}
$$

When both donors and acceptors are present, they **compensate**: the net doping $N_D - N_A$ (or $N_A - N_D$) sets the type. That's how integrated circuits build n-regions inside p-regions: by counter-doping.

At very high doping (above about $10^{18}$ cm⁻³), the Fermi level enters the band, the Boltzmann approximation fails, and the material is called **degenerate**: it behaves almost like a metal. Degenerate layers are used for ohmic contacts (Lesson 2.3) and tunnel junctions.

## Key takeaways

- Fermi–Dirac occupancy $f(E) = 1/(1 + e^{(E - E_F)/kT})$; $kT = 0.0259$ eV at 300 K.
- $np = n_i^2$ in equilibrium; for Si at 300 K, $n_i \approx 10^{10}$ cm⁻³, rising steeply with temperature.
- Donors make n-type ($n \approx N_D$), acceptors p-type ($p \approx N_A$); minority = $n_i^2$/majority.
- $E_F - E_i = kT\ln(n/n_i)$.
