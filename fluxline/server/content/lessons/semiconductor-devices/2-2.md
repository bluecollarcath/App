## Why current flows one way

Forward bias lowers the junction barrier from $V_{bi}$ to $V_{bi} - V$. The number of carriers energetic enough to cross depends **exponentially** on the barrier height (Boltzmann statistics), so lowering it by $V$ raises the minority-carrier density at each edge of the depletion region by $e^{V/V_T}$, where $V_T = kT/q$:

$$
p_n(x_n) = p_{n0}\,e^{V/V_T}, \qquad n_p(-x_p) = n_{p0}\,e^{V/V_T}
$$

This is the **law of the junction**: forward bias **injects** minority carriers into each side.

## From injection to current

The injected minority carriers diffuse away from the junction and recombine over a diffusion length (Lesson 1.4): $\Delta p_n(x) = p_{n0}(e^{V/V_T} - 1)e^{-x/L_p}$. The diffusion current at the edge is $qD_p\,d\Delta p/dx$. Adding the electron current injected into the p side gives the **ideal diode (Shockley) equation**:

$$
I = I_0\left(e^{V/V_T} - 1\right), \qquad
I_0 = qAn_i^2\left(\frac{D_p}{L_pN_D} + \frac{D_n}{L_nN_A}\right)
$$

Two important insights hide in $I_0$:

- It's proportional to $n_i^2$, so it rises very steeply with temperature and is tiny in wide-bandgap materials.
- Each term is inversely proportional to its side's doping, so the current is carried mostly by carriers injected **into the lightly doped side**. A $p^+$–$n$ diode's current is almost all holes.

> **Example.** $N_A = 10^{17}$, $N_D = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³, $\tau = 1\ \mu$s on both sides, minority mobilities 800 (electrons in the p side) and 450 (holes in the n side): $D_n = 20.7$, $D_p = 11.7$ cm²/s, $L_n = 45\ \mu$m, $L_p = 34\ \mu$m.
>
> $J_0 = 1.6\times10^{-19}(10^{20})\left(\dfrac{11.7}{3.4\times10^{-3} \cdot 10^{16}} + \dfrac{20.7}{4.6\times10^{-3} \cdot 10^{17}}\right) = 6.2\times10^{-12}$ A/cm²
>
> 88% of it is hole current into the lightly doped n side. For a $100 \times 100\ \mu$m diode ($A = 10^{-4}$ cm²), $I_0 = 6.2\times10^{-16}$ A, and at 0.7 V forward bias, $I = 0.34$ mA.

## Forward and reverse

- **Forward:** current rises by a factor of $e$ every 25.9 mV, or tenfold every **60 mV**. That's why a silicon diode's "turn-on" voltage looks like a fixed 0.6–0.7 V on a linear plot: a tenth of a volt more means about 50 times more current.
- **Reverse:** $e^{V/V_T} \to 0$ and $I \to -I_0$, a tiny saturation current, femtoamps to nanoamps, until breakdown (next lesson).

## Real diodes

Measured diodes depart from the ideal equation in predictable ways, captured by an **ideality factor** $n$ in $e^{V/nV_T}$:

- At low forward bias, recombination **inside** the depletion region adds current with $n \approx 2$.
- At high current, **high-level injection** ($n \approx 2$) and the series resistance of the neutral regions flatten the curve.
- In reverse, generation in the depletion region adds leakage that grows with $W$.

Temperature matters too: at constant current, the forward voltage falls by about **2 mV/°C**. That's used in on-chip temperature sensors and bandgap references, and it's the cause of thermal runaway in paralleled bipolar devices.

## Key takeaways

- Law of the junction: forward bias multiplies the minority density at each depletion edge by $e^{V/V_T}$.
- $I = I_0(e^{V/V_T} - 1)$ with $I_0 = qAn_i^2(D_p/L_pN_D + D_n/L_nN_A)$; the lightly doped side dominates.
- 60 mV per decade of current; forward voltage drops about 2 mV/°C.
- Real diodes: ideality factor 1–2, series resistance, generation leakage.
