## The heart of every MOSFET

Stack a metal (or heavily doped polysilicon) gate, a thin insulating oxide and a semiconductor substrate, and you have a **MOS capacitor**. It's the MOSFET without its source and drain, and its behavior under gate voltage explains how a channel forms and what sets the threshold voltage.

Take a p-type substrate with acceptor density $N_A$. Three regimes appear as the gate voltage $V_G$ rises:

1. **Accumulation** ($V_G$ below the flat-band voltage): the negative gate attracts extra holes to the surface. The structure is a simple parallel-plate capacitor of the oxide, $C_{ox} = \varepsilon_{ox}/t_{ox}$ per unit area.
2. **Depletion** (between flat band and threshold): the positive gate pushes holes away, exposing negative acceptor ions in a depletion layer. Its capacitance appears in series with the oxide, so the total capacitance drops.
3. **Inversion** ($V_G$ above threshold): the surface is bent so far that electrons, the minority carriers, gather at the surface in a thin conducting **inversion layer**: the channel. Further gate voltage adds electrons to the channel instead of widening the depletion layer.

## Threshold voltage

Strong inversion is defined as the point where the surface is as n-type as the bulk is p-type: the surface potential has bent by $2\phi_F$, with $\phi_F = (kT/q)\ln(N_A/n_i)$. The gate voltage needed is

$$
V_T = V_{FB} + 2\phi_F + \frac{\sqrt{2\varepsilon_sqN_A(2\phi_F)}}{C_{ox}}
$$

- $V_{FB}$ (flat-band voltage) accounts for the work-function difference between gate and substrate, plus any trapped oxide charge.
- $2\phi_F$ is the band bending needed to invert the surface.
- The last term is the voltage needed across the oxide to hold the depletion charge.

> **Example.** $N_A = 3\times10^{17}$ cm⁻³, $t_{ox} = 5$ nm, n+ polysilicon gate.
>
> $\phi_F = 0.0259\ln(3\times10^7) = 0.446$ V, so $2\phi_F = 0.892$ V.
>
> $C_{ox} = 3.9 \times 8.854\times10^{-14}/5\times10^{-7} = 6.91\times10^{-7}$ F/cm².
>
> Depletion charge: $\sqrt{2(1.04\times10^{-12})(1.6\times10^{-19})(3\times10^{17})(0.892)} = 2.98\times10^{-7}$ C/cm², which needs $0.431$ V across the oxide.
>
> $V_{FB} \approx -(0.56 + \phi_F) = -1.01$ V for an n+ poly gate.
>
> $V_T = -1.01 + 0.89 + 0.43 = 0.32$ V.

Designers set $V_T$ by adjusting the substrate doping (with a shallow implant) and the gate material. Higher doping or thicker oxide raises $V_T$.

## The C–V curve

Measuring capacitance vs. gate voltage is the standard way to characterize a MOS process: it reveals oxide thickness, doping, flat-band voltage and interface quality.

```plot
x: -3, 3, V_G (V)
y: 0, 1.1, C / C_ox
f: 0.25 + 0.75*(1 - tanh(2.5*(x + 0.6)))/2 + 0.75*(1 + tanh(3*(x - 0.6)))/2 | low frequency
f: 0.25 + 0.75*(1 - tanh(2.5*(x + 0.6)))/2 | high frequency | dash
caption: Typical C–V curves of a MOS capacitor on p-type silicon (schematic). Capacitance is $C_{ox}$ in accumulation, falls in depletion, and in inversion recovers to $C_{ox}$ only at low frequency, when the inversion charge can follow the signal.
```

In inversion at high frequency, minority carriers can't be generated fast enough to follow the signal, so the capacitance stays at its minimum. In a MOSFET, the source and drain supply the channel electrons instantly, so a transistor's gate capacitance returns to $C_{ox}$.

## Key takeaways

- A MOS capacitor goes through accumulation, depletion and inversion as $V_G$ rises.
- $V_T = V_{FB} + 2\phi_F + \sqrt{2\varepsilon_sqN_A(2\phi_F)}/C_{ox}$; doping and gate material set it.
- $C_{ox} = \varepsilon_{ox}/t_{ox}$; C–V measurements characterize the oxide and doping.
