## Elimination, recorded

Gaussian elimination (Lesson 1.1) turns $A$ into an upper-triangular $U$ by subtracting multiples of rows. If you **record the multipliers** in a lower-triangular matrix $L$ (with ones on its diagonal), you get the factorization

$$
A = LU
$$

> $$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
> The multiplier 2 (row 2 minus 2 × row 1) is stored in $L$.

## Why factor?

Solving $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ with $A = LU$ splits into two triangular solves:

1. **Forward substitution:** $L\mathbf y = \mathbf b$ (top to bottom).
2. **Back substitution:** $U\mathbf x = \mathbf y$ (bottom to top).

Each triangular solve costs only about $n^2$ operations, while the factorization costs about $\tfrac23n^3$. So when the **same matrix** must be solved with many right-hand sides (a circuit driven by different sources, each time step of a transient simulation with a fixed step, sensitivity analyses), factor once and reuse $L$ and $U$.

> For $n = 1000$: factoring costs about $6.7\times10^8$ operations, but each extra solve only $2\times10^6$, over 300 times cheaper.

## Pivoting

Elimination divides by the pivot. A zero pivot stops it; a **tiny** pivot magnifies rounding errors enormously. **Partial pivoting** swaps rows to bring the largest available entry into the pivot position, giving $PA = LU$ with a permutation matrix $P$. Every serious solver does this.

> $\begin{bmatrix} 10^{-20} & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$: without pivoting, the multiplier $10^{20}$ swamps everything in floating point and the computed answer is badly wrong. Swapping rows first gives the right answer.

## Conditioning: when the problem itself is sensitive

Even perfect algorithms can't help an **ill-conditioned** matrix, one that's nearly singular. The **condition number** $\kappa(A)$ measures how much relative errors in the data are amplified in the solution:

$$
\frac{\|\delta\mathbf x\|}{\|\mathbf x\|} \le \kappa(A)\,\frac{\|\delta\mathbf b\|}{\|\mathbf b\|}
$$

A rule of thumb: you lose about $\log_{10}\kappa$ significant digits.

> $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.0001 \end{bmatrix}$ has $\kappa \approx 40\,000$: about 4.6 of double precision's 16 digits are lost. Physically, its two equations are nearly the same measurement.

Ill-conditioning in circuits comes from wildly different element values (a 1 mΩ resistor next to a 1 GΩ one) or near-floating nodes; SPICE's `gmin` adds tiny conductances to every node partly to keep the matrix well conditioned.

## How SPICE solves circuits

Circuit simulators build the **modified nodal analysis** matrix, which is large (up to millions of unknowns) but **sparse** (each node touches only a few others). They use sparse LU factorization, choosing the pivot order to keep $L$ and $U$ sparse (minimizing "fill-in"), and re-solve at every Newton iteration and time step. Solving a circuit with a million nodes takes seconds rather than the years dense elimination would need.

## Key takeaways

- $A = LU$ records elimination; solve by forward then back substitution ($\sim n^2$ each after a $\tfrac23n^3$ factorization).
- Partial pivoting ($PA = LU$) avoids tiny pivots; it's always used.
- Condition number $\kappa$: about $\log_{10}\kappa$ digits are lost; it's a property of the problem.
- SPICE uses sparse LU on the MNA matrix at every iteration.
