## Why linear systems are everywhere in EE

Write nodal equations for a circuit with 1,000 nodes and you have 999 simultaneous linear equations. Power-flow studies, finite-element field solvers, control systems, and machine-learning hardware all reduce to the same problem:

$$
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}
$$

This lesson develops the systematic method for solving it.

## From equations to an augmented matrix

The system

$$
\begin{aligned}
x + y + z &= 6\\
2y + 5z &= -4\\
2x + 5y - z &= 27
\end{aligned}
$$

is written as the **augmented matrix**

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c}
1 & 1 & 1 & 6\\
0 & 2 & 5 & -4\\
2 & 5 & -1 & 27
\end{array}\right]
$$

## Elementary row operations

Three operations never change the solution set:

1. Swap two rows.
2. Multiply a row by a nonzero constant.
3. Add a multiple of one row to another.

## Gaussian elimination

Use row operations to reach **row echelon form** (zeros below each pivot), then back-substitute.

> **Step 1.** $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 2R_1$:
> $$\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&1&1&6\\ 0&2&5&-4\\ 0&3&-3&15 \end{array}\right]$$
> **Step 2.** $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - \tfrac32 R_2$:
> $$\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1&1&1&6\\ 0&2&5&-4\\ 0&0&-10.5&21 \end{array}\right]$$
> **Back-substitute:** $z = 21/(-10.5) = -2$. Then $2y + 5(-2) = -4 \Rightarrow y = 3$. Then $x + 3 - 2 = 6 \Rightarrow x = 5$.

```plot

y: -2, 5, y
f: 5 - 2*x | 2x + y = 5
f: x - 1 | x − y = 1
mark: 2, 1, (2, 1)
caption: Each equation in a two-variable system is a line. The unique solution is where they cross.
```

## Three possible outcomes

| Echelon form shows… | Solutions |
|---|---|
| a pivot in every variable column | exactly one |
| a row $[0\ 0\ \cdots\ 0 \mid c]$ with $c \ne 0$ | none (inconsistent) |
| fewer pivots than variables, consistent | infinitely many (free variables) |

A well-posed circuit always has exactly one solution. If your nodal matrix is **singular**, you have usually made a modeling error — for example, a floating node with no path to ground, or a loop of ideal voltage sources.

## Cost

Gaussian elimination on an $n \times n$ system takes about $\tfrac{2}{3}n^3$ floating-point operations. Real circuit matrices are **sparse** (each node touches only a few others), and sparse solvers exploit this to handle millions of unknowns.

## Key takeaways

- Circuits, fields, and control problems all reduce to $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$.
- Gaussian elimination + back-substitution solves any square nonsingular system.
- A singular circuit matrix usually signals a modeling error.
