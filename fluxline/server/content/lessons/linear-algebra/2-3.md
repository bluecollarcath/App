## Lengths, angles and perpendicularity

The **inner (dot) product** turns a vector space into geometry:

$$
\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = \mathbf a^T\mathbf b = \sum a_ib_i, \qquad \|\mathbf a\| = \sqrt{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a}, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\|\mathbf a\|\,\|\mathbf b\|}
$$

Vectors are **orthogonal** when $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = 0$.

> $(1, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$: $\cos\theta = 1/\sqrt2$, so $\theta = 45°$.

The same idea extends to signals: the inner product of two functions over a period is $\int f(t)g(t)\,dt$. Sines and cosines of different harmonic frequencies are **orthogonal** in this sense, which is exactly why Fourier coefficients can be computed one at a time (Signals & Systems, Lesson 2.1), and why OFDM subcarriers don't interfere (Communication Systems, Lesson 3.3).

## Projection onto a line

The closest point to $\mathbf b$ on the line through $\mathbf a$ is the **projection**

$$
\mathbf p = \frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a}\,\mathbf a
$$

and the error $\mathbf e = \mathbf b - \mathbf p$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf a$.

> Project $\mathbf b = (3, 4)$ onto $\mathbf a = (1, 1)$: $\mathbf p = \tfrac72(1, 1) = (3.5, 3.5)$, and $\mathbf e = (-0.5, 0.5)$, which is indeed perpendicular to $(1, 1)$.

## Projection onto a subspace

To project onto the column space of a matrix $A$ (with independent columns), require the error to be perpendicular to every column: $A^T(\mathbf b - A\hat{\mathbf x}) = \mathbf 0$. That gives

$$
A^TA\,\hat{\mathbf x} = A^T\mathbf b, \qquad \mathbf p = A\hat{\mathbf x} = \underbrace{A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T}_{P}\,\mathbf b
$$

The **projection matrix** $P$ satisfies $P^2 = P$ (projecting twice changes nothing) and $P^T = P$. This is the engine of least squares (next lesson).

## Orthonormal bases and Gram–Schmidt

Computations are easiest in an **orthonormal** basis (mutually perpendicular unit vectors $\mathbf q_i$): coordinates are just dot products, $c_i = \mathbf q_i\cdot\mathbf b$, with no system to solve. A matrix $Q$ with orthonormal columns satisfies $Q^TQ = I$, and an orthogonal transform preserves lengths and angles: rotations and reflections.

**Gram–Schmidt** builds an orthonormal basis from any independent set: take each vector, subtract its projections onto the previous $\mathbf q$'s, and normalize.

> From $\mathbf a_1 = (1, 1, 0)$ and $\mathbf a_2 = (1, 0, 1)$: $\mathbf q_1 = (1, 1, 0)/\sqrt2$. Then $\mathbf a_2 - (\mathbf q_1\cdot\mathbf a_2)\mathbf q_1 = (1, 0, 1) - \tfrac12(1, 1, 0) = (\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 1)$, normalized to $\mathbf q_2 = (1, -1, 2)/\sqrt6$.

Recording the steps gives the factorization $A = QR$ ($R$ upper triangular), the numerically stable way to solve least-squares problems.

## Key takeaways

- $\cos\theta = \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b/(\|\mathbf a\|\|\mathbf b\|)$; orthogonal means zero dot product (also for functions).
- Projection onto a line: $(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b/\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a)\mathbf a$; onto a column space: $P = A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$.
- Orthonormal bases make coordinates simple dot products; Gram–Schmidt builds them ($A = QR$).
