## Changing to the eigenvector basis

If an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors, put them as the columns of $V$ and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of $\Lambda$. Then $AV = V\Lambda$, so

$$
A = V\Lambda V^{-1}
$$

This is **diagonalization**. In the eigenvector basis, $A$ is just a diagonal matrix: each coordinate is scaled independently, with no coupling. Every symmetric matrix can be diagonalized (with orthogonal $V$: $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$); a matrix with repeated eigenvalues sometimes can't.

> $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3\end{bmatrix}$ (Lesson 3.2): $V = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2\end{bmatrix}$, $\Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}$.

## Powers made easy

$$
A^k = V\Lambda^kV^{-1}
$$

and $\Lambda^k$ just raises each eigenvalue to the $k$th power. For large $k$, the term with the **largest** $|\lambda|$ dominates. That's how one analyzes repeated processes: a discrete-time system $\mathbf x[k] = A^k\mathbf x[0]$, a Markov chain's long-run behavior, or the growth of the Fibonacci numbers (dominated by the golden ratio, $1.618\ldots$, the largest eigenvalue of $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$).

## The matrix exponential

The solution of the scalar equation $\dot x = ax$ is $x = e^{at}x_0$. For a **system** $\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x$, the solution is

$$
\mathbf x(t) = e^{At}\mathbf x_0, \qquad e^{At} = I + At + \frac{(At)^2}{2!} + \cdots
$$

defined by the same Taylor series as the scalar exponential (Calculus, Lesson 3.2). Diagonalization makes it computable:

$$
e^{At} = Ve^{\Lambda t}V^{-1}, \qquad e^{\Lambda t} = \operatorname{diag}\big(e^{\lambda_1t}, \ldots, e^{\lambda_nt}\big)
$$

The solution is a sum of **modes**: each eigenvector, times $e^{\lambda_it}$, weighted by how much of that eigenvector the initial state contains.

> **Example.** $\dot{\mathbf x} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf x$ (the state-space form of $\ddot y + 3\dot y + 2y = 0$), with $\mathbf x(0) = (1, 0)$.
>
> Eigenvalues: $\lambda^2 + 3\lambda + 2 = 0$, so $\lambda = -1, -2$, with eigenvectors $(1, -1)$ and $(1, -2)$.
>
> Expand the initial state: $(1, 0) = 2(1, -1) - (1, -2)$. So
> $$\mathbf x(t) = 2e^{-t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1\end{bmatrix} - e^{-2t}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2\end{bmatrix}, \qquad x_1(t) = 2e^{-t} - e^{-2t}$$
> Check: $x_1(0) = 1$ and $\dot x_1(0) = -2 + 2 = 0$. ✓ Both modes decay; the slow $e^{-t}$ mode dominates after a fraction of a second.

With an input, $\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x + B\mathbf u$, the solution adds a convolution: $\mathbf x(t) = e^{At}\mathbf x_0 + \int_0^te^{A(t-\tau)}B\mathbf u(\tau)\,d\tau$. Sampling it at intervals $T$ gives the discrete model $\Phi = e^{AT}$ used in digital control (Control Systems, Lesson 4.2).

## Key takeaways

- Diagonalization $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$ decouples a system into independent modes.
- $A^k = V\Lambda^kV^{-1}$: the largest $|\lambda|$ dominates repeated processes.
- $\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x$ has solution $e^{At}\mathbf x_0 = \sum c_ie^{\lambda_it}\mathbf v_i$: a sum of modes.
- $e^{AT}$ discretizes continuous systems for digital control.
