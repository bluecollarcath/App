## Matrices as objects

Lesson 1.1 used matrices as bookkeeping for equations. Treating them as objects in their own right, with their own arithmetic, makes linear systems compact to write and fast to compute: $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ for a circuit with a thousand nodes is one line.

## Multiplication

For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ and an $n \times p$ matrix $B$, the product $AB$ is $m \times p$, with entries

$$
(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}b_{kj}
$$

the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$. The inner dimensions must match.

> $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$
> Multiplying on the right by this permutation matrix swapped the columns. In the other order, $BA$ swaps the **rows**: $AB \ne BA$ in general.

Two ways to see $A\mathbf x$, both useful:

- **row view:** each entry is a row of $A$ dotted with $\mathbf x$ (one equation each);
- **column view:** $A\mathbf x$ is a **combination of the columns** of $A$, weighted by the entries of $\mathbf x$. That view drives the rest of the course.

Multiplying two $n \times n$ matrices takes $n^3$ multiplications, which is why large simulations exploit sparsity and structure.

## Identity, inverse and transpose

- The **identity** $I$ has ones on the diagonal: $AI = IA = A$.
- The **inverse** $A^{-1}$, if it exists, satisfies $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I$, and solves $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ as $\mathbf x = A^{-1}\mathbf b$. For a 2 × 2 matrix:
$$\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$$
A matrix is invertible exactly when its determinant $ad - bc$ (Lesson 3.1) is nonzero.
- The **transpose** $A^T$ swaps rows and columns.

> $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{-2}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1.5 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix}$

Rules that trip people up: $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$ and $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ (the order reverses).

**In practice, never compute an inverse to solve a system.** Elimination (or LU, next lesson) is faster and more accurate; the inverse is a notation.

## Matrices in circuits

Nodal analysis gives $G\mathbf v = \mathbf i$, with the **conductance matrix** $G$ built by inspection: each diagonal entry is the sum of conductances at a node, and each off-diagonal entry is minus the conductance between two nodes. $G$ is **symmetric** ($G = G^T$) for circuits of resistors, which numerical solvers exploit.

> Node 1 connects to ground through 2 Ω and to node 2 through 4 Ω; node 2 connects to ground through 8 Ω; a 3 A source feeds node 1:
> $$\begin{bmatrix} 0.75 & -0.25 \\ -0.25 & 0.375 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \ \Rightarrow\ v_1 = 5.14\ \text{V},\ v_2 = 3.43\ \text{V}$$

## Block matrices

Matrices can be partitioned into blocks that multiply like entries (when the sizes fit). State-space models, $\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf x} \\ \mathbf y\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf x \\ \mathbf u\end{bmatrix}$, and modified nodal analysis in SPICE are written this way.

## Key takeaways

- $(AB)_{ij}$ = row $i$ · column $j$; $A\mathbf x$ is a combination of $A$'s columns; $AB \ne BA$ in general.
- 2 × 2 inverse: swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal, divide by $ad - bc$.
- $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$, $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$; solve systems by elimination, not inverses.
- Nodal analysis gives a symmetric conductance matrix $G\mathbf v = \mathbf i$.
