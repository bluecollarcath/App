## Spaces of vectors

A **vector space** is a set of objects you can add and scale, with the results staying in the set. $\mathbb R^n$ is the familiar example, but so are polynomials, sinusoids of a fixed frequency (phasors) and solutions of linear differential equations. Linear algebra's power is that the same ideas apply to all of them.

A **subspace** is a subset that is itself a vector space: it contains the zero vector and is closed under addition and scaling. In $\mathbb R^3$, the subspaces are the origin, lines through the origin, planes through the origin, and $\mathbb R^3$ itself. A plane **not** through the origin is not a subspace.

## Span

The **span** of a set of vectors is everything you can build from them as linear combinations $c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2 + \cdots$. Two non-parallel vectors in $\mathbb R^3$ span a plane; a third vector outside that plane extends the span to all of $\mathbb R^3$.

The question "is $\mathbf b$ in the span of the columns of $A$?" is exactly "does $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ have a solution?" (the column view of Lesson 1.2).

## Linear independence

Vectors are **linearly independent** if none of them is a combination of the others. Equivalently, the only way to get zero is the trivial combination:

$$
c_1\mathbf v_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf v_k = \mathbf 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad c_1 = \cdots = c_k = 0
$$

To test it, put the vectors as columns of a matrix and eliminate: they're independent if every column gets a pivot (for $n$ vectors in $\mathbb R^n$: if the determinant is nonzero).

> $(1, 2, 3)$, $(0, 1, 1)$ and $(1, 3, 4)$: the third is the sum of the first two, so they're **dependent**, and they span only a plane.

More than $n$ vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ are always dependent.

## Basis and dimension

A **basis** is an independent set that spans the space: the minimum set of "building blocks." Every vector has **unique** coordinates in a given basis. All bases of a space have the same number of vectors: its **dimension**.

- The standard basis of $\mathbb R^3$ is $\hat{\mathbf x}, \hat{\mathbf y}, \hat{\mathbf z}$, but any three independent vectors work.
- Polynomials of degree ≤ 2 have basis $1, t, t^2$: dimension 3.
- Sinusoids $A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ at a fixed $\omega$ form a 2-dimensional space with basis $\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t$, which is why a phasor needs exactly two numbers.

Choosing a good basis is half of engineering mathematics: Fourier analysis expresses signals in a basis of sinusoids; eigenvectors (Unit 3) give the basis in which a system decouples.

## Key takeaways

- Subspaces contain zero and are closed under addition and scaling (lines and planes through the origin).
- Span = all linear combinations; $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ is solvable iff $\mathbf b$ is in the span of $A$'s columns.
- Independent: no vector is a combination of the others; test with elimination.
- A basis is an independent spanning set; its size is the dimension.
