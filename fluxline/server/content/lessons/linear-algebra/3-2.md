## Directions a matrix doesn't turn

Most vectors change direction when multiplied by a matrix. Special ones, the **eigenvectors**, only get scaled:

$$
A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v
$$

The scale factor $\lambda$ is the **eigenvalue**. Along an eigenvector, a complicated matrix acts like a plain number. That's why eigenvalues describe the natural behavior of systems: the modes of a vibrating structure, the poles of a circuit, the growth or decay of each pattern in a dynamic system.

## Finding them

$A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v$ means $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$ has a nonzero solution, so $A - \lambda I$ must be singular:

$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = 0
$$

This **characteristic polynomial** has degree $n$, so an $n \times n$ matrix has $n$ eigenvalues (counting repeats, possibly complex). For each $\lambda$, solve $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$ for the eigenvector(s).

> $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$: $\det\begin{bmatrix} 4 - \lambda & 1 \\ 2 & 3 - \lambda\end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2)$.
>
> $\lambda = 5$: $\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$ gives $\mathbf v = (1, 1)$.
>
> $\lambda = 2$: $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$ gives $\mathbf v = (1, -2)$.

Two quick checks: the eigenvalues **sum to the trace** (sum of the diagonal, $4 + 3 = 7$) and **multiply to the determinant** ($12 - 2 = 10$).

## Useful facts

- A triangular matrix's eigenvalues are its diagonal entries.
- A **symmetric** matrix ($A = A^T$) has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. Conductance matrices, covariance matrices and stiffness matrices are symmetric.
- Real matrices can have complex eigenvalues, in conjugate pairs: $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -4 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ has $\lambda = \pm j2$, a rotation-like behavior that means **oscillation**.
- $A^k$ has eigenvalues $\lambda^k$ with the same eigenvectors; $A^{-1}$ has $1/\lambda$.

## Eigenvalues and stability

For a linear system $\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x$ (state space, Control Systems Lesson 1.2), each eigenvector is a **mode** that evolves as $e^{\lambda t}$:

- $\text{Re}(\lambda) < 0$ for all eigenvalues: every mode decays: **stable**;
- any $\text{Re}(\lambda) > 0$: that mode grows: **unstable**;
- complex $\lambda = \sigma \pm j\omega$: oscillation at $\omega$ with envelope $e^{\sigma t}$.

The eigenvalues of the state matrix are exactly the **poles** of the system's transfer function. For a discrete-time system $\mathbf x[k+1] = A\mathbf x[k]$, stability needs $|\lambda| < 1$ instead.

> A series RLC circuit with state matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1/LC & -R/L\end{bmatrix}$ has characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2 + (R/L)\lambda + 1/LC$, the familiar second-order denominator.

Google's original PageRank, the principal components of a data set, a bridge's resonant frequencies and a molecule's energy levels are all eigenvalue problems.

## Key takeaways

- $A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v$; find $\lambda$ from $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$, then $\mathbf v$ from the null space.
- Sum of eigenvalues = trace; product = determinant; symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors.
- For $\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x$: modes $e^{\lambda t}$; stable iff all $\text{Re}\,\lambda < 0$; eigenvalues = poles.
