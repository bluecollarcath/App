## A grid built for spinning machines

For a century, the grid's behavior was set by large synchronous generators: their spinning mass supplied **inertia**, their governors corrected frequency, and their internal voltages held the network up during faults. Solar panels, wind turbines (modern ones) and batteries connect through **inverters** (Power Electronics, Lesson 2.2) instead. These **inverter-based resources** (IBRs) behave in fundamentally different ways, and as they supply a growing share of energy, grid engineering is being rewritten.

## Frequency and inertia

Grid frequency is a real-time measure of the balance between generation and load. Lose a large generator, and the shortfall is initially supplied from the kinetic energy of every rotating machine, so frequency falls at a **rate of change of frequency** (ROCOF) set by the system's total inertia:

$$
\text{ROCOF} = \frac{df}{dt} = \frac{\Delta P\,f_0}{2HS}
$$

($H$ in seconds on system rating $S$.)

> Losing 1000 MW in a 50,000 MVA system with $H = 4$ s: $\text{ROCOF} = 1000(60)/(2 \times 4 \times 50\,000) = 0.15$ Hz/s.

Inverter-based plants have no inherent rotating mass, so as they displace synchronous machines, inertia falls and frequency moves faster after disturbances, leaving less time for controls to act before under-frequency load shedding (around 59.3 Hz in North America) begins.

## Frequency control in layers

1. **Inertial response** (instant): stored kinetic energy, or **synthetic inertia** and fast frequency response from inverters and batteries, which can inject power within 100–500 ms.
2. **Primary control** (seconds): governors respond in proportion to the frequency error with **droop** $R$: $\Delta P/P_{rated} = -(\Delta f/f_0)/R$. A 5% droop means a 5% frequency drop would take a unit from zero to full output. All units on droop share the change in proportion to their ratings.
3. **Secondary control** (minutes): automatic generation control restores frequency to exactly 60 Hz and scheduled interchange between regions.

> A 300 MW unit with 4% droop sees frequency fall 0.2 Hz: $\Delta P = (0.2/60)/0.04 \times 300 = 25$ MW more output.

## Grid-following and grid-forming

- **Grid-following** inverters (nearly all installed today) measure the grid's voltage angle with a phase-locked loop and inject a controlled current in step with it. They need a strong grid to follow; on a **weak grid** (low short-circuit MVA, Lesson 3.2) their PLLs can interact and oscillate.
- **Grid-forming** inverters behave like a voltage source behind an impedance, setting their own voltage and frequency much like a synchronous machine (often emulating its swing equation). They can provide inertia, operate in islands, and even restart a dead grid. Large batteries in Australia, Hawaii and the UK already run this way.

## Faults and protection

Inverters must limit current to protect their semiconductors, typically to 1.1–1.5 times rating, while a synchronous generator delivers 5–10 times rating into a fault. Protection schemes designed around large fault currents may not detect faults fed mainly by IBRs. **Ride-through** rules (IEEE 1547, IEEE 2800) require inverters to stay connected and support voltage during faults rather than tripping, because simultaneous tripping of many plants during one fault has caused real events (Blue Cut fire, California, 2016: 1200 MW of solar briefly lost).

## Variability

Solar and wind outputs vary with weather and time of day, and their **capacity factors** (average output ÷ rating) are around 20–35%, versus 90%+ for nuclear. The midday solar surge and evening ramp produce California's famous "duck curve." Managing it takes forecasting, flexible generation, transmission to share resources across regions, demand response, and storage, now dominated by lithium-ion batteries, which also provide fast frequency response.

## Key takeaways

- $\text{ROCOF} = \Delta Pf_0/(2HS)$: less inertia means faster frequency changes.
- Frequency control: inertial (instant), primary droop (seconds), secondary AGC (minutes).
- Grid-following inverters track the grid; grid-forming inverters set voltage and frequency and can provide inertia.
- IBRs limit fault current, need ride-through, and bring variability managed by forecasting, transmission and storage.
