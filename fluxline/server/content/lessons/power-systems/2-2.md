## How much detail does a line model need?

All lines are distributed (Electromagnetics, Lesson 1.1), but at 60 Hz a simpler lumped model is usually enough. The choice depends on length:

| Line length | Model |
|---|---|
| Short, under ~80 km | series impedance $Z = (r + j\omega L)\ell$ only |
| Medium, ~80–250 km | **nominal π**: $Z$ in series, $Y/2$ at each end |
| Long, over ~250 km | exact distributed model (or equivalent π) |

## Two-port (ABCD) description

Any of these models relates sending-end and receiving-end quantities by a two-port matrix:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} V_S \\ I_S \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} V_R \\ I_R \end{bmatrix}
$$

| Model | $A = D$ | $B$ | $C$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short | 1 | $Z$ | 0 |
| Nominal π | $1 + \dfrac{YZ}{2}$ | $Z$ | $Y\left(1 + \dfrac{YZ}{4}\right)$ |
| Long | $\cosh\gamma\ell$ | $Z_c\sinh\gamma\ell$ | $\dfrac{1}{Z_c}\sinh\gamma\ell$ |

with $Z_c = \sqrt{z/y}$ and $\gamma = \sqrt{zy}$ per unit length.

## The Ferranti effect

At no load ($I_R = 0$), $V_R = V_S/A$. For medium and long lines, $|A| < 1$, so the **receiving-end voltage rises above the sending end**: the line's capacitance draws charging current through its inductance, and the reactive voltage drop is negative.

> **Example.** A 150 km line with $z = 0.03 + j0.49\ \Omega$/km and $y = j3.3\ \mu$S/km. Then $Z = 4.5 + j73.5\ \Omega$ and $Y = j495\ \mu$S, so
> $$A = 1 + \frac{YZ}{2} = 0.982 + j0.001$$
> At no load, $V_R = V_S/0.982$: the far end sits 1.85% **above** the sending end. On a 500 km line the rise exceeds 10%, so long lines need **shunt reactors** to absorb the excess charging current at light load.

## Surge impedance loading

When a lossless line is terminated in its surge impedance $Z_c$, the reactive power its inductance absorbs exactly equals what its capacitance generates, and the voltage profile is flat along the whole line. The power delivered then is the **surge impedance loading**:

$$
\text{SIL} = \frac{V_{LL}^2}{Z_c}
$$

> A 345 kV line with $Z_c = 300\ \Omega$: $\text{SIL} = 345^2/300 = 397$ MW.

SIL is a natural yardstick. Below it, the line generates net reactive power and voltages tend to rise; above it, the line absorbs reactive power and voltages sag. Short lines can be loaded well beyond SIL (limited by conductor heating), but long lines are limited to around SIL by voltage drop and **stability** (Lesson 4.1), since the maximum power a lossless line can carry is

$$
P_{max} = \frac{V_SV_R}{X}
$$

at a 90° angle between the ends. Series capacitors (cancelling part of $X$) and higher voltages raise it. That's why long-distance transmission keeps moving to 500 kV, 765 kV, and HVDC.

## Key takeaways

- Short lines: series $Z$. Medium: nominal π with $A = 1 + YZ/2$. Long: hyperbolic functions of $\gamma\ell$.
- Ferranti effect: at light load, $V_R = V_S/|A|$ rises above $V_S$; shunt reactors control it.
- $\text{SIL} = V^2/Z_c$ gives a flat voltage profile; long lines are limited near SIL; $P_{max} = V_SV_R/X$.
