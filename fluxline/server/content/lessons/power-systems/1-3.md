## The machine that makes the grid possible

Transformers let power be generated at around 20 kV, stepped up to hundreds of kilovolts for efficient transmission (lower current, so far lower $I^2R$ loss), then stepped down in stages to the 120/240 V at your outlet. They're also the most efficient machines ever built: large units exceed 99.5% efficiency.

## The equivalent circuit

A real transformer is an ideal transformer plus:

- **series** winding resistance $R$ and **leakage reactance** $X_\ell$ (flux that links only one winding);
- a **shunt** magnetizing branch, $R_c \parallel X_m$, representing core loss and the current needed to magnetize the core.

```circuit
T 0,0 ""
R 0,0 1.6,0 R
L 1.6,0 3.2,0 X_ℓ
W 3.2,0 4,0
DOT 4,0
R 4,0 4,2 R_c side=-1
W 4,0 5.2,0
L 5.2,0 5.2,2 X_m
W 5.2,0 6.4,0
T 6.4,0 ""
T 0,2 ""
W 0,2 6.4,2
DOT 4,2
T 6.4,2 ""
caption: Simplified per-unit transformer model, referred to one side: series resistance and leakage reactance, and a shunt magnetizing branch. In per-unit the ideal transformer disappears.
```

In per-unit, the ideal transformer disappears (Lesson 1.2). For most system studies the magnetizing branch (drawing about 1% of rated current) is neglected, leaving just the series impedance, typically $0.005 + j0.1$ pu for a large unit: reactance dominates.

**Tests** find the parameters: the **open-circuit test** (rated voltage, no load) measures core loss and magnetizing current; the **short-circuit test** (secondary shorted, rated current) measures copper loss and the series impedance.

## Voltage regulation and efficiency

$$
\text{VR} = \frac{V_{no\ load} - V_{full\ load}}{V_{full\ load}} \approx R\cos\theta + X\sin\theta \quad\text{(per-unit, lagging pf)}
$$

> A transformer with $X = 0.08$ pu (neglecting $R$) at full load and 0.8 pf lagging: $\text{VR} \approx 0.08 \times 0.6 = 4.8\%$. Tap changers adjust the turns ratio by about ±10% to compensate.

Efficiency counts **core loss** (constant whenever energized) and **copper loss** (proportional to load squared):

> A 10 MVA transformer has 10 kW core loss and 60 kW full-load copper loss. At full load and 0.8 pf: $P_{out} = 8$ MW, so $\eta = 8/8.07 = 99.13\%$. Efficiency peaks where copper loss equals core loss, at $\sqrt{10/60} = 41\%$ of rated load, which is about where distribution transformers spend most of their time.

## Three-phase connections

Three-phase transformers (or banks of three single-phase units) connect each side in wye (Y) or delta (Δ):

| Connection | Properties |
|---|---|
| Y–Y | no phase shift; problems with third harmonics unless grounded or with a tertiary Δ |
| **Δ–Y** (step-up/down) | 30° phase shift; Δ side traps zero-sequence currents and third harmonics; Y side provides a grounded neutral |
| Y–Δ | 30° phase shift |
| Δ–Δ | no neutral; one unit can be removed (open-Δ) |

Δ–Y is the workhorse: generator step-up transformers are Δ on the generator side and grounded-Y on the high-voltage side, and distribution substation transformers are Δ–grounded-Y, giving a neutral for single-phase customer loads. The **30° phase shift** must be accounted for when paralleling transformers or analyzing faults; the Δ winding's role in blocking zero-sequence current shows up again in Lesson 3.3.

## Key takeaways

- Model: series $R + jX_\ell$ plus shunt $R_c \parallel jX_m$; system studies usually keep only the series reactance.
- $\text{VR} \approx R\cos\theta + X\sin\theta$; efficiency peaks where copper loss equals core loss.
- Δ–Y connections give a 30° shift, a grounded neutral, and block zero-sequence current on the Δ side.
