## Staying in step

Every synchronous generator on an interconnected grid spins in lockstep with all the others. After a disturbance (a fault, a line tripping, a generator lost), the rotors swing relative to one another. **Stability** asks whether they settle back into synchronism, or a machine **slips poles** and must be tripped, possibly cascading into a blackout. The 2003 Northeast blackout, which cut power to 50 million people, began with lines tripping and ended in exactly such a cascade.

## The swing equation

A rotor accelerates when mechanical input power $P_m$ exceeds the electrical power it delivers, $P_e$. In per-unit, with inertia constant $H$ (stored kinetic energy at rated speed divided by rated MVA, typically 2–9 s):

$$
\frac{2H}{\omega_s}\frac{d^2\delta}{dt^2} = P_m - P_e
$$

where $\delta$ is the rotor angle relative to a synchronously rotating reference. For a generator connected through reactance $X$ to a large grid (an "infinite bus"):

$$
P_e = P_{max}\sin\delta, \qquad P_{max} = \frac{E'V}{X}
$$

## Small disturbances

Linearizing around the operating angle $\delta_0$ gives an oscillator. The rotor swings at the natural frequency

$$
\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{\omega_sP_{max}\cos\delta_0}{2H}}
$$

typically 0.5–2 Hz ("electromechanical modes"). Heavily loaded machines ($\delta_0$ near 90°) have little synchronizing torque and swing slowly. Poorly damped inter-area oscillations, where whole regions swing against each other at 0.1–0.8 Hz, are damped by **power system stabilizers** in generator excitation controls.

> With $H = 5$ s, $P_{max} = 2$ pu and $\delta_0 = 30°$: $\omega_n = \sqrt{377 \times 2 \times 0.866/10} = 8.1$ rad/s, or 1.29 Hz.

## Large disturbances: the equal-area criterion

During a fault, $P_e$ collapses (to zero for a fault at the generator's terminals), so the rotor **accelerates** and $\delta$ grows. After the fault clears, $P_e$ exceeds $P_m$ and the rotor **decelerates**. The machine stays in step if the decelerating energy available can absorb all the kinetic energy gained: on the power–angle curve, the **decelerating area** must be at least the **accelerating area**.

For a fault that drops $P_e$ to zero and is then cleared with the original network restored, the **critical clearing angle** is

$$
\cos\delta_{cr} = (\pi - 2\delta_0)\sin\delta_0 - \cos\delta_0
$$

and, since the rotor accelerates uniformly during the fault, the **critical clearing time** is

$$
t_{cr} = \sqrt{\frac{4H(\delta_{cr} - \delta_0)}{\omega_sP_m}}
$$

> **Example.** $P_{max} = 2$, $P_m = 1$, so $\delta_0 = 30°$. Then $\cos\delta_{cr} = (\pi - 1.047)(0.5) - 0.866 = 0.181$, so $\delta_{cr} = 79.6°$. With $H = 5$ s at 60 Hz: $t_{cr} = \sqrt{4(5)(0.865)/(377)} = 0.21$ s, about 13 cycles. Relays and breakers must clear the fault faster than that, with margin: modern protection clears transmission faults in 3–5 cycles.

## Improving stability

Faster fault clearing; higher inertia; lower series reactance (more lines, series capacitors); higher internal voltages through fast **excitation systems**; and fast valving, braking resistors or controlled islanding. Stability limits, not thermal limits, often cap how much power long lines can carry.

## Key takeaways

- Swing equation: $(2H/\omega_s)\,\ddot\delta = P_m - P_e$, with $P_e = P_{max}\sin\delta$.
- Small disturbances: oscillation at $\omega_n = \sqrt{\omega_sP_{max}\cos\delta_0/2H}$, typically around 1 Hz.
- Equal-area criterion; for a terminal fault $\cos\delta_{cr} = (\pi - 2\delta_0)\sin\delta_0 - \cos\delta_0$.
- Fast protection, inertia, lower reactance and excitation control improve stability.
