## When something shorts

A tree touches a line, lightning flashes over an insulator, a cable's insulation fails. **Faults** create currents many times normal load, which must be calculated to size circuit breakers (they must interrupt the worst-case current), set protective relays, and check that equipment withstands the mechanical forces and heating until the fault clears. The simplest case, a **balanced three-phase fault**, is also the most severe in most systems, and it can be analyzed with one per-phase circuit.

## Generators during a fault

A synchronous generator's internal reactance isn't constant during a fault. Just after it, currents induced in the rotor's damper windings and field oppose any change in flux, so the generator looks like a small reactance; as those currents decay, the reactance grows:

| Interval | Reactance | Typical value | Lasts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subtransient | $X''_d$ | 0.12–0.25 pu | first few cycles |
| Transient | $X'_d$ | 0.2–0.4 pu | up to ~1 s |
| Steady state | $X_d$ | 1–2 pu | after that |

Breakers must interrupt within a few cycles, so their duty is computed with $X''_d$.

## Calculating fault current

Model the network before the fault with each generator as a voltage behind its subtransient reactance, then use **Thévenin's theorem** at the faulted bus. With prefault voltage $V_f$ (usually 1.0 pu):

$$
I_f = \frac{V_f}{Z_{th}}
$$

> **Example.** A generator ($X''_d = 0.2$ pu) feeds a step-up transformer ($X = 0.1$ pu) on a 100 MVA base, and a fault occurs at the transformer's high-voltage terminals: $I_f = 1/0.3 = 3.33$ pu. On the 13.8 kV generator side, $I_{base} = 4184$ A, so the generator carries $3.33 \times 4184 = 13.9$ kA.

The **short-circuit MVA** at a bus summarizes its strength:

$$
S_{sc} = \frac{S_{base}}{Z_{th,pu}}
$$

(333 MVA in the example). A "stiff" bus with high short-circuit MVA holds its voltage well when loads change; a "weak" one doesn't, which matters a great deal for connecting inverter-based resources (Lesson 4.2).

## The DC offset

The fault current is an AC sinusoid **plus** a decaying DC component, whose size depends on where in the voltage cycle the fault occurs (it's the transient response of an RL circuit; Circuit Analysis I, Lesson 3.3). In the worst case, the first peak nearly doubles the symmetrical value. Breaker ratings account for this asymmetry with a multiplying factor (around 1.6 for the first half-cycle).

## Large networks: the Z-bus

For a network with many buses, invert $\mathbf Y_{bus}$ (with generators included as shunt admittances) to get $\mathbf Z_{bus}$. Its diagonal entry $Z_{kk}$ **is** the Thévenin impedance at bus $k$, so the fault current at every bus comes from one matrix:

$$
I_{f,k} = \frac{V_f}{Z_{kk}}, \qquad V_i^{(fault)} = V_f\left(1 - \frac{Z_{ik}}{Z_{kk}}\right)
$$

The second formula gives the voltage dip at every other bus during the fault, which determines which loads ride through and which drop out.

## Key takeaways

- Generator reactance during a fault rises from $X''_d$ through $X'_d$ to $X_d$; breakers use $X''_d$.
- $I_f = V_f/Z_{th}$; short-circuit MVA $= S_{base}/Z_{th}$ measures bus strength.
- Fault current includes a decaying DC offset that can nearly double the first peak.
- $\mathbf Z_{bus}$ diagonal entries give Thévenin impedances, hence fault currents and voltage dips everywhere.
