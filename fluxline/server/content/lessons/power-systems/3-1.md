## The grid's most-run calculation

Every few minutes, grid operators solve a **power flow** (load flow): given the power each generator produces and each load consumes, find the voltage magnitude and angle at every bus, and from those the flow on every line. It answers whether any line is overloaded, whether voltages stay within ±5%, and what happens if a line trips (contingency analysis, run for thousands of cases).

## The bus admittance matrix

Write nodal equations for the network (Circuit Analysis I, nodal analysis) in admittance form:

$$
\mathbf I = \mathbf Y_{bus}\mathbf V
$$

$\mathbf Y_{bus}$ is built by inspection:

- **diagonal** $Y_{ii}$ = sum of all admittances connected to bus $i$ (including shunt charging);
- **off-diagonal** $Y_{ij}$ = $-$(admittance of the branch between $i$ and $j$), or 0 if they aren't connected.

> **Example.** Three buses with line admittances $y_{12} = -j10$, $y_{13} = -j5$, $y_{23} = -j4$ pu (pure reactances):
> $$\mathbf Y_{bus} = \begin{bmatrix} -j15 & j10 & j5 \\ j10 & -j14 & j4 \\ j5 & j4 & -j9 \end{bmatrix}$$
> Real grids have tens of thousands of buses, each connected to only a few others, so $\mathbf Y_{bus}$ is very **sparse**, which is what makes solving it fast.

## Why it's nonlinear

Loads and generators are specified by **power**, not current, so the equations become nonlinear in the voltages:

$$
P_i - jQ_i = V_i^*\sum_j Y_{ij}V_j
$$

Each bus has four quantities ($P$, $Q$, $|V|$, $\delta$); two are known and two are unknown, depending on the **bus type**:

| Bus type | Known | Unknown | Typical |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Slack** (one per system) | $\lvert V\rvert$, $\delta = 0$ | $P$, $Q$ | a large generator; balances losses, sets the angle reference |
| **PV** | $P$, $\lvert V\rvert$ | $Q$, $\delta$ | generators with voltage control |
| **PQ** | $P$, $Q$ | $\lvert V\rvert$, $\delta$ | loads (most buses) |

## Solving it

- **Gauss–Seidel:** rearrange each bus equation for $V_i$ and iterate, using the newest values. Simple, but convergence is slow and degrades for large systems.
- **Newton–Raphson:** linearize the mismatch equations with the **Jacobian** and solve for corrections; converges quadratically, typically in 3–6 iterations regardless of size. It's the industry standard.
- **Fast decoupled** and **DC power flow** approximations exploit the fact that, on high-voltage networks, real power depends mainly on **angles** and reactive power mainly on **voltage magnitudes**.

## The DC power flow and the key insight

On a lossless line with voltages near 1 pu and small angle differences, the power flow is

$$
P_{12} = \frac{V_1V_2}{X}\sin(\delta_1 - \delta_2) \approx \frac{\delta_1 - \delta_2}{X}
$$

**Real power flows from leading angle to lagging angle**, roughly in proportion to the angle difference. Reactive power flows from higher voltage magnitude to lower. That linear "DC" approximation is used in electricity market software and fast contingency screening.

> **Example.** Two buses at 1.0 pu with $X = 0.1$ pu transfer 1.0 pu (100 MW on a 100 MVA base): $\sin\delta = 0.1$, so $\delta = 5.7°$. The theoretical maximum, at 90°, is $1/0.1 = 10$ pu.

## Key takeaways

- $\mathbf I = \mathbf Y_{bus}\mathbf V$: diagonal = sum of connected admittances, off-diagonal = minus branch admittance.
- Power injections make the equations nonlinear; buses are slack, PV or PQ.
- Newton–Raphson is the standard solver; Gauss–Seidel is simpler but slower.
- Real power follows angle differences ($P \approx \Delta\delta/X$); reactive power follows voltage magnitude.
