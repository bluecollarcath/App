## What a power line looks like electrically

An overhead transmission line is a set of bare aluminum conductors hanging from towers, kilometers long, a few meters apart. It's a transmission line in the Electromagnetics sense, characterized per unit length by **series resistance** and **inductance** and **shunt capacitance** (shunt conductance, from insulator leakage and corona, is usually negligible). At 60 Hz the wavelength is 5000 km, so most power lines are electrically short, but their parameters still decide how much power they carry and how voltages behave.

## Resistance

Conductors are **ACSR** (aluminum conductor, steel reinforced): aluminum strands for conductivity around a steel core for strength. Resistance per unit length is $\rho/A$, increased a few percent by stranding, skin effect (Electromagnetics, Lesson 3.2) and temperature (about 0.4% per °C). On high-voltage lines resistance is much smaller than reactance ($X/R$ of 5 to 20), but it sets the losses.

## Inductance

For a single-phase two-wire line or a balanced, transposed three-phase line, the inductance per phase per meter is

$$
L = 2\times10^{-7}\ln\frac{D_{eq}}{r'} \quad\text{H/m}
$$

- $r' = 0.7788\,r$ is the conductor's **geometric mean radius** (GMR), accounting for flux inside a solid round conductor.
- $D_{eq} = \sqrt[3]{D_{ab}D_{bc}D_{ca}}$ is the **geometric mean distance** between phases. Real lines are **transposed** (each conductor rotates through each position along the route) so the three phases see equal average inductance.

## Capacitance

Similarly, the capacitance to neutral per phase per meter is

$$
C = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(D_{eq}/r)} \quad\text{F/m}
$$

using the actual radius $r$ (charge sits on the surface, so there's no internal correction).

> **Example.** Conductor radius 1.5 cm ($r' = 1.17$ cm), $D_{eq} = 8$ m:
>
> $L = 2\times10^{-7}\ln(800/1.17) = 1.31$ mH/km, so $X_L = 377L = 0.49\ \Omega$/km at 60 Hz.
>
> $C = 2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(800/1.5) = 8.86$ nF/km.
>
> Surge impedance $Z_c = \sqrt{L/C} = 384\ \Omega$, typical of a single-conductor overhead line. ($LC$ is $1/c^2$, as Electromagnetics predicts.)

## Bundled conductors

Lines above about 230 kV use **bundles** of 2, 3 or 4 subconductors per phase, held tens of centimeters apart by spacers. A bundle acts like one conductor of much larger effective radius:

- the effective GMR rises, so **inductance drops** and **capacitance rises**, lowering $Z_c$ (to about 250–300 Ω) and raising the power the line can carry;
- the **electric field at the conductor surface drops**, preventing **corona** (air ionization that causes loss, audible noise and radio interference).

For a two-conductor bundle with spacing $d$, the effective GMR is $\sqrt{r'd}$; for three, $\sqrt[3]{r'd^2}$.

## Key takeaways

- $L = 2\times10^{-7}\ln(D_{eq}/r')$ H/m with $r' = 0.7788r$; $C = 2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(D_{eq}/r)$ F/m.
- $D_{eq} = \sqrt[3]{D_{ab}D_{bc}D_{ca}}$ for transposed lines.
- Typical overhead line: about 0.5 Ω/km reactance, 9 nF/km, $Z_c \approx 400\ \Omega$ (250–300 Ω bundled).
- Bundles lower $L$, raise $C$, and suppress corona.
