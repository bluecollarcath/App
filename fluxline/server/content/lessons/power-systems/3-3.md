## Breaking the symmetry

Most faults aren't balanced. About 70–80% are **single line-to-ground** (one conductor touching a tree or tower), with **line-to-line** and **double line-to-ground** faults making up most of the rest. An unbalanced system can't be reduced to one per-phase circuit, but Charles Fortescue showed in 1918 that **any** set of three unbalanced phasors is the sum of three balanced sets. Each balanced set can be analyzed per phase, and the results added.

## The three sequences

With the operator $a = 1\angle120°$ (so $a^2 = 1\angle240°$ and $1 + a + a^2 = 0$):

- **positive sequence** (1): balanced, normal rotation $a$–$b$–$c$;
- **negative sequence** (2): balanced, reversed rotation $a$–$c$–$b$;
- **zero sequence** (0): three equal phasors, all in phase.

$$
\begin{bmatrix} V_a \\ V_b \\ V_c \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & a^2 & a \\ 1 & a & a^2 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} V_0 \\ V_1 \\ V_2 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\begin{bmatrix} V_0 \\ V_1 \\ V_2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac13
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & a & a^2 \\ 1 & a^2 & a \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} V_a \\ V_b \\ V_c \end{bmatrix}
$$

The same transform applies to currents. Two physical facts:

- **Zero-sequence current** is $I_0 = (I_a + I_b + I_c)/3$, so it flows only if there's a return path: the neutral or ground. $3I_0$ is the neutral current, which ground-fault relays measure.
- **Negative-sequence current** creates a magnetic field rotating backwards in a motor or generator, inducing double-frequency currents that overheat the rotor. Generators have negative-sequence protection for this reason.

## Sequence networks

Balanced equipment doesn't mix sequences, so each sequence gets its own network:

- **Positive:** the normal per-phase network, with generator EMFs.
- **Negative:** same structure, no sources; generator reactance $X_2 \approx X''_d$.
- **Zero:** depends on grounding and transformer connections. Ungrounded neutrals block it; **Δ windings trap it** (zero-sequence current circulates inside the Δ but can't pass through); grounding impedances appear **three times** ($3Z_n$) because all three phases' zero-sequence current flows through them.

## Fault formulas

Connecting the sequence networks according to each fault's boundary conditions gives (with prefault voltage $V_f$ and Thévenin sequence impedances $Z_1$, $Z_2$, $Z_0$ at the fault):

| Fault | Fault current |
|---|---|
| Three-phase (balanced) | $I_f = \dfrac{V_f}{Z_1}$ |
| Single line-to-ground | $I_f = 3I_{a0} = \dfrac{3V_f}{Z_1 + Z_2 + Z_0}$ |
| Line-to-line | $\lvert I_f\rvert = \dfrac{\sqrt3\,V_f}{\lvert Z_1 + Z_2\rvert}$ |
| Double line-to-ground | $I_{a1} = \dfrac{V_f}{Z_1 + Z_2 \parallel Z_0}$ |

For the SLG fault, the three networks connect **in series**; for LL, the positive and negative networks connect in parallel (no zero sequence); for DLG, all three in parallel.

> **Example.** At a bus with $Z_1 = Z_2 = j0.2$ and $Z_0 = j0.1$ pu:
>
> - three-phase: $1/0.2 = 5.0$ pu;
> - SLG: $3/0.5 = 6.0$ pu, **larger** than the three-phase fault, which happens near solidly grounded transformers where $Z_0 < Z_1$;
> - line-to-line: $\sqrt3/0.4 = 4.33$ pu, always $\sqrt3/2 = 0.87$ times the three-phase value when $Z_1 = Z_2$.

Utilities often add a neutral grounding resistor or reactor to raise $Z_0$ and limit SLG currents.

## Key takeaways

- Unbalanced phasors = zero + positive + negative sequence sets ($a = 1\angle120°$).
- Zero sequence needs a ground or neutral path; Δ windings trap it; grounding impedance appears as $3Z_n$.
- SLG: $3V_f/(Z_1 + Z_2 + Z_0)$; LL: $\sqrt3V_f/|Z_1 + Z_2|$; three-phase: $V_f/Z_1$.
- SLG can exceed the three-phase fault current when $Z_0 < Z_1$.
