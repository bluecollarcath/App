## Why engineers normalize everything

A power system mixes voltage levels: 13.8 kV at the generator, 345 kV on transmission lines, 13.8 kV again on distribution feeders, 480 V and 120 V at customers. Analyzing it in volts, amps and ohms means converting through every transformer ratio. The **per-unit system** expresses every quantity as a fraction of a chosen **base**, so that:

- transformers (ideally) disappear: 1.0 pu on one side is 1.0 pu on the other;
- normal operating values cluster near 1.0, so errors stand out immediately (a bus at 1.3 pu is obviously wrong);
- equipment impedances, quoted in per-unit on their own ratings, fall in narrow, familiar ranges whatever the size of the machine.

$$
\text{per-unit value} = \frac{\text{actual value}}{\text{base value}}
$$

## Choosing bases

Pick two bases; the others follow. For three-phase systems the convention is a **three-phase power base** $S_{base}$ (one value for the whole system, often 100 MVA) and a **line-to-line voltage base** $V_{base}$ in each zone (changing across transformers by their ratio). Then

$$
I_{base} = \frac{S_{base}}{\sqrt3\,V_{base}}, \qquad Z_{base} = \frac{V_{base}^2}{S_{base}}
$$

> **Example.** $S_{base} = 100$ MVA, $V_{base} = 138$ kV: $Z_{base} = 138^2/100 = 190.4\ \Omega$ and $I_{base} = 100\times10^6/(\sqrt3 \times 138\times10^3) = 418$ A. A 20 Ω line is $20/190.4 = 0.105$ pu; a 300 A current is 0.72 pu.

In per-unit, the three-phase power equation loses its $\sqrt3$: $S = VI^*$ (all in pu).

## Change of base

Nameplate impedances are given in per-unit on the **equipment's own** rating. To use them in a system study, convert to the system base:

$$
Z_{pu,new} = Z_{pu,old}\left(\frac{V_{base,old}}{V_{base,new}}\right)^2\left(\frac{S_{base,new}}{S_{base,old}}\right)
$$

> A 50 MVA transformer with 8% (0.08 pu) impedance, in a study on a 100 MVA base at the same voltage: $0.08 \times 100/50 = 0.16$ pu.

## Typical per-unit values

| Equipment | Typical impedance on own rating |
|---|---|
| Large power transformer | 0.08–0.15 pu |
| Distribution transformer | 0.02–0.06 pu |
| Generator subtransient reactance $X''_d$ | 0.12–0.25 pu |
| Generator synchronous reactance $X_d$ | 1.0–2.0 pu |

So without knowing anything about a particular 500 MVA transformer, you can guess its impedance is about 0.1 pu, and a calculation that gives 2 pu is wrong.

## Key takeaways

- Choose $S_{base}$ (system-wide) and $V_{base}$ (per zone); $Z_{base} = V_{base}^2/S_{base}$, $I_{base} = S_{base}/\sqrt3V_{base}$.
- Per-unit hides transformer ratios and makes typical values easy to sanity-check.
- Change of base: $Z_{new} = Z_{old}(V_{old}/V_{new})^2(S_{new}/S_{old})$.
