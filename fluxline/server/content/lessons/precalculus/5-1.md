## The question calculus answers

In Lesson 2.2 you found the slope of a straight line: a rate of change that stays the same everywhere. But most things don't change at a steady rate. A dropped ball speeds up; a capacitor charges quickly at first and then slowly. So we need to ask:

> **How fast is something changing at one particular instant?**

That question, and its answer, is the first half of calculus. This lesson builds the idea using nothing but algebra you already know.

## Average rate of change

The **average rate of change** of a function between two inputs $a$ and $b$ is the slope of the straight line joining those two points on its graph:

$$
\text{average rate} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}
$$

That joining line is called a **secant line**.

> **Example 1.** A dropped ball falls a distance $d(t) = 5t^2$ meters in $t$ seconds. (That's close to real gravity.) What is its average speed between $t = 1$ s and $t = 3$ s?
>
> $d(1) = 5$ m and $d(3) = 45$ m, so
>
> $$
> \text{average speed} = \frac{45 - 5}{3 - 1} = \frac{40}{2} = 20\ \text{m/s}
> $$

But the ball doesn't fall at 20 m/s the whole time: it starts slow and speeds up. What is its speed at exactly $t = 1$?

## Zooming in

Take shorter and shorter time intervals starting at $t = 1$ and compute the average speed on each:

| Interval | Distance change | Time change | Average speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1$ to $2$ | $20 - 5 = 15$ | $1$ | $15$ m/s |
| $1$ to $1.5$ | $11.25 - 5 = 6.25$ | $0.5$ | $12.5$ m/s |
| $1$ to $1.1$ | $6.05 - 5 = 1.05$ | $0.1$ | $10.5$ m/s |
| $1$ to $1.01$ | $5.1005 - 5 = 0.1005$ | $0.01$ | $10.05$ m/s |
| $1$ to $1.001$ | $5.010005 - 5 = 0.010005$ | $0.001$ | $10.005$ m/s |

The averages are closing in on **10 m/s**. That's the ball's speed at the instant $t = 1$: its **instantaneous rate of change**.

## Proving it with algebra

We can't make the interval zero (that would divide by zero), but we can let it be any small amount $h$ and watch what happens as $h$ shrinks. The average speed from $t = 1$ to $t = 1 + h$ is

$$
\frac{d(1 + h) - d(1)}{h} = \frac{5(1 + h)^2 - 5}{h}
$$

Expand $(1 + h)^2 = 1 + 2h + h^2$ (Lesson 1.4):

$$
\frac{5 + 10h + 5h^2 - 5}{h} = \frac{10h + 5h^2}{h} = 10 + 5h
$$

As $h$ gets closer and closer to 0, $10 + 5h$ gets closer and closer to **10**. That matches the table exactly. (Check: $h = 0.1$ gives $10.5$.)

## Limits

What we just did has a name. The **limit** of an expression is the value it **approaches** as the input approaches some number, even if you can't plug that number in directly. We write

$$
\lim_{h \to 0} \,(10 + 5h) = 10
$$

Read: "the limit as $h$ approaches 0 of $10 + 5h$ is 10."

> **Example 2.** What does $\dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$ approach as $x$ approaches 1?
>
> At $x = 1$ it's $\dfrac{0}{0}$: undefined. But factor the top (difference of squares, Lesson 1.4):
>
> $$
> \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \frac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{x - 1} = x + 1 \quad (\text{for } x \ne 1)
> $$
>
> As $x \to 1$, $x + 1 \to 2$. So $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = 2$.

## The picture: secants become a tangent

As the second point slides toward the first, the secant line swings around and settles on a line that just grazes the curve at that point: the **tangent line**. Its slope is the instantaneous rate of change.

```plot
x: 0, 3.5, x
y: -1, 10, y
f: x^2 | y = x²
f: 4*x - 3 | secant from x = 1 to x = 3 (slope 4) | dash
f: 2*x - 1 | tangent at x = 1 (slope 2) | dot
mark: 1, 1, (1, 1)
mark: 3, 9, (3, 9)
caption: For $y = x^2$, the secant from $x = 1$ to $x = 3$ has slope $\frac{9 - 1}{3 - 1} = 4$. As the second point moves toward $x = 1$, the secant approaches the tangent line, whose slope is 2: the instantaneous rate of change at $x = 1$.
```

## This is the derivative

In calculus, the instantaneous rate of change is called the **derivative**, and the very first definition you'll see is exactly what we just did:

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

You now understand every piece of it: the average rate over a small interval $h$, and the limit as that interval shrinks to nothing.

**In electronics,** current is the rate at which electric charge flows. If $q(t)$ is the charge that has passed a point by time $t$, the current is its rate of change. Over an interval, the average current is $\dfrac{\Delta q}{\Delta t}$ (the symbol $\Delta$, "delta," means "change in"), and the instantaneous current is the limit. The same idea gives the voltage across an inductor and the current into a capacitor.

### Common mistakes

- **Plugging in too early.** In a limit like $\dfrac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$, substituting $x = 1$ right away gives $\dfrac{0}{0}$. Simplify first, then let $x$ approach the value.
- **Average vs instantaneous.** The average rate is over an interval; the instantaneous rate is at a single point.

## Key takeaways

- Average rate of change $= \dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$, the slope of the secant line.
- Shrinking the interval gives the **instantaneous** rate: the slope of the tangent line.
- A **limit** is the value an expression approaches; simplify before letting the input approach the value.
- The derivative is $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$, the first idea of calculus.
- Current is the rate of change of charge.
