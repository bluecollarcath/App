## The shape of AC electricity

The voltage from a wall outlet isn't steady; it swings smoothly up and down, reversing direction 120 times a second (60 full cycles). Radio signals, audio, and the vibration of a guitar string all have the same shape: a **sine wave**, the graph you met on the unit circle. To work with them, engineers describe every sine wave with three numbers: how **big**, how **fast**, and **when** it starts.

## The general sine wave

$$
v(t) = A \sin(\omega t + \varphi)
$$

| Symbol | Name | Meaning | Units |
|---|---|---|---|
| $A$ | **amplitude** | the peak height of the wave | volts (or whatever $v$ measures) |
| $\omega$ | **angular frequency** | how fast the angle turns | radians per second (rad/s) |
| $\varphi$ | **phase** | the starting angle: a head start or delay | radians (or degrees) |

($\omega$ is the Greek letter **omega**; $\varphi$ is **phi**.)

## Amplitude

The wave swings between $+A$ and $-A$ because $\sin$ always stays between $-1$ and $1$. The total swing from bottom to top, the **peak-to-peak** value, is $2A$.

## Period and frequency

- The **period** $T$ is the time for one complete cycle (in seconds).
- The **frequency** $f$ is how many cycles happen per second, measured in **hertz** (Hz):

$$
f = \frac{1}{T} \qquad T = \frac{1}{f}
$$

A 60 Hz wave repeats 60 times per second, so each cycle takes $T = \tfrac{1}{60}$ s $\approx 16.7$ ms.

**Angular frequency.** One cycle is one trip around the unit circle: $2\pi$ radians. Doing $f$ cycles per second means turning $2\pi f$ radians per second:

$$
\omega = 2\pi f
$$

> **Example 1 (wall outlet, US).** $f = 60$ Hz, so $\omega = 2\pi \times 60 \approx 377$ rad/s. The outlet's peak voltage is about 170 V, so
>
> $$
> v(t) = 170 \sin(377\,t)
> $$

```plot
x: 0, 33.33, t (ms)
y: -200, 200, v (V)
f: 170*sin(2*pi*0.06*x) | v(t) = 170 sin(377t)
hline: 0
vline: 16.667, T = 16.7 ms
mark: 4.1667, 170, peak +170 V
mark: 12.5, -170
caption: Two cycles of the US mains voltage, swinging between +170 V and −170 V. Each cycle takes $T = 1/60$ s ≈ 16.7 ms. The peak is reached a quarter-period after the start, at about 4.2 ms.
```

**About the "120 V" label.** Outlets are rated 120 V, but the peak is about 170 V. The 120 V is the **RMS** value, a kind of average that gives the same heating effect as 120 V of steady DC. For a sine wave, $\text{peak} = \sqrt{2} \times \text{RMS} \approx 1.414 \times 120 \approx 170$ V. You'll learn where the $\sqrt{2}$ comes from in Calculus.

> **Example 2.** A signal is $v(t) = 10 \sin(1000\,t)$ volts. Find its amplitude, frequency and period.
>
> Amplitude: $A = 10$ V. Angular frequency: $\omega = 1000$ rad/s.
>
> $f = \dfrac{\omega}{2\pi} = \dfrac{1000}{6.2832} \approx 159.2$ Hz, and $T = \dfrac{1}{f} \approx 6.28$ ms.

## Phase

The phase $\varphi$ gives the wave a head start. A **positive** phase shifts the wave **earlier** (to the left); we say it **leads**. A negative phase shifts it later; it **lags**.

```plot
x: 0, 12.57, ωt (radians)
y: -1.3, 1.3
f: sin(x) | sin(ωt)
f: sin(x + 0.7854) | sin(ωt + 45°): leads by 45° | dash
f: sin(x + 1.5708) | sin(ωt + 90°) = cos(ωt) | dot
hline: 0
caption: Positive phase moves the wave earlier. Adding 90° gives exactly the cosine wave, which reaches its peak a quarter cycle before the sine does.
```

To convert a phase into a time shift, divide by $\omega$: a phase of $\varphi$ radians is a time shift of $\dfrac{\varphi}{\omega}$ seconds.

> **Example 3.** At 60 Hz ($\omega \approx 377$ rad/s), a 90° phase lead ($\pi/2 \approx 1.571$ rad) is a time shift of $\dfrac{1.571}{377} \approx 4.17$ ms, a quarter of the 16.7 ms period, as expected.

Phase is central in AC circuits. In an inductor, the voltage **leads** the current by 90°; in a capacitor, it **lags** by 90°. You'll see why in Circuit Analysis, and you now have the vocabulary to understand it.

## Offset

Sometimes a wave rides on top of a steady value: $v(t) = D + A\sin(\omega t)$ swings between $D - A$ and $D + A$. For example, an audio signal on a 5 V supply might be $2.5 + 1\sin(\omega t)$, swinging between 1.5 V and 3.5 V.

### Common mistakes

- **Confusing $f$ and $\omega$.** $f$ is cycles per second (Hz); $\omega = 2\pi f$ is radians per second. Formulas inside $\sin(\ )$ need $\omega$.
- **Calculator in degree mode** when $\omega t$ is in radians. $\sin(377t)$ needs radian mode.
- **Peak vs RMS.** Outlets are labeled with RMS values; the actual peak is $\sqrt{2}$ times larger.

## Key takeaways

- $v(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$: amplitude $A$, angular frequency $\omega$, phase $\varphi$.
- Period $T$ and frequency $f$: $f = 1/T$, measured in hertz.
- $\omega = 2\pi f$ (rad/s); US mains is 60 Hz, $\omega \approx 377$ rad/s.
- Positive phase leads (shifts earlier); a 90° lead turns sine into cosine.
- Peak-to-peak is $2A$; for a sine wave, peak $= \sqrt{2} \times$ RMS.
