## Building new functions from old ones

Once you know a few basic graphs, you can understand many more by **moving, stretching and combining** them. This lesson covers three tools: transformations (moving graphs), composition (chaining functions) and inverses (undoing functions). All three come up constantly in calculus and engineering.

## Transformations: moving a graph

Start from a known function $f(x)$. Then:

| New function | What happens to the graph |
|---|---|
| $f(x) + k$ | shifts **up** by $k$ (down if $k$ is negative) |
| $f(x - h)$ | shifts **right** by $h$ |
| $f(x + h)$ | shifts **left** by $h$ |
| $a \cdot f(x)$ | stretches **vertically** by a factor $a$ |
| $-f(x)$ | flips **upside down** (across the $x$-axis) |

```plot
x: -3, 5, x
y: -1, 8, y
f: x^2 | y = x²
f: (x - 2)^2 | y = (x − 2)² (right 2) | dash
f: x^2 + 1 | y = x² + 1 (up 1) | dot
mark: 2, 0, (2, 0)
mark: 0, 1, (0, 1)
caption: Starting from $y = x^2$: subtracting 2 **inside** moves the parabola right 2; adding 1 **outside** moves it up 1.
```

**Why does $f(x - 2)$ move right, not left?** In $(x - 2)^2$, the bottom of the parabola happens where the inside is zero: $x - 2 = 0$, so $x = 2$. Everything that used to happen at $x$ now happens 2 units later. Changes **inside** the parentheses affect the input and feel backwards; changes **outside** affect the output and do what they look like.

> **Example 1.** $y = 3(x + 1)^2 - 4$ is the parabola $x^2$ shifted **left** 1, stretched **vertically** by 3, then shifted **down** 4. Its lowest point is at $(-1, -4)$.

## Composition: chaining functions

**Composing** functions means feeding the output of one into another. Written $f(g(x))$, read "$f$ of $g$ of $x$": **do $g$ first, then $f$**.

> **Example 2.** Let $f(x) = x^2$ and $g(x) = x + 3$.
>
> $f(g(2))$: first $g(2) = 2 + 3 = 5$, then $f(5) = 25$.
>
> $g(f(2))$: first $f(2) = 4$, then $g(4) = 7$.

The order matters: $f(g(2)) = 25$ but $g(f(2)) = 7$. You can also compose as formulas: $f(g(x)) = (x + 3)^2$, while $g(f(x)) = x^2 + 3$.

**Why this matters for calculus:** most real formulas are compositions. $\sin(2\pi f t)$ is "multiply by $2\pi f$, then take the sine." Calculus has a rule for these, the **chain rule**, and recognizing the inside and outside functions is most of the work.

## Inverse functions: undoing a function

An **inverse function** undoes another function. If $f$ turns 2 into 7, its inverse $f^{-1}$ turns 7 back into 2. Read $f^{-1}$ as "$f$ inverse."

**To find an inverse:**
1. Write $y = f(x)$.
2. Solve for $x$ in terms of $y$ (Lesson 1.3).
3. Swap the names: call the input $x$ again.

> **Example 3.** Find the inverse of $f(x) = 2x + 3$.
>
> $y = 2x + 3 \;\Rightarrow\; y - 3 = 2x \;\Rightarrow\; x = \dfrac{y - 3}{2}$.
>
> So $f^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{x - 3}{2}$.
>
> **Check:** $f(4) = 11$, and $f^{-1}(11) = \dfrac{11 - 3}{2} = 4$. ✓ The inverse takes you back to where you started.

The graph of an inverse is the original graph **mirrored across the line $y = x$**, because inputs and outputs trade places.

You already know some inverse pairs:
- $x^2$ and $\sqrt{x}$ (for $x \ge 0$): squaring then square-rooting gets you back.
- Converting °C to °F and °F to °C (Lesson 1.3).
- Coming next unit: exponentials and **logarithms**, the most important inverse pair in this course.

### Common mistake

**$f^{-1}(x)$ does not mean $\dfrac{1}{f(x)}$.** Here the $-1$ means "inverse function," not a power. (It's an unfortunate notation that trips everyone up at first.)

## An engineering example

A temperature sensor outputs a voltage that depends on temperature: $V = 0.01\,T$, which is 10 mV per °C. Your circuit measures $V$, but you want $T$. That's the inverse function:

$$
T = \frac{V}{0.01} = 100\,V
$$

A reading of 0.25 V means $T = 25$ °C. Every sensor in every gadget is read this way: measure the output, apply the inverse function, get the quantity you care about.

## Key takeaways

- Outside changes move graphs as expected: $f(x) + k$ goes up. Inside changes feel backward: $f(x - h)$ goes **right**.
- $a \cdot f(x)$ stretches vertically; $-f(x)$ flips upside down.
- Composition $f(g(x))$: do $g$ first, then $f$. Order matters.
- An inverse $f^{-1}$ undoes $f$: solve $y = f(x)$ for $x$. Its graph is mirrored across $y = x$.
- $f^{-1}(x)$ is not $1/f(x)$.
