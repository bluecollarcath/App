## The question a logarithm answers

Exponents answer "what is 2 multiplied by itself 3 times?" ($2^3 = 8$). A **logarithm** answers the reverse question: **"2 to what power gives 8?"** The answer is 3, and we write

$$
\log_2 8 = 3
$$

Read it as "log base 2 of 8 is 3." In general:

$$
\log_b x = y \quad\text{means}\quad b^y = x
$$

A logarithm **is an exponent**: the exponent you need to put on the base $b$ to get $x$. Whenever logs feel confusing, rewrite them as exponents.

> **Example 1.** $\log_2 32 = 5$, because $2^5 = 32$.
>
> **Example 2.** $\log_{10} 1000 = 3$, because $10^3 = 1000$.
>
> **Example 3.** $\log_{10} 0.01 = -2$, because $10^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{100} = 0.01$.
>
> **Example 4.** $\log_5 1 = 0$, because $5^0 = 1$. (The log of 1 is always 0.)

## The two logs on your calculator

- **log** means base 10: $\log 1000 = 3$. Called the **common log**.
- **ln** means base $e$: $\ln x = \log_e x$. Called the **natural log**, it's the one used most in science and engineering.

So $\ln e = 1$ (because $e^1 = e$) and $\ln 1 = 0$.

## Logs undo exponentials

Logarithms and exponentials are **inverse functions** (Lesson 2.4): each undoes the other.

$$
\ln(e^x) = x \qquad e^{\ln x} = x
$$

The same is true for any base: $\log_{10}(10^x) = x$.

## The log rules

Because logs are exponents, the exponent rules from Lesson 1.2 turn into log rules. They work for any base:

| Rule | Comes from | Example |
|---|---|---|
| $\log(xy) = \log x + \log y$ | multiplying adds exponents | $\log 2 + \log 5 = \log 10 = 1$ |
| $\log\!\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right) = \log x - \log y$ | dividing subtracts exponents | $\log 1000 - \log 10 = \log 100 = 2$ |
| $\log(x^n) = n \log x$ | a power multiplies exponents | $\ln(e^4) = 4 \ln e = 4$ |

These rules are why logs were invented: they turn multiplication into addition, and powers into multiplication.

### Common mistakes

- **$\log(x + y)$ is not $\log x + \log y$.** The sum rule is for a **product** inside the log. There's no simple rule for a sum.
- **$\log x \cdot \log y$ is not $\log(xy)$** either.
- **Logs of zero or negative numbers don't exist** (with ordinary numbers). Your calculator will give an error.

## Solving exponential equations

The main practical use of logs: getting a variable **down from an exponent**. Take the log of both sides, then use $\log(x^n) = n \log x$.

> **Example 5.** Solve $2^x = 20$.
>
> Take ln of both sides: $\ln(2^x) = \ln 20$, so $x \ln 2 = \ln 20$.
>
> Divide: $x = \dfrac{\ln 20}{\ln 2} \approx \dfrac{2.996}{0.693} \approx 4.32$.
>
> **Check:** $2^{4.32} \approx 20$. ✓ (It makes sense: $2^4 = 16$ and $2^5 = 32$.)

> **Example 6.** How long does money take to double at 5% per year?
>
> Solve $1.05^t = 2$: $\;t = \dfrac{\ln 2}{\ln 1.05} \approx \dfrac{0.6931}{0.04879} \approx 14.2$ years.

> **Example 7 (electronics).** A capacitor's voltage decays as $e^{-t/\tau}$. When has it fallen to 10% of its starting value?
>
> Solve $e^{-t/\tau} = 0.1$. Take ln: $-\dfrac{t}{\tau} = \ln 0.1 \approx -2.303$.
>
> So $t \approx 2.303\,\tau$. With $\tau = 3$ ms, that's about 6.9 ms.

## Decibels

Engineers compare signal powers with **decibels** (dB), a log scale:

$$
\text{gain in dB} = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}}\right)
$$

Logs squeeze huge ranges into manageable numbers:

| Power ratio | Decibels |
|---|---|
| ×2 | +3 dB |
| ×10 | +10 dB |
| ×100 | +20 dB |
| ×1000 | +30 dB |
| ×0.5 (half) | −3 dB |

An amplifier that multiplies power by 1000 has a gain of $10 \log_{10} 1000 = 30$ dB. Stacking two amplifiers **multiplies** their power gains but simply **adds** their decibels, thanks to the log rule $\log(xy) = \log x + \log y$.

## Key takeaways

- $\log_b x = y$ means $b^y = x$: a log is an exponent.
- log is base 10; ln is base $e$. Logs undo exponentials: $\ln(e^x) = x$.
- $\log(xy) = \log x + \log y$, $\log(x/y) = \log x - \log y$, $\log(x^n) = n \log x$; but $\log(x + y)$ doesn't split.
- To solve for an exponent, take the log of both sides: $b^x = c \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{\ln c}{\ln b}$.
- Decibels: $10 \log_{10}(\text{power ratio})$; ×10 is 10 dB, ×2 is about 3 dB.
