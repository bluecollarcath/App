## Why trigonometry?

Trigonometry started as the math of triangles: finding a distance or angle you can't measure directly. For an electrical engineer, it matters for a bigger reason: the electricity in every wall outlet is a **sine wave**, and describing AC circuits is mostly trigonometry. This lesson covers triangles and angles; the next two turn them into the waves you'll use in circuits.

## Angles and right triangles

Angles are measured in **degrees** (°): a full turn is 360°, a half turn is 180°, and a quarter turn (a square corner) is 90°, called a **right angle**. A **right triangle** has one right angle. Its longest side, opposite the right angle, is the **hypotenuse**.

## The Pythagorean theorem

In a right triangle with shorter sides $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$:

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

> **Example 1.** The two shorter sides are 3 and 4. Then $c^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$, so $c = 5$.
>
> **Example 2.** The hypotenuse is 10 and one side is 6. Then $6^2 + b^2 = 10^2$, so $b^2 = 100 - 36 = 64$ and $b = 8$.

The three basic **trigonometric ratios** are:

$$
\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \qquad
\cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \qquad
\tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}
$$

A popular way to remember them: **SOH CAH TOA** (Sine = Opposite/Hypotenuse, Cosine = Adjacent/Hypotenuse, Tangent = Opposite/Adjacent).

The key fact: **these ratios depend only on the angle**, not on the triangle's size. Every right triangle with a 30° angle has $\sin 30° = 0.5$, whether it's an inch across or a mile. That's why your calculator can have a sin button.

> **Example 3.** In the 3-4-5 triangle, take $\theta$ as the angle opposite the side of length 3. Then $\sin\theta = \tfrac{3}{5} = 0.6$, $\cos\theta = \tfrac{4}{5} = 0.8$ and $\tan\theta = \tfrac{3}{4} = 0.75$.

## Using trig to find sides

Rearrange a ratio, just like Ohm's law in Lesson 1.3.

> **Example 4.** A ramp is 10 m long and rises at 30°. How high does it go, and how far does it reach horizontally?
>
> Height (opposite): $\sin 30° = \dfrac{\text{opp}}{10}$, so $\text{opp} = 10 \sin 30° = 10 \times 0.5 = 5$ m.
>
> Reach (adjacent): $\text{adj} = 10 \cos 30° \approx 10 \times 0.866 = 8.66$ m.

## Using trig to find angles

To go from a ratio back to an angle, use the **inverse** trig functions, written $\sin^{-1}$, $\cos^{-1}$ and $\tan^{-1}$ (also called arcsin, arccos, arctan). Your calculator has them, usually as the second function of each button.

> **Example 5.** A right triangle has opposite side 4 and adjacent side 3. Then $\tan\theta = \tfrac{4}{3}$, so
>
> $$
> \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{4}{3}\right) \approx 53.13°
> $$

As in Lesson 2.4, the $-1$ means "inverse function," not $\dfrac{1}{\sin}$.

## Radians

Degrees aren't the only way to measure angles. **Radians** measure an angle by the arc it sweeps on a circle, measured in radiuses: one radian is the angle where the arc length equals the radius (about 57.3°).

A full circle's circumference is $2\pi r$, so a full turn is $2\pi$ radians:

$$
360° = 2\pi\ \text{rad} \qquad 180° = \pi\ \text{rad}
$$

**To convert:** multiply degrees by $\dfrac{\pi}{180}$ to get radians; multiply radians by $\dfrac{180}{\pi}$ to get degrees.

| Degrees | 0° | 30° | 45° | 60° | 90° | 180° | 360° |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Radians | $0$ | $\tfrac{\pi}{6}$ | $\tfrac{\pi}{4}$ | $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$ | $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ | $\pi$ | $2\pi$ |

**Why bother with radians?** Calculus formulas and AC circuit formulas (like $\omega = 2\pi f$, coming in Lesson 4.3) only work cleanly in radians. Engineers use radians for almost everything except everyday angle descriptions.

### Common mistakes

- **Calculator in the wrong mode.** Calculators have a DEG/RAD setting. $\sin 30$ gives $0.5$ in degree mode but $-0.988$ in radian mode. When an answer looks strange, check the mode first.
- **Labeling sides from the wrong angle.** "Opposite" and "adjacent" depend on which angle you're using. The hypotenuse never changes.
- **Using Pythagoras or SOH CAH TOA on a triangle without a right angle.** These only work for right triangles.

## Where you'll see this: the power triangle

In AC circuits, power is described by a right triangle. If a motor draws an **apparent power** $S = 1000$ VA (the hypotenuse) at an angle $\theta = 36.87°$, the **real power** that does useful work is the adjacent side:

$$
P = S\cos\theta = 1000 \times 0.8 = 800\ \text{W}
$$

The factor $\cos\theta$ is called the **power factor**, and utilities care about it a lot. You'll study it in Power Systems.

## Key takeaways

- Pythagorean theorem: $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ for right triangles.
- SOH CAH TOA: $\sin\theta = \tfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}$, $\cos\theta = \tfrac{\text{adj}}{\text{hyp}}$, $\tan\theta = \tfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}}$. They depend only on the angle.
- Inverse trig ($\sin^{-1}$, …) turns a ratio back into an angle.
- $180° = \pi$ radians; multiply by $\pi/180$ to convert to radians.
- Check your calculator's DEG/RAD mode.
