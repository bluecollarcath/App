## Where quadratics show up

A **quadratic** is an expression whose highest power is 2, like $x^2 - x - 6$. They appear whenever something depends on a square: the area of a square, the distance a dropped object falls, the power in a resistor ($P = I^2R$), and, later in electronics, the equation that decides whether a circuit rings like a bell or settles smoothly. This lesson covers how to multiply them out, factor them back, and solve them.

## Expanding: multiply every term by every term

To multiply two brackets, multiply **each term in the first by each term in the second**, then combine like terms:

$$
(x + 2)(x + 3) = x \cdot x + x \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot x + 2 \cdot 3 = x^2 + 3x + 2x + 6 = x^2 + 5x + 6
$$

**Like terms** are terms with the same variable and power; $3x$ and $2x$ combine to $5x$, but $x^2$ and $x$ can't be combined.

Two patterns worth memorizing:

$$
(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \qquad (a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2
$$

> **Example 1.** $(x + 5)^2 = (x + 5)(x + 5) = x^2 + 5x + 5x + 25 = x^2 + 10x + 25$.

### Common mistake

**$(x + 5)^2$ is not $x^2 + 25$.** The middle term $2 \cdot 5 \cdot x = 10x$ is easy to forget. Check with a number: if $x = 1$, then $(1 + 5)^2 = 36$, while $1 + 25 = 26$. Different, so the shortcut is wrong.

## Factoring: expanding in reverse

**Factoring** rewrites an expression as a product. It's the key to solving quadratics.

**1. Pull out a common factor.** Look for something that divides every term:

$$
6x^2 + 9x = 3x(2x + 3)
$$

Check by distributing: $3x \cdot 2x = 6x^2$ and $3x \cdot 3 = 9x$. ✓

**2. Factor $x^2 + bx + c$.** Find two numbers that **multiply to $c$** and **add to $b$**.

> **Example 2.** Factor $x^2 + 7x + 12$.
>
> We need two numbers that multiply to 12 and add to 7. List the pairs that multiply to 12: $1 \times 12$, $2 \times 6$, $3 \times 4$. Only $3 + 4 = 7$. So
>
> $$
> x^2 + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)
> $$

> **Example 3.** Factor $x^2 - x - 6$.
>
> Multiply to $-6$ and add to $-1$. Since the product is negative, one number is negative. Try $-3$ and $2$: $(-3)(2) = -6$ and $-3 + 2 = -1$. ✓ So $x^2 - x - 6 = (x - 3)(x + 2)$.

**3. Difference of squares.** $x^2 - 9 = (x - 3)(x + 3)$, from the pattern $a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$.

## Solving quadratic equations by factoring

The key fact: **if two numbers multiply to zero, at least one of them is zero.** (There's no other way to get zero from a product.)

> **Example 4.** Solve $x^2 - x - 6 = 0$.
>
> Factor: $(x - 3)(x + 2) = 0$.
>
> So either $x - 3 = 0$, giving $x = 3$, or $x + 2 = 0$, giving $x = -2$.
>
> **Check** $x = -2$: $(-2)^2 - (-2) - 6 = 4 + 2 - 6 = 0$. ✓

A quadratic usually has **two** solutions. On a graph, they're where the curve crosses the horizontal axis:

```plot
x: -4, 5, x
y: -8, 8, y
f: x^2 - x - 6 | y = x² − x − 6
hline: 0
mark: -2, 0, x = −2
mark: 3, 0, x = 3
caption: The graph of a quadratic is a U-shaped curve called a **parabola**. It crosses $y = 0$ at exactly the two solutions of $x^2 - x - 6 = 0$.
```

**Important:** the equation must equal **zero** before you factor. To solve $x^2 = x + 6$, first subtract $x$ and $6$ from both sides to get $x^2 - x - 6 = 0$.

## The quadratic formula

Some quadratics don't factor nicely. The **quadratic formula** solves any equation of the form $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$:

$$
x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}
$$

The $\pm$ ("plus or minus") means you get two answers: one using $+$ and one using $-$.

> **Example 5.** Solve $2x^2 + 3x - 2 = 0$.
>
> Here $a = 2$, $b = 3$, $c = -2$. Work out the piece under the root first:
>
> $$
> b^2 - 4ac = 3^2 - 4(2)(-2) = 9 + 16 = 25
> $$
>
> So $x = \dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt{25}}{2 \cdot 2} = \dfrac{-3 \pm 5}{4}$.
>
> With $+$: $x = \dfrac{2}{4} = 0.5$. With $-$: $x = \dfrac{-8}{4} = -2$.

> **Example 6.** Solve $x^2 + 4x - 1 = 0$ (this one doesn't factor).
>
> $a = 1$, $b = 4$, $c = -1$: $\;b^2 - 4ac = 16 + 4 = 20$.
>
> $x = \dfrac{-4 \pm \sqrt{20}}{2} \approx \dfrac{-4 \pm 4.472}{2}$, so $x \approx 0.236$ or $x \approx -4.236$.

**The discriminant** $b^2 - 4ac$ (the part under the root) tells you how many real solutions there are before you finish:

- **Positive:** two solutions (the parabola crosses the axis twice).
- **Zero:** one solution (it just touches the axis).
- **Negative:** no real solutions (it never reaches the axis), because you can't take the square root of a negative number with ordinary numbers.

### Common mistakes

- **Sign errors with $-b$ and negative $c$.** Write $a$, $b$ and $c$ down with their signs before plugging in, and put negative numbers in parentheses: $-4(2)(-2) = +16$.
- **Dividing only part of the top by $2a$.** The whole $-b \pm \sqrt{\ldots}$ is divided by $2a$.




## Key takeaways

- Expanding: multiply every term by every term; $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$, not $a^2 + b^2$.
- Factoring $x^2 + bx + c$: find two numbers that multiply to $c$ and add to $b$.
- To solve, get zero on one side, factor, and set each factor to zero.
- The quadratic formula $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$ solves any quadratic; the discriminant $b^2 - 4ac$ tells you how many solutions.
- The solutions are where the parabola crosses the horizontal axis.
