## The other half of calculus

Lesson 5.1 asked: **how fast is something changing right now?** This lesson asks the opposite question: **if I know the rate, how much has built up in total?**

- If you know a car's speed at every moment, how far did it go?
- If you know the current into a capacitor at every moment, how much charge did it collect?
- If you know a heater's power at every moment, how much energy did it use?

The answer, in every case, is an **area** under a graph.

## Constant rates: a rectangle

When a rate is constant, the total is just **rate × time**:

> **Example 1.** Driving at a steady 60 km/h for 2.5 hours covers $60 \times 2.5 = 150$ km.

Draw speed against time, and that's a flat line at 60. The distance, $60 \times 2.5$, is the **area of the rectangle** under the line: height (speed) × width (time). Notice the units work out: km/h × h = km.

## Changing rates: still an area

Now let the rate change. Our falling ball from Lesson 5.1 has speed $v(t) = 10t$ m/s: 0 at the start, 10 m/s after 1 second, 30 m/s after 3 seconds. Its speed graph is a sloping line, and the distance fallen is still **the area under the speed graph**.

From 0 to 3 seconds, that area is a triangle with base 3 and height 30:

$$
\text{distance} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 3 \times 30 = 45\ \text{m}
$$

That matches Lesson 5.1, where $d(3) = 5 \times 3^2 = 45$ m. The rate and the total fit together perfectly.

## When the shape isn't simple: rectangles

For curved graphs there's no triangle formula. The trick is to **chop the time into thin slices**, pretend the rate is constant during each slice, and add up the rectangles.

```plot
x: 0, 3.2, t (s)
y: 0, 32, speed (m/s)
f: 10*x | v(t) = 10t (exact speed)
f: 10*floor(2*x)/2 | tops of the rectangles (speed at the start of each 0.5 s slice) | dash
caption: Six rectangles of width 0.5 s, each using the speed at the start of its slice. Their total area (37.5 m) underestimates the true 45 m because each rectangle misses a small triangle. Thinner slices miss less.
```

> **Example 2.** Estimate the distance from 0 to 3 s with 3 slices of 1 second, using the speed at the **start** of each slice:
>
> $$
> (0 + 10 + 20) \times 1 = 30\ \text{m}
> $$
>
> Using the speed at the **end** of each slice instead: $(10 + 20 + 30) \times 1 = 60$ m.

The true answer is between them. With more, thinner slices, both estimates close in on it:

| Number of slices | Start-of-slice estimate | End-of-slice estimate |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 30 m | 60 m |
| 6 | 37.5 m | 52.5 m |
| 30 | 43.5 m | 46.5 m |
| 300 | 44.85 m | 45.15 m |

As the slices get infinitely thin, the sum approaches exactly 45 m. That's another **limit** (Lesson 5.1). This idea is the **integral**, written

$$
\int_0^3 10t\,dt = 45
$$

Read it as "the integral of $10t$ from 0 to 3." The tall S is a stretched "S" for **sum**: a sum of infinitely many thin rectangles, each $10t$ tall and $dt$ wide.

## Accumulation in electronics

The same idea appears throughout circuits:

- **Charge** is the area under a **current** graph: $\text{charge} = \text{current} \times \text{time}$ when the current is constant. A steady 3 mA for 5 s delivers $0.003 \times 5 = 0.015$ C = 15 mC (coulombs, the unit of charge).
- **Energy** is the area under a **power** graph. A 1.5 kW heater running 2 hours uses $1.5 \times 2 = 3$ kWh (kilowatt-hours, the unit on your electric bill).

> **Example 3.** A current rises steadily from 0 to 4 A over 2 seconds. How much charge flows?
>
> The current graph is a triangle with base 2 s and height 4 A: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 2 \times 4 = 4$ C.

## The big idea: rate and total are opposites

Put the two lessons side by side:

- From the **distance** $d(t) = 5t^2$, the instantaneous rate (Lesson 5.1) gave the **speed** $10t$.
- From the **speed** $10t$, the area (this lesson) gave back the **distance**, 45 m after 3 s.

Finding a rate and adding up a total **undo each other**, like multiplication and division. This is the **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus**, the most important idea in the subject, and you've just seen it work with a real example.

### Common mistakes

- **Forgetting units.** Area under a graph has units of (vertical units) × (horizontal units): (m/s) × s = m, A × s = C, kW × h = kWh.
- **Mixing time units.** If current is in amps, time must be in seconds to get coulombs; if power is in kW, time in hours gives kWh.

## Key takeaways

- With a constant rate, total = rate × time: the area of a rectangle.
- With a changing rate, the total is still the **area under the rate graph**.
- Thin rectangles estimate the area; in the limit they give it exactly. That's the **integral**.
- Charge is the area under current; energy is the area under power. Watch the units.
- Rates and totals undo each other: the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
