## Why exponents matter

Engineers work with very large and very small numbers all the time. A radio signal might be 2,400,000,000 cycles per second; a capacitor might store 0.000000001 farads. Writing all those zeros is slow and error-prone. Exponents give a short, exact way to write them, and the prefixes on electronic parts (kilo, milli, micro, nano) are built on them.

## What an exponent means

An exponent says how many times to multiply a number by itself:

$$
2^5 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 32
$$

The 2 is the **base** and the 5 is the **exponent** (or **power**). Read $2^5$ as "two to the fifth power." Two special names: $x^2$ is "$x$ squared" and $x^3$ is "$x$ cubed."

## The exponent rules

Every rule below comes from just counting how many times the base is multiplied. Try writing each one out the long way once to see why it works.

| Rule | Example | Why |
|---|---|---|
| $a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$ | $2^3 \cdot 2^2 = 2^5 = 32$ | three 2s times two 2s is five 2s |
| $\dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$ | $\dfrac{10^5}{10^2} = 10^3$ | two 10s on the bottom cancel two on top |
| $(a^m)^n = a^{m \cdot n}$ | $(3^2)^3 = 3^6 = 729$ | three copies of $3^2$ is six 3s |
| $(ab)^n = a^n b^n$ | $(2x)^3 = 8x^3$ | each factor gets the power |
| $a^0 = 1$ | $7^0 = 1$ | see below |
| $a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}$ | $2^{-3} = \dfrac{1}{8}$ | see below |

**Why is $a^0 = 1$?** By the division rule, $\dfrac{5^3}{5^3} = 5^{3-3} = 5^0$. But anything divided by itself is 1. So $5^0 = 1$.

**Why is a negative exponent a fraction?** By the same rule, $\dfrac{2^1}{2^4} = 2^{1-4} = 2^{-3}$. Writing it out: $\dfrac{2}{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2} = \dfrac{1}{2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2} = \dfrac{1}{8}$. A negative exponent means "one over" the positive power. It does **not** make the number negative.

> **Example 1.** Simplify $\dfrac{x^7 \cdot x^2}{x^4}$.
>
> Top: $x^{7+2} = x^9$. Then divide: $x^{9-4} = x^5$.

## Roots

A **square root** undoes squaring. $\sqrt{9} = 3$ because $3^2 = 9$. Similarly, the **cube root** $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2$ because $2^3 = 8$.

Roots are also exponents, written as fractions:

$$
\sqrt{a} = a^{1/2}, \qquad \sqrt[3]{a} = a^{1/3}
$$

This fits the rules: $(a^{1/2})^2 = a^{1/2 \cdot 2} = a^1 = a$.

**Useful facts:**
- $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b}$, so $\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25}\sqrt{2} = 5\sqrt{2} \approx 7.07$.
- Square roots of negative numbers aren't ordinary numbers. (Engineers do use them later, as *complex numbers*, but not in this course.)

### Common mistake

**Roots and powers don't split over addition.** $\sqrt{9 + 16}$ is $\sqrt{25} = 5$, **not** $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 7$. Likewise $(a + b)^2$ is **not** $a^2 + b^2$; you'll see what it really is in Lesson 1.4.

## Scientific notation

**Scientific notation** writes a number as (a number from 1 to just under 10) × (a power of 10):

$$
4{,}700 = 4.7 \times 10^3, \qquad 0.00022 = 2.2 \times 10^{-4}
$$

To convert, count how many places the decimal point moves:
- $4700$: the point moves 3 places **left** to get $4.7$, so the power is $+3$.
- $0.00022$: the point moves 4 places **right** to get $2.2$, so the power is $-4$.

Big numbers have positive powers; small numbers (less than 1) have negative powers.

**Multiplying and dividing** is easy: handle the front numbers and the powers of 10 separately, using the exponent rules.

> **Example 2.** $(3 \times 10^4)(2 \times 10^{-6}) = (3 \times 2) \times 10^{4 + (-6)} = 6 \times 10^{-2} = 0.06$

> **Example 3.** $\dfrac{8 \times 10^{9}}{4 \times 10^{3}} = 2 \times 10^{9-3} = 2 \times 10^{6}$

On a calculator, $6 \times 10^{-2}$ is usually shown as `6E-2`. The "E" means "times ten to the."

## Engineering prefixes

Engineers use exponents that are multiples of 3, each with a name. You'll see these on every part and every meter:

| Prefix | Symbol | Multiplier | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| tera | T | $10^{12}$ | 1 THz |
| giga | G | $10^{9}$ | 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi |
| mega | M | $10^{6}$ | 1 MΩ resistor |
| kilo | k | $10^{3}$ | 4.7 kΩ resistor |
| milli | m | $10^{-3}$ | 20 mA LED current |
| micro | µ | $10^{-6}$ | 10 µF capacitor |
| nano | n | $10^{-9}$ | 100 nF capacitor |
| pico | p | $10^{-12}$ | 22 pF capacitor |

To use a prefix, replace it with its multiplier:

> **Example 4.** $4.7\ \text{k}\Omega = 4.7 \times 10^3\ \Omega = 4700\ \Omega$.
>
> **Example 5.** $0.0047\ \text{A} = 4.7 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{A} = 4.7\ \text{mA}$.
>
> **Example 6.** How many µF is 220 nF? $220\ \text{nF} = 220 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{F} = 0.22 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{F} = 0.22\ \mu\text{F}$. (Each prefix step is a factor of 1000.)

### Common mistakes

- **m and M are different.** m (milli) is a thousandth; M (mega) is a million. A factor of a billion apart!
- **Count the zeros carefully.** When converting between neighboring prefixes, you always move the decimal point exactly three places.

## Key takeaways

- $a^m a^n = a^{m+n}$, $\dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$, $(a^m)^n = a^{mn}$, $a^0 = 1$, and $a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}$.
- Roots are fractional exponents: $\sqrt{a} = a^{1/2}$. Roots and powers don't split over addition.
- Scientific notation: a number from 1 to 10 times a power of 10; small numbers get negative powers.
- Engineering prefixes jump by $10^3$: k, M, G up; m, µ, n, p down. Watch m vs M.
