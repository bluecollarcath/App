## Two unknowns need two facts

Suppose 10 people bought tickets: adults pay \$12 and children pay \$8, and the total was \$104. How many adults came? There are two unknowns (adults and children), so one equation isn't enough. We need **two equations**, solved together. That's a **system of equations**.

Systems are everywhere in engineering. When you analyze a circuit, you'll write one equation per loop or junction and solve them together to find every current. Large circuits have dozens of equations (computers do those), but the ideas are exactly what you'll learn here with two.

Let $a$ be the number of adults and $c$ the number of children:

$$
a + c = 10 \qquad 12a + 8c = 104
$$

## Method 1: substitution

1. Solve one equation for one variable.
2. **Substitute** that into the other equation, which leaves just one variable.
3. Solve, then go back for the other variable.

> **Example 1 (tickets).** From the first equation, $c = 10 - a$.
>
> Substitute into the second: $12a + 8(10 - a) = 104$.
>
> Distribute: $12a + 80 - 8a = 104$, so $4a + 80 = 104$, $4a = 24$, and $a = 6$.
>
> Then $c = 10 - 6 = 4$. **Six adults and four children.**
>
> **Check:** $6 + 4 = 10$ ✓ and $12(6) + 8(4) = 72 + 32 = 104$ ✓.

Substitution is easiest when one equation already says "$y = \ldots$" or a variable has no number in front of it.

## Method 2: elimination

Add or subtract the equations so one variable **cancels out**.

> **Example 2.** Solve $x + y = 7$ and $x - y = 1$.
>
> Add the equations: the $+y$ and $-y$ cancel.
>
> $$
> (x + y) + (x - y) = 7 + 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2x = 8 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 4
> $$
>
> Then $4 + y = 7$, so $y = 3$.

If nothing cancels yet, **multiply** one equation (every term!) by a number so that something will.

> **Example 3.** Solve $2x + 3y = 12$ and $x - y = 1$.
>
> Multiply the second equation by 3: $3x - 3y = 3$.
>
> Add it to the first: $(2x + 3y) + (3x - 3y) = 12 + 3$, so $5x = 15$ and $x = 3$.
>
> Back in $x - y = 1$: $3 - y = 1$, so $y = 2$.
>
> **Check** in the first equation: $2(3) + 3(2) = 6 + 6 = 12$ ✓.

## The picture: where two lines cross

Each linear equation is a line (Lesson 2.2). The solution of the system is the point that lies on **both** lines: where they cross.

```plot
x: -1, 8, x
y: -2, 8, y
f: 7 - x | x + y = 7
f: x - 1 | x − y = 1 | dash
mark: 4, 3, solution (4, 3)
hline: 0
vline: 0
caption: The lines $x + y = 7$ (rewritten as $y = 7 - x$) and $x - y = 1$ (as $y = x - 1$) cross at $(4, 3)$, the solution of Example 2.
```

The picture explains the three possible outcomes:
- **One solution:** the lines cross once. (The usual case.)
- **No solution:** the lines are **parallel** (same slope, different intercepts) and never meet. Algebra tells you this by producing something impossible, like $0 = 5$.
- **Infinitely many solutions:** both equations describe the **same line**. Algebra gives something always true, like $0 = 0$.

## An engineering example

> **Example 4.** In a circuit, two branch currents $I_1$ and $I_2$ (in amps) satisfy
>
> $$
> I_1 + I_2 = 5 \qquad 2I_1 - 3I_2 = 0
> $$
>
> The first says the total current is 5 A; the second comes from the voltages in the circuit. From the first, $I_2 = 5 - I_1$. Substitute:
>
> $$
> 2I_1 - 3(5 - I_1) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 2I_1 - 15 + 3I_1 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; 5I_1 = 15 \;\Rightarrow\; I_1 = 3\ \text{A}
> $$
>
> So $I_2 = 2$ A. Different letters, same method.

### Common mistakes

- **Multiplying only part of an equation.** If you multiply an equation by 3, multiply **every** term on **both** sides.
- **Subtracting signs wrong.** When subtracting one equation from another, subtract every term, and put the subtracted equation in parentheses.
- **Stopping halfway.** Find both unknowns, and check both in both equations.

## Key takeaways

- Two unknowns need two equations, solved together.
- **Substitution:** solve one equation for a variable and plug it into the other.
- **Elimination:** add or subtract equations (after multiplying if needed) to cancel a variable.
- Graphically, the solution is where the lines cross: one crossing, none (parallel), or infinitely many (same line).
- Circuit analysis uses exactly this, just with more equations.
