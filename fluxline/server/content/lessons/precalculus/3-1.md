## Adding versus multiplying

Some things change by **adding** the same amount each step: \$100 saved every month. That's **linear** growth: a straight line.

Other things change by **multiplying** by the same factor each step: a population that doubles every hour, money earning 5% interest, a capacitor losing the same fraction of its charge every millisecond. That's **exponential** change, and it behaves very differently.

```plot
x: 0, 7, steps
y: 0, 130, amount
f: 2^x | doubling each step (×2)
f: 10*x | adding 10 each step (+10) | dash
mark: 6, 64, 64
mark: 7, 128, 128
caption: Adding 10 each step stays ahead at first, but doubling catches up and then runs away. That's the signature of exponential growth.
```

## Exponential functions

An exponential function has the variable in the **exponent**:

$$
f(t) = a \cdot b^{t}
$$

- $a$ is the **starting amount** (the value at $t = 0$, since $b^0 = 1$).
- $b$ is the **growth factor**: what you multiply by for each unit of $t$.
  - $b > 1$: growth.
  - $0 < b < 1$: decay (shrinking).

> **Example 1.** A culture starts with 100 bacteria and doubles every hour: $N(t) = 100 \cdot 2^t$.
>
> After 5 hours: $N(5) = 100 \cdot 2^5 = 100 \cdot 32 = 3200$ bacteria.

**Percentage growth.** Growing by $r$ percent means multiplying by $1 + r$ (as a decimal). 5% growth means multiplying by $1.05$; 20% decay means multiplying by $0.80$.

> **Example 2.** \$1000 earns 5% interest per year. After 10 years:
>
> $$
> 1000 \cdot 1.05^{10} \approx 1000 \cdot 1.6289 = 1628.89
> $$

**Half-life.** When something halves every $T$ time units, after time $t$ it has been halved $t/T$ times:

$$
A(t) = A_0 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/T}
$$

> **Example 3.** A medicine dose of 80 mg has a half-life of 6 hours. After 18 hours it has halved $18/6 = 3$ times: $80 \to 40 \to 20 \to 10$ mg. With the formula: $80 \cdot (1/2)^3 = 80/8 = 10$ mg.

## The number $e$

One base shows up so often in nature and engineering that it has its own letter:

$$
e \approx 2.71828
$$

Like $\pi$, its digits go on forever without repeating. It appears whenever something changes at a rate **proportional to how much there is right now**: populations, interest compounded continuously, radioactive decay and, importantly for you, capacitors and inductors charging and discharging. Your calculator has an $e^x$ button (often shared with the **ln** key).

The function $e^x$ is called **the exponential function**. Calculus explains why it's so special: it's the one function whose rate of change equals itself.

## Exponential decay and the time constant

In electronics, decay is usually written

$$
x(t) = x_0\, e^{-t/\tau}
$$

- $x_0$ is the starting value.
- $\tau$ (the Greek letter **tau**) is the **time constant**: it sets how fast the decay happens.

The time constant has a memorable meaning. After **one** time constant ($t = \tau$):

$$
e^{-1} \approx 0.368
$$

so **36.8% remains**: the quantity has fallen by 63.2%. After each further $\tau$, it keeps 36.8% of what was left.

```plot
x: 0, 5, t / τ
y: 0, 1.05, fraction remaining
f: exp(-x) | e^{−t/τ}
mark: 1, 0.368, 36.8% at t = τ
mark: 2, 0.135, 13.5% at 2τ
mark: 5, 0.0067, under 1% at 5τ
caption: Exponential decay. It falls fast at first, then slower and slower. After about five time constants it's essentially zero (less than 1% remains).
```

> **Example 4.** A capacitor charged to 10 V discharges through a resistor with $\tau = 2$ ms. Its voltage is $V(t) = 10\, e^{-t/2}$ (with $t$ in milliseconds).
>
> At $t = 2$ ms (one time constant): $V = 10\, e^{-1} \approx 3.68$ V.
>
> At $t = 10$ ms (five time constants): $V = 10\, e^{-5} \approx 0.067$ V, which is essentially discharged.

You'll meet this exact formula again in Circuit Analysis, where you'll find $\tau$ from the circuit's resistor and capacitor.

### Common mistakes

- **Treating exponential like linear.** Doubling every hour for 5 hours is $\times 32$, not $\times 10$.
- **Order of operations with exponents.** In $100 \cdot 2^5$, do the power first ($32$), then multiply. $(100 \cdot 2)^5$ is something else entirely.
- **Percentages as factors.** 5% growth is $\times 1.05$, not $\times 0.05$ and not $\times 5$.

## Key takeaways

- Linear change adds the same amount; exponential change multiplies by the same factor.
- $f(t) = a \cdot b^t$: $a$ is the start, $b > 1$ grows, $0 < b < 1$ decays. $r\%$ growth means $\times(1 + r)$.
- Half-life: $A_0 (1/2)^{t/T}$.
- $e \approx 2.71828$; decay in circuits is $x_0 e^{-t/\tau}$.
- After one time constant, 36.8% remains; after five, essentially none.
