## The standard second-order form

Every stable second-order system can be written in a universal form that makes its behavior easy to read:

$$
y'' + 2\zeta\omega_0\,y' + \omega_0^2\,y = \omega_0^2\,x(t)
$$

- $\omega_0$, the **natural frequency**: how fast it would oscillate with no damping;
- $\zeta$, the **damping ratio**: underdamped for $\zeta < 1$, critically damped at $\zeta = 1$, overdamped above;
- $Q = \dfrac{1}{2\zeta}$, the **quality factor**: high $Q$ means light damping, a sharp resonance, and many cycles of ringing.

The roots are $s = -\zeta\omega_0 \pm \omega_0\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}$; when underdamped, the oscillation frequency is $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$.

## The series RLC circuit

For a series RLC, the capacitor voltage obeys $LCv_C'' + RCv_C' + v_C = v_s$. Matching the standard form:

$$
\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad \zeta = \frac R2\sqrt{\frac CL}, \qquad Q = \frac1R\sqrt{\frac LC}
$$

> $L = 10$ mH, $C = 1\ \mu$F, $R = 20\ \Omega$: $\omega_0 = 10^4$ rad/s ($f_0 = 1.59$ kHz), $\zeta = 0.1$, $Q = 5$. The circuit rings for roughly $Q$ cycles after a disturbance.

(A parallel RLC has the same $\omega_0$ but $Q = R\sqrt{C/L}$: there, a **larger** resistance means less damping.)

## Resonance

Drive the system with a sinusoid of frequency $\omega$ and look at the steady-state amplitude, using the particular solution from Lesson 2.2. With $r = \omega/\omega_0$:

$$
\left|\frac{Y}{X}\right| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}
$$

```plot
x: 0, 3, ω / ω_0
y: 0, 5.5, amplitude ratio
f: 1/sqrt((1 - x^2)^2 + (0.2*x)^2) | ζ = 0.1 (Q = 5)
f: 1/sqrt((1 - x^2)^2 + (0.6*x)^2) | ζ = 0.3 | dash
f: 1/sqrt((1 - x^2)^2 + (1.414*x)^2) | ζ = 0.707 | dot
caption: Steady-state amplitude of a driven second-order system. Light damping produces a tall resonant peak near $\omega_0$, about $Q$ times the low-frequency response.
```

At $\omega = \omega_0$ the response is exactly $1/(2\zeta) = Q$ times the DC response. In a series RLC driven by a voltage, the capacitor and inductor voltages at resonance are each $Q$ times the source voltage: with $Q = 50$, a 1 V source puts 50 V across the capacitor. This **voltage magnification** must be respected when choosing component ratings.

The **bandwidth** between the half-power (−3 dB) frequencies of a resonant circuit is

$$
B = \frac{\omega_0}{Q} = 2\zeta\omega_0
$$

> In the example, $B = 10^4/5 = 2000$ rad/s.

So $Q$ trades selectivity for speed: a high-$Q$ radio tuner picks one station out of many, but takes many cycles to respond to changes. At $\zeta = 0.707$ (Butterworth) the response is maximally flat with no peak, the usual choice for filters and control loops.

## Resonance in the wild

Resonance can be useful (radio tuning, crystal oscillators, wireless charging, MRI) or destructive (the Tacoma Narrows bridge, mechanical vibration fatigue, power-system oscillations, LC ringing on circuit boards that causes EMI). Engineers add **damping** (resistance, snubbers, shock absorbers, power-system stabilizers) to tame resonances they don't want.

## Key takeaways

- Standard form: $y'' + 2\zeta\omega_0y' + \omega_0^2y$; $Q = 1/(2\zeta)$; $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$.
- Series RLC: $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, $Q = (1/R)\sqrt{L/C}$.
- At resonance the response is magnified $Q$ times; bandwidth $= \omega_0/Q$.
- $\zeta = 0.707$ gives a flat response; damping tames unwanted resonances.
