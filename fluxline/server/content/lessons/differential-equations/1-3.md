## Does a solution exist, and is it the only one?

Before computing anything, an engineer wants to know the model is sound: that a solution exists, and that the initial condition pins it down uniquely. Otherwise the circuit or machine being modeled would have no predictable behavior.

**Existence and uniqueness theorem (Picard–Lindelöf).** For $y' = f(t, y)$ with $y(t_0) = y_0$: if $f$ and $\partial f/\partial y$ are continuous near $(t_0, y_0)$, then exactly one solution exists, at least for a while near $t_0$.

All linear equations with continuous coefficients pass this test, which is why linear circuit models always have unique solutions. Nonlinear equations can fail:

> $y' = \sqrt y$, $y(0) = 0$: both $y = 0$ and $y = t^2/4$ are solutions. Here $\partial f/\partial y = 1/(2\sqrt y)$ blows up at $y = 0$, and uniqueness is lost.

> $y' = y^2$, $y(0) = 1$ has solution $y = 1/(1 - t)$, which **blows up** at $t = 1$: the solution exists only for a finite time. Nonlinear models can do this; real systems instead saturate or break.

## Direction (slope) fields

Even without solving, $y' = f(t, y)$ tells you the slope of the solution through every point. Drawing short line segments with those slopes on a grid gives a **direction field**; solution curves follow the arrows. It's a quick way to see the qualitative behavior of an equation you can't solve, and it's the idea behind numerical methods (Lesson 4.2).

## Equilibria and their stability

For an **autonomous** equation $y' = f(y)$ (no explicit $t$), the **equilibria** are the constant solutions, where $f(y) = 0$. Their stability follows from the sign of $f'(y)$:

- $f'(y^*) < 0$: nearby solutions move back toward $y^*$: **stable**.
- $f'(y^*) > 0$: they move away: **unstable**.

That's linearization (Calculus, Lesson 3.2) applied to dynamics: near the equilibrium, $y' \approx f'(y^*)(y - y^*)$, an exponential with rate $f'(y^*)$.

> **Logistic equation** $y' = y(1 - y)$: equilibria at $y = 0$ ($f' = 1$, unstable) and $y = 1$ ($f' = -1$, stable). Every positive starting value approaches 1.

```plot

y: 0, 1.6, y
f: 1/(1 + 9*exp(-x)) | y(0) = 0.1
f: 1/(1 + exp(-x)) | y(0) = 0.5 | dash
f: 1/(1 - (1/3)*exp(-x)) | y(0) = 1.5 | dot
hline: 1
caption: Solutions of the logistic equation $y' = y(1 - y)$ from three starting values. All converge to the stable equilibrium $y = 1$; the equilibrium at 0 repels.
```

The same reasoning finds the operating points of nonlinear circuits and tells you which are stable: a tunnel-diode or latch circuit can have three equilibria, two stable (the stored 0 and 1) and one unstable in between, which is the origin of metastability (VLSI Design, Lesson 3.1).

## Key takeaways

- If $f$ and $\partial f/\partial y$ are continuous, a unique solution exists locally; linear models always qualify.
- Nonlinear equations can lose uniqueness or blow up in finite time.
- Direction fields show qualitative behavior without solving.
- Equilibria of $y' = f(y)$ are stable when $f'(y^*) < 0$.
