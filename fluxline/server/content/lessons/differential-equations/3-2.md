## Getting back to time

Solving with the Laplace transform leaves an answer like $Y(s) = \dfrac{s + 3}{(s + 1)(s + 2)}$, a ratio of polynomials. To get $y(t)$, split it into simple pieces, each of which appears in the table of Lesson 3.1: **partial fraction expansion** (Calculus, Lesson 2.2). Each piece corresponds to one **pole** of $Y(s)$ and one mode of the response.

First make sure the fraction is **proper** (numerator degree less than denominator degree); if not, divide first.

## Case 1: distinct real poles

$$
\frac{N(s)}{(s - p_1)(s - p_2)\cdots} = \frac{K_1}{s - p_1} + \frac{K_2}{s - p_2} + \cdots, \qquad K_i = \big[(s - p_i)Y(s)\big]_{s = p_i}
$$

The coefficients $K_i$ (the **residues**) come from the cover-up method.

> $$Y = \frac{s + 3}{(s + 1)(s + 2)}: \quad K_1 = \frac{-1 + 3}{-1 + 2} = 2, \quad K_2 = \frac{-2 + 3}{-2 + 1} = -1$$
> $$y(t) = 2e^{-t} - e^{-2t}$$

## Case 2: repeated poles

A pole of multiplicity $m$ needs $m$ terms:

$$
\frac{N(s)}{(s - p)^2} = \frac{K_1}{s - p} + \frac{K_2}{(s - p)^2}, \qquad K_2 = \big[(s - p)^2Y\big]_{s=p}, \quad K_1 = \frac{d}{ds}\big[(s - p)^2Y\big]_{s=p}
$$

and $\dfrac{1}{(s - p)^2} \leftrightarrow te^{pt}$. That $t$ factor is the critically damped response of Lesson 2.1.

> $$Y = \frac{s + 2}{(s + 1)^2} = \frac{1}{s + 1} + \frac{1}{(s + 1)^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad y = (1 + t)e^{-t}$$

## Case 3: complex poles

Complex poles come in conjugate pairs. Rather than complex residues, complete the square and match the damped-sinusoid pairs:

$$
\frac{s + a}{(s + a)^2 + \omega^2} \leftrightarrow e^{-at}\cos\omega t, \qquad \frac{\omega}{(s + a)^2 + \omega^2} \leftrightarrow e^{-at}\sin\omega t
$$

> $$Y = \frac{s + 3}{s^2 + 2s + 5} = \frac{(s + 1) + 2}{(s + 1)^2 + 4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad y = e^{-t}(\cos2t + \sin2t)$$

## Reading the response from the poles

Without inverting anything, the **pole locations** tell you the shape of the response:

- a real pole at $s = -a$: a decaying exponential $e^{-at}$;
- complex poles $-\alpha \pm j\omega$: a damped oscillation at $\omega$ with envelope $e^{-\alpha t}$;
- poles in the right half-plane: growth (instability); poles on the $j\omega$ axis: sustained oscillation;
- the pole nearest the imaginary axis decays slowest and **dominates** the late response.

That's the bridge to transfer functions, poles and zeros in Signals & Systems and Control Systems.

## Key takeaways

- Partial fractions: residues by cover-up for distinct poles; extra $te^{pt}$ terms for repeated poles.
- Complex poles: complete the square and use $e^{-at}\cos\omega t$ and $e^{-at}\sin\omega t$.
- Pole locations dictate the response's form; the slowest pole dominates.
