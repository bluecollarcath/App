## When there's no formula

Most real models (nonlinear circuits, power grids, motors with saturation, anything with measured inputs) have no closed-form solution. Computers solve them **numerically**, stepping forward in time from the initial condition. Every circuit simulator, control-system tool and physics engine does this; knowing how it works tells you when to trust the results.

All methods solve $y' = f(t, y)$ by stepping from $t_n$ to $t_{n+1} = t_n + h$.

## Euler's method

Follow the slope at the current point for one step:

$$
y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,f(t_n, y_n)
$$

It's the direction field of Lesson 1.3, turned into an algorithm. The error per step is $O(h^2)$, and the accumulated (global) error is $O(h)$: halving the step only halves the error.

> $y' = -2y$, $y(0) = 1$, $h = 0.1$: each step multiplies by $1 - 0.2 = 0.8$. After 4 steps ($t = 0.4$): $0.8^4 = 0.4096$, versus the exact $e^{-0.8} = 0.4493$, a 9% error.

## Runge–Kutta

**Fourth-order Runge–Kutta** (RK4) samples the slope four times per step (at the start, twice at the midpoint, and at the end) and takes a weighted average:

$$
y_{n+1} = y_n + \frac h6(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4)
$$

Its global error is $O(h^4)$: halving $h$ cuts the error 16 times. On the example above, RK4 with the same $h = 0.1$ gives 0.44933, matching the exact answer to five digits. Practical solvers (MATLAB's `ode45`, SciPy's `solve_ivp`) also **adapt** the step size, taking small steps where the solution changes quickly and large ones where it's smooth.

## Stability and stiffness

Accuracy isn't the only concern. Apply Euler's method to $y' = \lambda y$: each step multiplies by $1 + h\lambda$. For a decaying solution ($\lambda < 0$), the numerical solution blows up unless

$$
|1 + h\lambda| < 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad h < \frac{2}{|\lambda|}
$$

> A circuit with time constants of 1 s and 1 ms has $\lambda = -1$ and $-1000$. Forward Euler needs $h < 2$ ms to stay stable, even long after the fast mode has died out. Simulating 10 s takes over 5000 steps, nearly all wasted.

A system with widely separated time scales is **stiff**, and stiff problems are the norm in electronics (picosecond transistor dynamics next to millisecond thermal ones). The fix is an **implicit** method, which evaluates the slope at the **new** point:

$$
\text{backward Euler:}\quad y_{n+1} = y_n + h\,f(t_{n+1}, y_{n+1})
$$

For $y' = \lambda y$ each step multiplies by $1/(1 - h\lambda)$, which is stable for any step size when $\lambda < 0$. The price is solving an equation (a linear system, by LU, for linear circuits; Newton's method for nonlinear ones) at every step. **SPICE** uses implicit methods (backward Euler and the trapezoidal rule, or Gear's methods) for exactly this reason, which is also why its transient analysis is built around matrix solves (Linear Algebra, Lesson 1.3).

## Key takeaways

- Euler: $y_{n+1} = y_n + hf$; global error $O(h)$. RK4: four slopes per step, error $O(h^4)$; adaptive steps in practice.
- Explicit methods are unstable unless $h < 2/|\lambda|$ for the fastest mode.
- Stiff systems (widely separated time scales) need implicit methods like backward Euler, which SPICE uses.
