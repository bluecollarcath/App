## When the variables come apart

Many first-order equations aren't linear, but some can still be solved exactly. The most common kind is **separable**: the rate depends on a product of a function of $t$ and a function of $y$,

$$
\frac{dy}{dt} = g(t)\,h(y)
$$

Move everything involving $y$ to one side and integrate both sides:

$$
\int\frac{dy}{h(y)} = \int g(t)\,dt + C
$$

> $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = ty$, $y(0) = 1$: $\int\dfrac{dy}{y} = \int t\,dt$, so $\ln y = \dfrac{t^2}{2} + C$ and $y = e^{t^2/2}$. At $t = 1$, $y = 1.649$.

Any linear equation with constant coefficients and no forcing ($y' = -ay$) is also separable, giving the exponential again.

## Applications

> **Newton's law of cooling.** A component at $T_0 = 90$ °C cools in 20 °C air with $\dfrac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - T_a)$, $k = 0.1$ min⁻¹. Separating: $\dfrac{dT}{T - 20} = -0.1\,dt$, so
> $$T(t) = 20 + 70e^{-0.1t}$$
> After 10 minutes, $T = 20 + 70e^{-1} = 45.8$ °C. Heatsinks, thermal time constants and the "thermal RC" models of power electronics (Lesson 3.1) behave exactly like this.

> **Logistic growth.** $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = ry\left(1 - \dfrac yK\right)$ models anything that grows exponentially until it saturates: populations, epidemics, technology adoption. Separating with partial fractions gives an S-shaped curve that levels off at $K$ (next lesson plots it).

> **A nonlinear circuit.** A capacitor discharging through a device whose current goes as $v^2$ ($C\,dv/dt = -kv^2$) gives $1/v = 1/v_0 + kt/C$: a slower-than-exponential "hyperbolic" decay.

## Exact equations

An equation written as $M(x, y)\,dx + N(x, y)\,dy = 0$ is **exact** if it's the total differential of some function $F(x, y)$, that is, if $M = \partial F/\partial x$ and $N = \partial F/\partial y$. The test:

$$
\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}
$$

Then the solutions are the level curves $F(x, y) = C$.

> $2xy\,dx + (x^2 + 1)\,dy = 0$: $\partial M/\partial y = 2x = \partial N/\partial x$, so it's exact. Integrating $M$ in $x$: $F = x^2y + g(y)$; matching $\partial F/\partial y = x^2 + g'(y) = x^2 + 1$ gives $g = y$. Solution: $x^2y + y = C$, or $y = C/(x^2 + 1)$.

Exact equations are the differential-equation form of conservative fields (Calculus, Lesson 4.3): $F$ plays the role of a potential.

## Key takeaways

- Separable: $dy/dt = g(t)h(y)$ → $\int dy/h = \int g\,dt$.
- Newton cooling $T = T_a + (T_0 - T_a)e^{-kt}$ is a thermal RC circuit.
- Exact equations ($\partial M/\partial y = \partial N/\partial x$) have solutions $F(x, y) = C$.
