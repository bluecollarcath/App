## Every circle needs a force

Lesson 1.4 showed that anything moving in a circle accelerates toward the center at $a_c = v^2/r$. Newton's second law now tells us something must **cause** that: a net force toward the center, called the **centripetal force**:

$$
F_c = m a_c = \frac{m v^2}{r} = m\omega^2 r
$$

"Centripetal force" isn't a new kind of force. It's a job title. Some real force, or combination of forces, does the job:

| Situation | What provides the inward force |
|---|---|
| ball on a string | tension |
| car turning on a flat road | friction between tires and road |
| satellite orbiting Earth | gravity |
| electron in a magnetic field (E&M) | magnetic force |

> **Example 1.** A 0.50 kg ball whirls in a horizontal circle of radius 1.2 m at 4.0 m/s on a string. The tension supplies the centripetal force: $T = \dfrac{0.50 \times 4.0^2}{1.2} = 6.7$ N.

> **Example 2 (how fast can a car take a curve?).** On a flat curve, static friction (the tires aren't sliding sideways) provides the inward force, up to $\mu_s mg$. Setting $\dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \mu_s mg$:
>
> $$v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s g r}$$
>
> For a 50 m curve on dry road ($\mu_s = 0.7$): $v_{\max} = \sqrt{0.7 \times 9.8 \times 50} = 18.5$ m/s (67 km/h). On ice ($\mu_s = 0.1$), only 7 m/s. Mass cancels out, so a heavy truck and a light car have the same limit (ignoring the truck's tendency to tip). Highway curves are **banked** so that part of the normal force points inward and helps.

**There's no "centrifugal force" pushing you out.** When a car turns left, you feel thrown to the right. What's really happening is that your body keeps going straight (inertia) while the car turns underneath you, until the door pushes you inward. From inside the car it *feels* like an outward force, but on a free-body diagram there's only the inward push of the door or seat.

## Apparent weight in circles

> **Example 3 (a Ferris wheel).** A 60 kg rider on a Ferris wheel of radius 10 m moving at 5.0 m/s. The centripetal acceleration is $5^2/10 = 2.5$ m/s².
>
> **At the top**, the center is *below*, so the net force must point down: $mg - N = ma_c$, giving $N = 60(9.8 - 2.5) = 438$ N. The rider feels lighter.
>
> **At the bottom**, the center is *above*: $N - mg = ma_c$, so $N = 60(9.8 + 2.5) = 738$ N. The rider feels heavier.

## Newton's law of universal gravitation

Newton's great insight was that the force pulling an apple down is the same force holding the Moon in its orbit. Every mass attracts every other mass:

$$
F = \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}
$$

where $r$ is the distance between their centers and $G$ is the gravitational constant:

$$
G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2
$$

$G$ is tiny: two 1 kg masses 1 m apart attract with $6.7 \times 10^{-11}$ N, which is why you don't feel pulled toward your furniture. Gravity only becomes noticeable when at least one mass is planet-sized.

It's an **inverse-square law**: double the distance and the force drops to a quarter. You'll meet exactly the same form in E&M: Coulomb's law for electric charges is $F = kq_1q_2/r^2$.

**Where $g$ comes from.** At Earth's surface ($M = 5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg, $R = 6.37 \times 10^6$ m), the force on a mass $m$ is $\dfrac{GMm}{R^2} = mg$, so

$$
g = \frac{GM}{R^2} = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2
$$

Higher up, $g$ is smaller. At the ISS's altitude of 400 km, it's still about 8.7 m/s², nearly 90% of surface gravity.

```plot
y: 0, 10.5, g (m/s²)

```

## Orbits

A satellite in a circular orbit is in free fall: gravity provides exactly the centripetal force needed to curve its path around Earth.

$$
\frac{GMm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}
$$

The satellite's mass cancels: a bolt and a space station at the same radius orbit at the same speed. And the period is $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v}$, so higher orbits are slower and take longer.

| Orbit | Radius (from Earth's center) | Speed | Period |
|---|---|---|---|
| ISS (400 km up) | 6,770 km | 7.67 km/s | 92 minutes |
| GPS satellites | 26,560 km | 3.87 km/s | 12 hours |
| Geostationary | 42,164 km | 3.07 km/s | 1 day |

**Geostationary orbit** is special: the period exactly matches Earth's rotation, so the satellite hovers over one spot on the equator. That's why satellite-TV dishes can be bolted in place. The altitude follows from setting $T$ = one sidereal day (23.93 h) and solving for $r$: about 35,800 km above the surface.

**Why astronauts float.** They aren't beyond gravity (it's 8.7 m/s² up there). They and their station are falling together around Earth at the same rate, so nothing pushes on them: no normal force means no felt weight. It's the same as the elevator in free fall from Lesson 2.1.

**An EE connection:** GPS receivers compute your position from the timing of signals from satellites in those 12-hour orbits, and the system even corrects for the tiny effects of relativity on the satellites' clocks.

### Common mistakes

- **Adding "centripetal force" as an extra force on a free-body diagram.** It's the net inward result of the real forces.
- **Drawing a centrifugal force.** In an inertial frame there isn't one.
- **Using altitude instead of distance from Earth's center** in $GMm/r^2$.
- **Thinking orbiting astronauts have no gravity.** They're in continuous free fall.

## Key takeaways

- Circular motion needs a net inward force $F_c = mv^2/r$; tension, friction, gravity or the normal force provides it. There's no outward "centrifugal" force in a free-body diagram.
- Flat-curve speed limit: $v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s g r}$, independent of mass.
- Universal gravitation: $F = Gm_1m_2/r^2$, $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ N·m²/kg²; inverse-square, like Coulomb's law.
- $g = GM/R^2$ at a planet's surface.
- Circular orbits: $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$, independent of the satellite's mass; higher orbits are slower. Orbiting astronauts are in free fall.
