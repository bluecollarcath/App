## Going in circles

Motors, hard drives, wheels, fans, turbines, centrifuges, satellites: an enormous amount of engineering is about things that move in circles. Circular motion also has a deep link to electrical engineering. A point moving steadily around a circle, viewed from the side, traces out a sine wave, and the AC generators that power the grid make sine-wave voltage by literally spinning a coil. The $\omega$ in $\sin(\omega t)$ is an angular speed.

## Describing rotation

Measure the position of an object on a circle of radius $r$ by its angle $\theta$, in **radians**. (One full turn is $2\pi$ radians; Precalculus covered why radians are the natural unit.) The distance traveled along the circle, the arc length, is

$$
s = r\theta \qquad (\theta \text{ in radians})
$$

**Angular velocity** $\omega$ is how fast the angle changes, in radians per second:

$$
\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}
$$

For steady (uniform) rotation, three ways of saying "how fast it spins" are linked:

$$
\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f
$$

where $T$ is the **period** (seconds per revolution) and $f$ is the **frequency** (revolutions per second, Hz). Motors are often rated in **rpm** (revolutions per minute); divide by 60 to get revolutions per second.

> **Example 1.** A two-pole motor on 60 Hz power spins at about 3600 rpm. What's its angular velocity?
>
> $f = 3600/60 = 60$ rev/s, so $\omega = 2\pi(60) = 377$ rad/s. That's the same 377 rad/s as the grid voltage $\sin(377t)$, because the motor's speed is locked to the AC frequency.

**Linear speed.** Every point on a spinning object has the same $\omega$, but points farther out move faster:

$$
v = \omega r
$$

## Centripetal acceleration

Here's the surprising part. An object moving around a circle at **constant speed** is still **accelerating**, because its velocity keeps changing direction. Velocity is a vector, and a change in direction is a change in velocity.

The velocity always points along the circle (tangent to it). The acceleration points **toward the center** and is called **centripetal** ("center-seeking") acceleration:

$$
a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r
$$

```

**Why toward the center?** Without any acceleration, the object would continue in a straight line along its velocity and drift outward. Constantly bending its path inward requires an inward acceleration, which (Unit 2) requires an inward force: tension in a string, friction on tires, gravity on a satellite.

> **Example 2 (a car on a curve).** A car takes a curve of radius 50 m at 20 m/s (72 km/h).
>
> $a_c = \dfrac{20^2}{50} = 8.0$ m/s², nearly the 9.8 m/s² of gravity. Friction between the tires and road must supply this; on ice, it can't, and the car slides straight on.

> **Example 3 (a hard drive).** A 7200 rpm hard-drive platter has a point 4.5 cm from the center. Find its speed and acceleration.
>
> $\omega = 7200 \times \dfrac{2\pi}{60} = 754$ rad/s.
>
> $v = \omega r = 754 \times 0.045 = 33.9$ m/s (about 122 km/h, under a read head floating nanometers above the surface).
>
> $a_c = \omega^2 r = 754^2 \times 0.045 = 25{,}600$ m/s², about 2600 times gravity. That's why platters must be strong and perfectly balanced.

> **Example 4 (the space station).** The ISS orbits at radius $6.77 \times 10^6$ m (from Earth's center) at 7660 m/s.
>
> $a_c = \dfrac{7660^2}{6.77 \times 10^6} = 8.67$ m/s². The astronauts are accelerating toward Earth at almost $g$; they're falling continuously, but moving sideways so fast that they keep missing. That's what "weightless" orbit really is (Lesson 2.3).

## Circles and sine waves

Place a point on a circle of radius $A$ spinning at $\omega$, starting at angle zero. Its vertical position is

$$
y(t) = A\sin(\omega t)
$$

and its horizontal position is $A\cos(\omega t)$. Uniform circular motion, seen edge-on, is a pure sinusoid. This is exactly why:
- **AC generators** produce sine waves: a coil spinning at $\omega$ in a magnetic field produces a voltage proportional to $\sin(\omega t)$.
- **Phasors** in circuit analysis are drawn as arrows rotating at $\omega$: the real AC voltage is their projection.

### Common mistakes

- **Using degrees in $s = r\theta$ or $v = \omega r$.** These need radians.
- **Mixing up rpm and rad/s.** Multiply rpm by $2\pi/60$ to get rad/s.
- **Thinking constant speed means no acceleration.** In a circle, direction changes, so there's always centripetal acceleration.
- **Pointing centripetal acceleration outward.** It points toward the center. The "outward pull" you feel in a turning car is your body trying to go straight.

```

```quiz
? A car takes a curve of radius 80 m at 25 m/s. What is its centripetal acceleration, in m/s²?
= 7.8125 | 0.003 | m/s²

```

```quiz
? A car wheel of radius 0.30 m rolls at 30 m/s. What is its rotation speed, in rpm?
= 954.9 | 0.003 | rpm
> $\omega = v/r = 100$ rad/s, which is $100 \times 60/(2\pi) \approx 955$ rpm.
```

```quiz
? An object spins in a circle at constant speed. You double its speed without changing the radius. By what factor does its centripetal acceleration change?
= 4 | 0.001 | ×
> $a_c = v^2/r$: doubling $v$ multiplies $a_c$ by 4.
```

```quiz
? A lab centrifuge needs $a_c = 9800$ m/s² (1000 g) at a radius of 0.10 m. How fast must it spin, in rpm?
= 2989 | 0.005 | rpm
> $\omega = \sqrt{a_c/r} = \sqrt{98000} = 313$ rad/s, which is $313 \times 60/(2\pi) \approx 2990$ rpm.
```

```quiz

+ Toward the center of the circle
- Along the velocity, tangent to the circle
- Away from the center
- There is no acceleration at constant speed.
> The velocity's direction keeps turning inward, so the acceleration points toward the center.
```

```quiz
? A point moves around a circle of radius 2 m at $\omega = 3$ rad/s, starting at angle zero. Its height is $y(t) = 2\sin(3t)$. What is the period of that sine wave, in s?
= 2.0944 | 0.003 | s
> $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi/3 \approx 2.09$ s, the time for one revolution.
```

## Key takeaways

- Angular quantities: $s = r\theta$ (radians), $\omega = d\theta/dt = 2\pi/T = 2\pi f$; rpm × $2\pi/60$ = rad/s.
- Linear speed of a point on a rotating object: $v = \omega r$.
- Uniform circular motion has centripetal acceleration toward the center: $a_c = v^2/r = \omega^2 r$.
- Something must provide the inward force: tension, friction, gravity.
- Circular motion seen edge-on is a sine wave, which is why generators make sinusoidal AC and why phasors rotate at $\omega$.
