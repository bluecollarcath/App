## Describing how things move

**Kinematics** is the description of motion: where something is, how fast it's going, and how its speed is changing, without yet asking *why*. (That's Newton's job, in Unit 2.) We'll start in one dimension, motion along a straight line, where the direction is just a plus or minus sign.

## Position, velocity and acceleration

**Position** $x$ is where the object is along the line, measured from a chosen origin, in meters.

**Average velocity** over a time interval is the displacement divided by the time:

$$
v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1}
$$

It can be negative: that just means motion in the $-x$ direction. **Speed** is the magnitude of velocity, always positive.

**Instantaneous velocity** is the velocity at one moment: shrink the time interval toward zero. That's exactly the derivative from Calculus:

$$
v = \frac{dx}{dt}
$$

On a graph of $x$ versus $t$, velocity is the **slope**.

**Acceleration** is how fast velocity changes, in m/s per second (m/s²):

$$
a = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}
$$

On a graph of $v$ versus $t$, acceleration is the slope. Going backward, **displacement is the area under the $v$–$t$ graph**, and change in velocity is the area under the $a$–$t$ graph, the integrals from Calculus.

> **Example 1 (using derivatives).** An object's position is $x(t) = 4t^2 - t^3$ meters. Find its position, velocity and acceleration at $t = 2$ s.
>
> $x(2) = 16 - 8 = 8$ m.
>
> $v = \dfrac{dx}{dt} = 8t - 3t^2$, so $v(2) = 16 - 12 = 4$ m/s.
>
> $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt} = 8 - 6t$, so $a(2) = -4$ m/s². It's moving forward but slowing down.

**Sign sense.** Acceleration and velocity in the same direction means speeding up; opposite directions means slowing down. A negative acceleration doesn't automatically mean "slowing down": a car moving in the $-x$ direction with negative acceleration is speeding up.

## Constant acceleration

Many real situations have (nearly) constant acceleration: a car speeding up steadily, braking, or anything falling near Earth's surface. Integrating $a$ = constant once and twice gives four equations. With initial position $x_0$ and initial velocity $v_0$:

$$
v = v_0 + at
$$

$$
x = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2
$$

$$
v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)
$$

$$
x - x_0 = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v)\,t
$$

Each equation leaves out one quantity. Pick the one that leaves out what you don't know and don't need:

| Equation | Doesn't involve |
|---|---|
| $v = v_0 + at$ | displacement |
| $x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ | final velocity |
| $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x$ | time |
| $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v)t$ | acceleration |

**Only use these when acceleration is constant.** If it varies, go back to derivatives and integrals.

> **Example 2 (a car accelerating).** A car goes from rest to 100 km/h (27.8 m/s) in 8.0 s. Find its acceleration and how far it travels.
>
> $a = \dfrac{v - v_0}{t} = \dfrac{27.8}{8.0} = 3.47$ m/s².
>
> $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(3.47)(64) = 111$ m.

> **Example 3 (braking distance).** A car traveling 30 m/s (108 km/h) brakes at 6.0 m/s². How far does it go before stopping?
>
> We don't know or need the time, so use $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x$ with $v = 0$ and $a = -6.0$:
>
> $0 = 900 - 12\,\Delta x$, so $\Delta x = 75$ m. (Time to stop: $30/6 = 5$ s.)
>
> Notice that stopping distance goes as $v_0^2$: double your speed and you need **four times** the distance. Add a 1.5 s reaction time at 30 m/s (another 45 m before the brakes even engage) and the total is 120 m.

```plot
x: 0, 6, time (s)
y: 0, 35, velocity (m/s)
f: max(30 - 6*x, 0) | braking from 30 m/s at 6 m/s²
caption: Velocity of the braking car. The slope is the acceleration, −6 m/s². The area under the line, a triangle of $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 5\ \text{s} \times 30\ \text{m/s}$, is the stopping distance: 75 m.
```

## Free fall

Near Earth's surface, ignoring air resistance, every object falls with the same constant acceleration, regardless of its mass:

$$
g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2 \text{ (downward)}
$$

Galileo is said to have shown this from the Leaning Tower of Pisa; an Apollo 15 astronaut confirmed it on the Moon by dropping a hammer and a feather, which hit the ground together. On Earth, air resistance slows feathers, but for dense objects over short drops it's small.

Take $+y$ as up, so $a = -g$. Then the constant-acceleration equations apply directly.

> **Example 4 (dropped).** A phone is dropped from rest from a 20 m balcony. How long does it fall, and how fast is it going at the bottom?
>
> $\Delta y = -20 = -\tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$, so $t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 20}{9.8}} = 2.02$ s.
>
> $v = -gt = -19.8$ m/s: 19.8 m/s downward (about 71 km/h).

> **Example 5 (thrown up).** A ball is thrown straight up at 15 m/s. How high does it go, and when does it come back?
>
> At the top, $v = 0$: $0 = 15^2 - 2(9.8)h$, so $h = \dfrac{225}{19.6} = 11.5$ m.
>
> Time to the top: $0 = 15 - 9.8t$, so $t = 1.53$ s. By symmetry it takes the same time to come down: back in the hand at 3.06 s, moving at 15 m/s downward.

```plot
x: 0, 3.2, time (s)
y: -1, 13, height (m)
f: 15*x - 4.9*x^2 | height of the ball (thrown up at 15 m/s)
mark: 1.53, 11.48, top: v = 0 here
caption: Height versus time for the ball, a parabola. Its slope (the velocity) starts at +15 m/s, is zero at the top, and is −15 m/s when it returns. The acceleration is −9.8 m/s² the whole time, including at the top.
```

**A classic trap:** at the top of its flight the ball's velocity is zero, but its acceleration is still $-9.8$ m/s². If acceleration were zero there, the ball would hang in the air forever.

**Where this shows up in EE:** your phone's accelerometer measures exactly these quantities. Integrating its acceleration once gives velocity and twice gives position, which is how inertial navigation works (and why small sensor errors grow quickly: they get integrated twice).

### Common mistakes

- **Using constant-acceleration equations when acceleration changes.**
- **Sign errors with gravity.** Choose a positive direction and stick to it; with $+y$ up, $a = -9.8$ m/s².
- **Thinking acceleration is zero at the top of a throw.** Velocity is zero; acceleration is still $g$ downward.
- **Assuming heavier things fall faster.** Without air resistance, all objects fall at $g$.

## Practice

```quiz
? A car accelerates uniformly from rest to 20 m/s in 5.0 s. What is its acceleration, in m/s²?
= 4 | 0.001 | m/s²
> $a = 20/5.0 = 4.0$ m/s².
```

```quiz
? A cyclist starts from rest and accelerates at 2.0 m/s² for 100 m. What is her final speed, in m/s?
= 20 | 0.001 | m/s
> $v^2 = 0 + 2(2.0)(100) = 400$, so $v = 20$ m/s.
```

```quiz
? A car moving at 25 m/s brakes at 5.0 m/s². How far does it travel while stopping, in m?
= 62.5 | 0.002 | m
> $\Delta x = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2a} = \dfrac{625}{10} = 62.5$ m.
```

```quiz
? A car's braking distance from 20 m/s is 30 m. With the same braking, what would it be from 40 m/s, in m?
= 120 | 0.001 | m
> Stopping distance goes as $v_0^2$: double the speed, four times the distance, 120 m.
```

```quiz
? A stone is dropped from rest off a 45 m cliff. How long does it take to hit the bottom, in s? (Use $g = 9.8$ m/s².)
= 3.03 | 0.003 | s
> $t = \sqrt{2(45)/9.8} = 3.03$ s.
```

```quiz
? A ball is thrown straight up at 15 m/s. What is its maximum height, in m?
= 11.48 | 0.005 | m
> $h = \dfrac{v_0^2}{2g} = \dfrac{225}{19.6} = 11.5$ m.
```

```quiz
? An object's position is $x(t) = 4t^2 - t^3$ m. What is its acceleration at $t = 2$ s, in m/s²?
= -4 | 0.001 | m/s²
> $a = \dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2} = 8 - 6t$, so $a(2) = -4$ m/s².
```

```quiz
? At the very top of a ball's upward flight, which is true?
+ Its velocity is zero and its acceleration is 9.8 m/s² downward.
- Its velocity and acceleration are both zero.
- Its velocity is 9.8 m/s and its acceleration is zero.
- Its acceleration is upward.
> Gravity acts the whole time. The velocity passes through zero at the top while the acceleration stays $g$ downward.
```

## Key takeaways

- Velocity is the slope of $x(t)$: $v = dx/dt$. Acceleration is the slope of $v(t)$: $a = dv/dt$. Displacement is the area under $v(t)$.
- For constant $a$: $v = v_0 + at$, $x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$, $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x$, $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v)t$. Pick the one that skips the unknown you don't need.
- Stopping distance grows as $v^2$.
- Free fall: $a = g = 9.8$ m/s² downward for every object (ignoring air), even at the top of a throw.
