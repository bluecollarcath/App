## The everyday forces

Newton's second law is only as useful as your ability to name the forces. This lesson covers the ones that show up in almost every real problem: the **normal force**, **friction**, and **tension**, and the classic setting where all three appear together, the **inclined plane**.

## The normal force

When an object presses on a surface, the surface pushes back, perpendicular ("normal") to itself. This **normal force** $N$ is a contact force: the surface's atoms get compressed very slightly and push back, like a stiff spring.

The normal force is **whatever it needs to be** to keep the object from going through the surface. On a level floor with nothing else pushing up or down, $N = mg$. But push down on a box and $N$ grows; lift on it and $N$ shrinks; put it on a ramp and $N$ is less than $mg$ (see below).

## Friction

Friction is the force that resists sliding between two surfaces. It acts **along** the surface, opposing the sliding (or attempted sliding). Its size depends on how hard the surfaces are pressed together, which is the normal force.

**Static friction** acts when the surfaces aren't sliding. It adjusts to whatever's needed to prevent motion, up to a maximum:

$$
f_s \le \mu_s N
$$

**Kinetic friction** acts once sliding starts, with a roughly constant size:

$$
f_k = \mu_k N
$$

The $\mu$'s (Greek "mu") are **coefficients of friction**, measured for each pair of materials. Usually $\mu_k < \mu_s$: it's harder to get something moving than to keep it moving.

| Surfaces | $\mu_s$ | $\mu_k$ |
|---|---|---|
| rubber on dry concrete | 1.0 | 0.8 |
| rubber on wet concrete | 0.7 | 0.5 |
| wood on wood | 0.5 | 0.3 |
| steel on steel (dry) | 0.7 | 0.6 |
| ice on ice | 0.1 | 0.03 |

> **Example 1.** A 20 kg crate sits on a floor with $\mu_s = 0.50$ and $\mu_k = 0.40$.
>
> To start it moving, you must exceed $\mu_s N = 0.50 \times 20 \times 9.8 = 98$ N. A 60 N push does nothing: static friction just matches it at 60 N.
>
> Once it's sliding, kinetic friction is $0.40 \times 196 = 78.4$ N. Push with 100 N and $a = \dfrac{100 - 78.4}{20} = 1.08$ m/s².

> **Example 2 (stopping a car).** Car tires on dry road have $\mu \approx 0.7$. The maximum braking deceleration is $\dfrac{\mu mg}{m} = \mu g = 0.7 \times 9.8 = 6.9$ m/s², independent of the car's mass. From 25 m/s, the stopping distance is $\dfrac{25^2}{2 \times 6.9} = 46$ m.
>
> Anti-lock brakes (ABS) keep the tires just short of skidding, so they use **static** friction (higher) rather than kinetic, which shortens the stop. ABS is an embedded control system: wheel-speed sensors, a microcontroller, and valves pulsing the brakes many times a second.

## Tension

A rope, cable or string pulls along its own length with a force called **tension** $T$. For a light (negligible-mass) rope, the tension is the same all along it, and a frictionless pulley just redirects it without changing its size.

> **Example 3 (a classic two-mass system).** A 4.0 kg block on a table ($\mu_k = 0.25$) is tied by a string over a pulley at the table's edge to a 2.0 kg mass hanging below. Find the acceleration and the tension.
>
> **Hanging mass** (down positive): $2.0(9.8) - T = 2.0a$.
>
> **Table block** (toward the pulley positive): $T - 0.25(4.0)(9.8) = 4.0a$.
>
> Add the equations so $T$ cancels: $19.6 - 9.8 = 6.0a$, so $a = 1.63$ m/s². Then $T = 19.6 - 2.0(1.63) = 16.3$ N.
>
> Notice the tension is less than the hanging mass's weight (19.6 N); if it weren't, the hanging mass couldn't accelerate downward.

## Inclined planes

On a ramp tilted at angle $\theta$, tilt your axes too: $x$ along the slope, $y$ perpendicular to it. Then the normal force and friction lie along the axes, and only gravity needs splitting:

$$
\text{along the slope: } mg\sin\theta \qquad \text{into the slope: } mg\cos\theta
$$

```circuit
TEXT 4.15,1.45 "f" anchor=start
TEXT 1.0,3.85 "θ"
caption: Forces on a block resting on a ramp. The normal force is perpendicular to the ramp, friction points up the slope (opposing the tendency to slide down), and weight points straight down. Split the weight into $mg\sin\theta$ down the slope and $mg\cos\theta$ into it.
```

Perpendicular to the slope there's no acceleration, so

$$
N = mg\cos\theta
$$

which is less than $mg$. Along the slope, gravity's component $mg\sin\theta$ pulls the object down, and friction opposes the motion.

> **Example 4 (frictionless ramp).** A cart rolls down a frictionless 30° ramp. Its acceleration is $a = g\sin 30° = 4.9$ m/s², independent of mass.

> **Example 5 (with friction).** A box slides down a 25° ramp with $\mu_k = 0.20$.
>
> $a = g\sin\theta - \mu_k g\cos\theta = 9.8(0.423 - 0.20 \times 0.906) = 2.37$ m/s².

**When does it start to slip?** A block stays put as long as static friction can supply $mg\sin\theta$, which requires $mg\sin\theta \le \mu_s mg\cos\theta$, or

$$
\tan\theta \le \mu_s
$$

With $\mu_s = 0.6$, a block starts sliding at $\tan^{-1}(0.6) = 31°$. Tilting a ramp until something slips is a simple way to measure $\mu_s$.

### Common mistakes

- **Using $\mu_s N$ as the static friction force automatically.** That's the *maximum*; static friction is only as big as needed.
- **Assuming $N = mg$ on a ramp.** On an incline, $N = mg\cos\theta$ (if nothing else pushes perpendicular).
- **Swapping sin and cos on the incline.** At $\theta = 0$ (flat), the along-slope part must be zero: that's $\sin$.
- **Pointing friction the wrong way.** Friction opposes the sliding (or the tendency to slide).

## Key takeaways

- Normal force is perpendicular to the surface and takes whatever value prevents penetration; on a ramp, $N = mg\cos\theta$.
- Static friction: $f_s \le \mu_s N$ (only as big as needed). Kinetic friction: $f_k = \mu_k N$. Usually $\mu_k < \mu_s$.
- A light rope has the same tension throughout; pulleys redirect it.
- On a ramp, use tilted axes: weight splits into $mg\sin\theta$ down the slope and $mg\cos\theta$ into it.
- A block slips when $\tan\theta > \mu_s$. Maximum braking deceleration is $\mu g$.
