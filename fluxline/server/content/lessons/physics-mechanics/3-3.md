## Collisions are hard with forces

When two cars collide, the forces between them are enormous, change wildly from millisecond to millisecond, and are almost impossible to measure. Yet you can predict how fast the wreckage moves afterward with one line of algebra. The tool is **momentum**, and the reason it works is Newton's third law.

## Momentum

The **momentum** of an object is its mass times its velocity:

$$
\vec{p} = m\vec{v}
$$

It's a vector (same direction as the velocity), measured in kg·m/s. A slow truck and a fast bullet can have similar momentum; both are hard to stop.

Newton actually wrote his second law in terms of momentum: the net force equals the rate of change of momentum,

$$
\sum \vec{F} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}
$$

which reduces to $F = ma$ when the mass is constant.

## Impulse

Multiply force by the time it acts and you get the **impulse**, which equals the change in momentum:

$$
\vec{J} = \vec{F}_{\text{avg}}\,\Delta t = \Delta\vec{p}
$$

For a changing force, the impulse is the **area under the force-versus-time graph**.

This explains a lot of safety engineering. To stop a moving person, you must remove a fixed amount of momentum. You can do it with a large force over a short time or a small force over a long time; the impulse is the same, but the force on the body is wildly different.

> **Example 1 (airbags).** A 70 kg driver moving at 15 m/s (54 km/h) must lose $70 \times 15 = 1050$ kg·m/s of momentum.
>
> Hitting a steering wheel, stopping in about 0.01 s: $F = \dfrac{1050}{0.01} = 105{,}000$ N.
>
> An airbag stretches the stop to about 0.1 s: $F = \dfrac{1050}{0.1} = 10{,}500$ N, ten times less.
>
> Crumple zones, bike helmets, padded dashboards and bending your knees when you land all work the same way: **more time, less force**.

> **Example 2 (a baseball).** A 0.145 kg ball arrives at 40 m/s and leaves the bat at 50 m/s in the opposite direction. Taking the outgoing direction as positive:
>
> $\Delta p = 0.145(50 - (-40)) = 13.1$ kg·m/s. Reversing direction counts double.
>
> If contact lasts 1.0 ms, the average force is $\dfrac{13.1}{0.001} = 13{,}000$ N, more than a ton of force for a thousandth of a second.

## Conservation of momentum

During a collision, the two objects push on each other with equal and opposite forces (third law), for the same amount of time. So the impulse one receives is exactly the negative of what the other receives: whatever momentum one gains, the other loses. If no **external** force acts on the pair (or external forces are small compared with the collision forces), the total momentum doesn't change:

$$
m_1\vec{v}_1 + m_2\vec{v}_2 = m_1\vec{v}_1' + m_2\vec{v}_2'
$$

(primes mean "after"). This holds for every collision and explosion, however messy, because the internal forces always cancel in pairs.

> **Example 3 (recoil).** A 4.0 kg rifle fires a 10 g bullet at 400 m/s. Before firing, total momentum is zero, so afterward:
>
> $0 = 0.010(400) + 4.0\,v_{\text{rifle}} \Rightarrow v_{\text{rifle}} = -1.0$ m/s. The rifle kicks back at 1 m/s.
>
> Rockets work on the same principle: they throw exhaust backward at high speed and gain forward momentum, with nothing to push against. That's why rockets work in the vacuum of space.

## Types of collisions

Momentum is always conserved (with no external forces). Kinetic energy may or may not be.

**Perfectly inelastic: they stick together.** The most kinetic energy is lost (to heat, sound, bent metal).

> **Example 4 (a car crash).** A 1200 kg car at 20 m/s rear-ends a stopped 1800 kg car, and they lock together.
>
> $1200(20) + 0 = (3000)v' \Rightarrow v' = 8.0$ m/s.
>
> Kinetic energy: before $\tfrac{1}{2}(1200)(20^2) = 240$ kJ; after $\tfrac{1}{2}(3000)(8^2) = 96$ kJ. 60% of the kinetic energy went into crushing metal. Accident investigators run this calculation backward, from skid marks and final positions, to find pre-crash speeds.

**Elastic: they bounce, and kinetic energy is conserved.** Billiard balls and steel ball bearings come close. For a head-on elastic collision where object 2 starts at rest, conserving both momentum and kinetic energy gives

$$
v_1' = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1 \qquad v_2' = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1
$$

Special cases worth remembering:
- **Equal masses:** they swap velocities. The first stops dead and the second moves off at the first's speed (Newton's cradle; a cue ball hitting another ball head-on).
- **Light hits heavy:** the light one bounces back; the heavy one barely moves (a ball off a wall).
- **Heavy hits light:** the heavy one barely slows; the light one shoots off at up to twice the heavy one's speed.

**Inelastic (in between):** they bounce apart, but some kinetic energy is lost. Most real collisions.

> **Example 5.** A 2.0 kg cart at 4.0 m/s hits a stationary 6.0 kg cart elastically.
>
> $v_1' = \dfrac{2 - 6}{8}(4.0) = -2.0$ m/s (bounces back), and $v_2' = \dfrac{2(2)}{8}(4.0) = 2.0$ m/s.
>
> Check momentum: $2(4) = 8$ before; $2(-2) + 6(2) = 8$ after. ✓

### Common mistakes

- **Dropping the sign of velocity.** Momentum is a vector; an object moving left has negative momentum. A bounce changes momentum by more than a stop does.
- **Assuming kinetic energy is conserved in every collision.** Only in elastic ones; momentum is conserved in all of them.
- **Applying conservation when a big external force acts** during the event (like friction during a long slide afterward).

```quiz
? Why do crumple zones make cars safer?
+ They make the collision last longer, so the same change in momentum needs a smaller force.

- They make the collision perfectly elastic.
- They increase the mass of the car.
> Impulse $F\Delta t = \Delta p$ is fixed; stretching $\Delta t$ lowers $F$ on the occupants.
```

## Key takeaways

- Momentum $\vec{p} = m\vec{v}$ is a vector; $\sum F = dp/dt$.
- Impulse $J = F_{\text{avg}}\Delta t = \Delta p$: the area under $F(t)$. Longer stops mean smaller forces (airbags, crumple zones).
- With no external force, total momentum is conserved in every collision and explosion, because internal forces cancel in third-law pairs.
- Elastic collisions also conserve kinetic energy; perfectly inelastic collisions (sticking together) lose the most.
- Equal masses in a head-on elastic collision swap velocities.
