## Stored energy

Lift a book onto a shelf and you do work on it. That work isn't lost: let the book fall, and it comes back as kinetic energy. While the book sits on the shelf, the energy is **stored** in its position. That's **potential energy**. Likewise, a stretched spring stores energy that it gives back when released, and a charged capacitor stores energy in its electric field (E&M). This lesson turns that idea into the most powerful shortcut in mechanics: **conservation of energy**.

## Gravitational potential energy

Near Earth's surface, raising a mass $m$ by a height $h$ against gravity stores

$$
PE_{\text{grav}} = mgh
$$

Only **changes** in height matter, so you can put $h = 0$ wherever is convenient (the floor, the ground, the bottom of a hill). The answer to any physical question won't depend on that choice.

## Spring potential energy

A spring stretched or compressed by $x$ from its natural length stores exactly the work it took to deform it (Lesson 3.1):

$$
PE_{\text{spring}} = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2
$$

## Conservation of mechanical energy

Gravity and spring forces are **conservative**: the work they do depends only on where you start and end, not the path, and you can always get the energy back. When only conservative forces do work, the total **mechanical energy** (kinetic plus potential) stays constant:

$$
KE_1 + PE_1 = KE_2 + PE_2
$$

Energy just shifts back and forth between motion and storage.

> **Example 1 (a drop).** A 2.0 kg object falls from rest from 20 m. Its speed at the bottom:
>
> $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(20)} = 19.8$ m/s.
>
> Same answer as Lesson 1.2, with no time and no kinematics equations. Mass cancels.


> **Example 2 (a roller coaster).** A coaster starts from rest at the top of a 40 m hill. How fast is it going at the bottom of a later dip, 15 m above the ground (ignoring friction)?
>
> It has dropped $40 - 15 = 25$ m, so $v = \sqrt{2(9.8)(25)} = 22.1$ m/s. The shape of the track in between doesn't matter at all. That's the power of energy methods: try doing this with forces on a curving track.

> **Example 3 (a spring launcher).** A spring with $k = 800$ N/m is compressed 0.10 m and launches a 0.20 kg ball straight up. How high does the ball rise?
>
> Spring energy: $\tfrac{1}{2}(800)(0.10)^2 = 4.0$ J. All of it becomes $mgh$ at the top:
>
> $h = \dfrac{4.0}{0.20 \times 9.8} = 2.0$ m.

## When friction is involved

Friction is **not** conservative: it turns mechanical energy into heat that you can't get back by reversing the motion. Include it as an energy loss:

$$
KE_1 + PE_1 = KE_2 + PE_2 + E_{\text{lost}}
$$

where $E_{\text{lost}}$ (for sliding friction, $f_k d$) becomes thermal energy. Total energy, including heat, is still perfectly conserved; it's just no longer all mechanical. **Energy is never created or destroyed, only converted.** That's the first law of thermodynamics, and it's the basis of every power and efficiency calculation in engineering.

> **Example 4.** A skier starts from rest and descends 50 m of height, but friction and air drag waste 30% of the energy. Speed at the bottom: $\tfrac{1}{2}v^2 = 0.70\,g(50)$, so $v = \sqrt{0.70 \times 2 \times 9.8 \times 50} = 26.2$ m/s.

## Power

**Power** is how fast work is done, or how fast energy is converted:

$$
P = \frac{W}{t} \qquad \text{or, for a force moving at speed } v: \quad P = Fv
$$

Its unit is the **watt**: $1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}$. This is the same watt as in circuits, where $P = VI$. A 1000 W motor and a 1000 W heater convert energy at the same rate.

**The kilowatt-hour.** Utilities sell energy in kilowatt-hours: 1 kW for 1 hour.

$$
1\ \text{kWh} = 1000\ \text{W} \times 3600\ \text{s} = 3.6 \times 10^6\ \text{J}
$$

> **Example 5 (an elevator motor).** An elevator lifts 1000 kg up 20 m in 10 s at steady speed. The useful power is $\dfrac{mgh}{t} = \dfrac{1000 \times 9.8 \times 20}{10} = 19{,}600$ W $\approx 20$ kW.

**Efficiency** is the fraction of input power that comes out as useful power:

$$
\eta = \frac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}}
$$

> **Example 6.** A motor draws 2.0 kW of electrical power and lifts a 100 kg load 6.0 m in 4.0 s. Useful output: $\dfrac{100 \times 9.8 \times 6.0}{4.0} = 1470$ W. Efficiency: $\dfrac{1470}{2000} = 73.5\%$. The other 26.5% becomes heat in the windings and bearings, which is why motors need cooling.

> **Example 7 (storing energy for the grid).** **Pumped hydro** storage pumps water uphill when electricity is cheap and runs it back through turbines when demand is high. Lifting 1000 m³ of water (1,000,000 kg) by 100 m stores $mgh = 9.8 \times 10^8$ J $\approx 272$ kWh, enough to run about nine US homes for a day. It's still the largest form of grid energy storage in the world.

### Common mistakes

- **Using height traveled instead of change in height.** Only the vertical drop matters for $mgh$.
- **Applying conservation of mechanical energy with friction present.** Account for the energy lost to heat.
- **Confusing energy and power.** Energy (J, kWh) is an amount; power (W) is a rate. A battery's capacity is energy; a charger's rating is power.

## Key takeaways

- Potential energy: $mgh$ for gravity (only height changes matter); $\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$ for springs.
- With only conservative forces, $KE + PE$ is constant: compare before and after, ignoring the path.
- Friction converts mechanical energy to heat; total energy is always conserved.
- Power $P = W/t = Fv$ in watts, the same watt as $P = VI$; 1 kWh $= 3.6 \times 10^6$ J.
- Efficiency $\eta = P_{\text{out}}/P_{\text{in}}$; the rest becomes heat.
