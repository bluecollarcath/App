## Real oscillators lose energy

Last lesson's mass on a spring oscillated forever. Real ones don't: a plucked guitar string fades, a car stops bouncing after a bump, a swing slows down unless someone pushes it. Friction and air drag drain energy every cycle. This lesson adds that **damping**, then asks what happens when you keep pushing an oscillator at some frequency, which leads to **resonance**, one of the most useful and most dangerous effects in engineering. And again, the mechanical equations will turn out to be exactly the equations of an **RLC circuit**.

## Damped oscillation

A common model for the drag is a force proportional to velocity, opposing the motion: $F_{\text{drag}} = -bv$, where $b$ is the **damping coefficient**. (A shock absorber, which pushes oil through small holes, works this way.) Newton's second law becomes

$$
m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = 0
$$

How the system behaves depends on how big $b$ is compared with $2\sqrt{km}$.

**Underdamped** (light damping): it still oscillates, but the amplitude shrinks exponentially:

$$
x(t) = A e^{-t/\tau}\cos(\omega_d t) \qquad \tau = \frac{2m}{b}
$$

The **time constant** $\tau$ is how long the amplitude takes to fall to $1/e$ (37%) of its starting value; after $3\tau$ it's down to 5%. The oscillation frequency $\omega_d$ is slightly below the undamped $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$.

**Critically damped**: $b = 2\sqrt{km}$. It returns to rest as fast as possible **without** overshooting.

**Overdamped** (heavy damping): it creeps back slowly without oscillating, like a door closer.

```plot
x: 0, 4, time (s)
y: -1.1, 1.1, displacement
f: exp(-0.6*x)*cos(2*pi*1.5*x) | underdamped: oscillates and fades
f: exp(-0.6*x) | envelope: ± exp(−t/τ) | dot | 2
f: -exp(-0.6*x) | | dot | 2
f: (1 + 9.42*x)*exp(-9.42*x) | critically damped: fastest return, no overshoot | dash
caption: The same oscillator with light damping (it rings down inside the dotted exponential envelope) and with critical damping (it returns to rest quickly without overshooting).
```

**Engineering choices:** car suspensions are tuned slightly underdamped for comfort with control; analog meter needles and door closers near critical; a guitar string very lightly damped so it rings. In circuits, you'll design control systems and filters to be critically damped or slightly underdamped for a fast, clean step response, the same trade-off.

## Driven oscillation and resonance

Now push the oscillator with a periodic force at some driving frequency $\omega$, like pushing a child on a swing. After any start-up wobble dies away, the system oscillates at the **driving** frequency, with an amplitude that depends strongly on how close $\omega$ is to the natural frequency $\omega_0$.

When the drive matches the natural frequency, each push adds energy in step with the motion, and the amplitude builds up to a large peak: **resonance**. The sharpness of that peak is described by the **quality factor**:

$$
Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\Delta\omega}
$$

where $\Delta\omega$ is the **bandwidth**, the width of the peak (measured where the energy has dropped to half its peak, the "half-power" points). For a mechanical oscillator, $Q = \dfrac{\sqrt{km}}{b}$: less damping means a higher, narrower peak. At resonance, the amplitude is about $Q$ times what a steady force of the same size would produce.

```plot
x: 0, 2, driving frequency / natural frequency
y: 0, 16, amplitude (× static response)
f: 1/sqrt((1 - x^2)^2 + (x/2)^2) | Q = 2 (heavy damping)
f: 1/sqrt((1 - x^2)^2 + (x/5)^2) | Q = 5 | dash
f: 1/sqrt((1 - x^2)^2 + (x/15)^2) | Q = 15 (light damping) | 4
caption: Resonance curves. Driving near the natural frequency produces a peak about $Q$ times the low-frequency response. Lighter damping gives a taller, narrower peak: higher $Q$ means more selective.
```

**Resonance can be destructive.** Soldiers break step when crossing bridges; washing machines walk across the floor when an unbalanced load hits the drum's resonant speed; a singer can shatter a wine glass by matching its natural frequency. In 2000, London's Millennium Bridge swayed alarmingly because pedestrians' footsteps fell into step with its sideways resonance, and dampers had to be retrofitted. (The famous 1940 Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse is often blamed on simple resonance; it was actually a more complex wind-driven instability, but the lesson is the same: structures must be checked for their natural frequencies.)

**Resonance is also extremely useful.** Every radio, Wi-Fi chip and phone selects one channel out of many by using a high-$Q$ resonator that responds strongly only near its tuned frequency. Quartz crystals have $Q$ values around 100,000, which is why they make such precise clocks and filters. MRI machines and microwave ovens work at resonances too.

## The RLC circuit: the same equations

Add a resistor to last lesson's LC circuit, and drive it with a voltage source. Kirchhoff's voltage law gives

$$
L\frac{d^2q}{dt^2} + R\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac{1}{C}q = V(t)
$$

Compare it term by term with the damped, driven mass on a spring, $m\ddot{x} + b\dot{x} + kx = F(t)$:

| Mechanical | Electrical (series RLC) |
|---|---|
| mass $m$ | inductance $L$ |
| damping $b$ | resistance $R$ |
| spring constant $k$ | $1/C$ |
| driving force $F(t)$ | source voltage $V(t)$ |
| displacement $x$, velocity $v$ | charge $q$, current $i$ |
| $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ | $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ |
| $Q = \sqrt{km}/b$ | $Q = \dfrac{1}{R}\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{C}}$ |
| friction heats the parts | the resistor dissipates power as heat |

**Resistance plays the role of friction.** It's the only element that turns energy into heat; the inductor and capacitor just store and return it. Every result in this lesson (decay time constants, critical damping, resonance peaks, bandwidth) carries over directly to circuits, and Circuit Analysis II will use exactly these ideas for filters and tuned circuits.

> **Example 1.** A series RLC circuit has $L = 10$ mH, $C = 100$ nF and $R = 10$ Ω.
>
> $f_0 = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} = 5030$ Hz.
>
> $Q = \dfrac{1}{10}\sqrt{\dfrac{0.010}{100 \times 10^{-9}}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{10^5}}{10} = 31.6$.
>
> Bandwidth: $\dfrac{f_0}{Q} = \dfrac{5030}{31.6} = 159$ Hz. The circuit responds strongly only between about 4950 and 5110 Hz.
>
> Raise $R$ to 50 Ω and $Q$ drops to 6.3: a wider, lower peak, less selective, like heavier damping on a spring.

> **Example 2 (a radio).** An FM station occupies about 200 kHz around 100 MHz. A tuned circuit selecting it needs $Q \approx \dfrac{100\ \text{MHz}}{200\ \text{kHz}} = 500$.

### Common mistakes

- **Thinking damping changes the natural frequency a lot.** Light damping barely shifts it; mainly it makes the amplitude decay.
- **Expecting a driven system to oscillate at its own frequency.** Once settled, it follows the driving frequency; only the amplitude depends on how close that is to resonance.
- **Getting $Q$ backward.** High $Q$ means *low* damping (small $b$ or small $R$) and a *narrow* peak.


## Key takeaways

- Damping ($-bv$) makes oscillations decay as $e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = 2m/b$. Underdamped rings; critically damped ($b = 2\sqrt{km}$) returns fastest without overshoot; overdamped creeps.
- Driving near the natural frequency causes resonance: amplitude about $Q$ times larger, with bandwidth $\Delta\omega = \omega_0/Q$.
- High $Q$ = light damping = tall, narrow, selective peak; useful for tuning and clocks, dangerous in structures.
- A series RLC circuit is the same system: $L \leftrightarrow m$, $R \leftrightarrow b$, $1/C \leftrightarrow k$, $V \leftrightarrow F$; $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ and $Q = \tfrac{1}{R}\sqrt{L/C}$.
