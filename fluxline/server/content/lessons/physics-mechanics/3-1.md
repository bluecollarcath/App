## A second way to solve problems

Newton's laws work, but some problems are painful with forces alone: a roller coaster whose track changes angle every meter, or a spring whose force keeps changing as it stretches. **Energy** gives a second, often much easier, route. Instead of tracking forces and accelerations moment by moment, you compare a "before" and an "after."

Energy is also the concept that ties all of engineering together. Electrical energy, mechanical energy, heat and light all trade with each other, measured in the same unit, the joule, and an electric bill is just a count of joules (in kilowatt-hours).

## Work

When a force pushes an object through a displacement, it does **work**:

$$
W = F d \cos\theta
$$

where $F$ is the force, $d$ is the displacement, and $\theta$ is the angle between them. Work is a scalar, measured in **joules**: $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N·m}$.

Only the part of the force **along** the motion does work:
- **Force along the motion** ($\theta = 0$): $W = Fd$, positive. The force adds energy.
- **Force against the motion** ($\theta = 180°$): $W = -Fd$, negative. Friction and braking remove energy.
- **Force perpendicular to the motion** ($\theta = 90°$): $W = 0$. The normal force on a sliding box, or the centripetal force on a circling ball, does no work, because it changes the direction of motion but not the speed.

> **Example 1.** You pull a suitcase 10 m along a level floor with a 50 N force on its handle, angled 30° above horizontal.
>
> $W = 50 \times 10 \times \cos 30° = 433$ J. The vertical part of your pull does no work, because the suitcase doesn't move vertically.

> **Example 2.** You lift a 20 kg box 1.5 m straight up at steady speed. Your force equals its weight, 196 N, upward, along the motion: $W = 196 \times 1.5 = 294$ J. Gravity does $-294$ J on the box at the same time.

**Holding something still does no work** in the physics sense, even though your muscles burn energy: if nothing moves, $d = 0$.

## Work by a changing force

When the force varies with position, work is the **area under the force-versus-position graph**, which is an integral:

$$
W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} F(x)\, dx
$$

The most important example is a **spring**. Stretching a spring a distance $x$ from its natural length takes a force proportional to the stretch (**Hooke's law**):

$$
F = kx
$$

where $k$ is the **spring constant** in N/m (a stiff spring has a large $k$). The work to stretch it from 0 to $x$ is the area of a triangle:

$$
W = \int_0^x kx'\, dx' = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2
$$

Notice the $x^2$: stretching a spring twice as far takes **four times** the work.

## Kinetic energy

A moving object can do work as it slows down: a hammer drives a nail, a bowling ball knocks over pins. That capacity to do work because of motion is **kinetic energy**:

$$
KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2
$$

Kinetic energy depends on the **square** of speed: double the speed and you have four times the energy. That's why highway crashes are so much more destructive than parking-lot bumps, and why stopping distance grows as $v^2$ (Lesson 1.2).

> **Example 3.** A 1200 kg car at 25 m/s (90 km/h) has $KE = \tfrac{1}{2}(1200)(25)^2 = 375{,}000$ J $= 375$ kJ.

## The work-energy theorem

Combine $F = ma$ with the constant-acceleration equation $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2ad$, and multiply by $\tfrac{1}{2}m$: you get the central result of this lesson.

$$
W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2
$$

**The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy.** (It holds for changing forces too, which you can prove with calculus.) Positive net work speeds an object up; negative net work slows it down.

> **Example 4 (finding speed without time).** A 30 N net force pushes a 2.0 kg cart from rest across 4.0 m. How fast is it going?
>
> $W = 30 \times 4.0 = 120$ J $= \tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)v^2$, so $v = \sqrt{120} = 11.0$ m/s.

> **Example 5 (braking).** To stop the 1200 kg car from 25 m/s, the brakes must do $-375$ kJ of work. That energy becomes heat in the brake discs. An **electric vehicle's regenerative braking** instead runs the motor as a generator and pushes much of that energy back into the battery: the same work-energy accounting, but recovering energy instead of wasting it.

> **Example 6 (friction).** A 5.0 kg box slides at 6.0 m/s onto a floor with $\mu_k = 0.30$. How far does it go?
>
> Friction does work $-\mu_k mg\,d$, which must remove all the kinetic energy:
>
> $\mu_k mg\,d = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 \Rightarrow d = \dfrac{v^2}{2\mu_k g} = \dfrac{36}{2(0.30)(9.8)} = 6.1$ m.

### Common mistakes

- **Using the full force when it's at an angle.** Only $F\cos\theta$, the part along the motion, does work.
- **Counting work by perpendicular forces.** The normal force on a level floor and centripetal forces do zero work.
- **Forgetting the square in $KE$ or in the spring work.** Doubling speed quadruples $KE$; doubling stretch quadruples spring work.
- **Using one force's work instead of the net work** in the work-energy theorem.

## Key takeaways

- Work: $W = Fd\cos\theta$, in joules. Only the force component along the motion counts; perpendicular forces do no work.
- For a changing force, $W = \int F\,dx$, the area under the $F$–$x$ graph. Springs: $F = kx$ and $W = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$.
- Kinetic energy: $KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$; doubling speed quadruples it.
- Work-energy theorem: $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE$. It finds speeds and distances without needing time.
