## Newton's second law for rotation

Last lesson built the rotational versions of force (torque) and mass (moment of inertia). Put them together and you get the rotational form of $F = ma$:

$$
\sum \tau = I\alpha
$$

Net torque causes angular acceleration; a bigger moment of inertia means less angular acceleration for the same torque.

> **Example 1 (spinning up a motor).** A small motor produces 0.20 N·m of torque on a rotor with $I = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}$ kg·m². Ignoring friction, how long does it take to reach 3000 rpm?
>
> $\alpha = \dfrac{\tau}{I} = \dfrac{0.20}{4.0 \times 10^{-4}} = 500$ rad/s².
>
> $\omega = 3000 \times \dfrac{2\pi}{60} = 314$ rad/s, so $t = \dfrac{314}{500} = 0.63$ s.
>
> Lighter rotors with less inertia respond faster. That's why servo motors for robots and disk drives are designed with low-inertia rotors.

> **Example 2 (a falling mass on a pulley).** A 1.0 kg mass hangs from a string wrapped around a 2.0 kg solid-disk pulley ($R = 0.10$ m, $I = \tfrac{1}{2}MR^2$).
>
> Mass: $mg - T = ma$. Pulley: $TR = I\alpha = \tfrac{1}{2}MR^2 \cdot \dfrac{a}{R}$, so $T = \tfrac{1}{2}Ma$.
>
> Add them: $mg = (m + \tfrac{1}{2}M)a$, so $a = \dfrac{1.0 \times 9.8}{1.0 + 1.0} = 4.9$ m/s². The pulley's inertia halves the acceleration compared with free fall.

## Rotational kinetic energy

A spinning object has kinetic energy even if it's not going anywhere. Adding up $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ for every piece, with $v = \omega r$:

$$
KE_{\text{rot}} = \tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2
$$

> **Example 3 (a flywheel battery).** A 50 kg solid-disk flywheel of radius 0.30 m spins at 10,000 rpm.
>
> $I = \tfrac{1}{2}(50)(0.30)^2 = 2.25$ kg·m², and $\omega = 1047$ rad/s.
>
> $KE = \tfrac{1}{2}(2.25)(1047)^2 = 1.23 \times 10^6$ J $\approx 0.34$ kWh.
>
> Flywheel energy storage is used in data-center backup power and grid frequency regulation because it can deliver large power almost instantly. The $\omega^2$ means doubling the speed stores four times the energy, so flywheels spin as fast as their materials can survive.

**Rolling.** A wheel or ball rolling without slipping both moves and spins, with $v = \omega R$. Its total kinetic energy is

$$
KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2
$$

So when something rolls down a hill, some of the gravitational energy goes into spinning, and less is left for forward speed. From a 2.0 m drop:

| Object | Speed at the bottom |
|---|---|
| frictionless sliding block | 6.26 m/s |
| solid sphere ($\tfrac{2}{5}MR^2$) | 5.29 m/s |
| solid cylinder ($\tfrac{1}{2}MR^2$) | 5.11 m/s |
| hoop ($MR^2$) | 4.43 m/s |

The more of its mass is at the rim, the more energy goes into spinning and the slower it rolls, regardless of the actual mass or size.

## Power in rotation: P = τω

For a linear force, $P = Fv$. For rotation:

$$
P = \tau\omega
$$

This is the equation behind every motor rating. Torque times angular speed (in rad/s) gives mechanical power in watts.

> **Example 4 (an EV motor).** An electric-car motor delivers 300 N·m at 4000 rpm. $\omega = 419$ rad/s, so $P = 300 \times 419 = 126{,}000$ W $= 126$ kW (about 168 hp).

**The DC motor's torque-speed curve.** A simple permanent-magnet DC motor produces the most torque when stalled ($\omega = 0$) and its torque falls in a straight line to zero at its no-load speed. Since $P = \tau\omega$, its output power is zero at both ends and peaks at **half** the no-load speed.

```plot
x: 0, 320, angular speed ω (rad/s)
y: 0, 2.2
f: 2*(1 - x/314.16) | torque τ (N·m)
f: 2*x*(1 - x/314.16)/100 | output power P = τω (in units of 100 watts) | dash
mark: 157.08, 1.5708, max power: 157 W at half speed
caption: A DC motor with 2 N·m stall torque and 3000 rpm (314 rad/s) no-load speed. Torque falls linearly with speed; power $\tau\omega$ is a parabola that peaks at half the no-load speed.
```

In Electric Machines you'll see why: the motor's spinning coils generate a back-voltage proportional to $\omega$, which reduces the current, and torque is proportional to current. The mechanics here and the circuit there are two halves of the same machine.

## Angular momentum

The rotational version of momentum is **angular momentum**:

$$
L = I\omega
$$

and the rotational version of Newton's second law in momentum form is $\sum\tau = dL/dt$. When there's no net external torque, **angular momentum is conserved**:

$$
I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2
$$

> **Example 5 (a figure skater).** A skater spins at 2.0 rev/s with arms out ($I = 3.0$ kg·m²), then pulls her arms in ($I = 1.0$ kg·m²).
>
> $3.0 \times 2.0 = 1.0 \times \omega_2 \Rightarrow \omega_2 = 6.0$ rev/s. Pulling her mass closer to the axis triples her spin.
>
> Her kinetic energy also triples ($\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2$ goes as $I\omega \cdot \omega$); the extra energy comes from the work her muscles do pulling her arms in.

**Angular momentum keeps things steady.** A spinning object resists changes to its axis direction: that's why a moving bicycle is easy to balance, why a thrown football spirals stably, and how **gyroscopes** hold a fixed orientation. Spacecraft turn themselves using **reaction wheels**: spinning a flywheel one way makes the craft rotate the other way, conserving total angular momentum. Your phone's gyroscope is a tiny vibrating MEMS structure that senses rotation using the same physics.

### Common mistakes

- **Using rpm in $P = \tau\omega$ or $\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2$.** Convert to rad/s first.
- **Forgetting rotational energy for rolling objects.** Rolling things are slower than sliding things from the same height.
- **Assuming kinetic energy is conserved when angular momentum is.** The spinning skater conserves $L$ but gains $KE$.

## Key takeaways

- Rotational second law: $\sum\tau = I\alpha$.
- Rotational kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2$; rolling objects have $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2$ and roll slower than sliding ones.
- Rotational power $P = \tau\omega$ (ω in rad/s). A DC motor's torque falls linearly with speed; its power peaks at half the no-load speed.
- Angular momentum $L = I\omega$ is conserved without external torque: spinning faster when mass moves inward; gyroscopes and reaction wheels.
