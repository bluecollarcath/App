## From describing motion to explaining it

Unit 1 described motion. This unit explains it. In 1687 Isaac Newton published three laws that connect motion to **forces**, pushes and pulls. With them you can predict how anything moves, from a falling phone to a satellite, as long as it's much bigger than an atom and much slower than light. (Quantum mechanics and relativity take over at those extremes; you'll meet the first in Modern Physics.)

## Force

A **force** is a push or a pull, a vector measured in **newtons** (N). One newton is about the weight of a small apple. Forces come from interactions between objects:
- **Contact forces:** a hand pushing, a rope pulling (tension), a floor pushing up (the normal force), friction.
- **Forces at a distance:** gravity, and the electric and magnetic forces you'll study in E&M.

When several forces act on an object, what matters is their vector sum, the **net force** $\sum \vec{F}$.

## Newton's first law: inertia

> An object stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, unless a net force acts on it.

Motion doesn't need a force to keep going; **changes** in motion need a force. This felt wrong for 2000 years, because on Earth things slow down when you stop pushing. But that's friction, a force, doing the slowing. On an air-hockey table, or in space, objects coast.

The tendency to resist changes in motion is **inertia**, and mass is its measure. It's why you lurch forward when a bus brakes: your body keeps going while the bus slows.

## Newton's second law: F = ma

> The net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration.

$$
\sum \vec{F} = m\vec{a}
$$

This is the central equation of mechanics. It's a vector equation, so it holds separately in each direction:

$$
\sum F_x = m a_x \qquad \sum F_y = m a_y
$$

Read it both ways: a net force causes acceleration, and any acceleration you see must have a net force behind it. More mass means less acceleration for the same force. That's what "1 N = 1 kg·m/s²" means: one newton accelerates one kilogram at one meter per second squared.

> **Example 1.** You push a 10 kg box across a frictionless floor with 40 N. Its acceleration is $a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{40}{10} = 4.0$ m/s².

> **Example 2 (working backward).** A 1500 kg car accelerates at 3.47 m/s² (0 to 100 km/h in 8 s). The net force on it is $F = ma = 1500 \times 3.47 = 5200$ N, supplied by friction between the driven tires and the road.

## Weight versus mass

**Mass** ($m$, in kg) is how much matter, how much inertia. It's the same everywhere.

**Weight** ($W$, in N) is the force of gravity on that mass:

$$
W = mg
$$

A 70 kg person weighs $70 \times 9.8 = 686$ N on Earth, and only $70 \times 1.62 = 113$ N on the Moon, but their mass is 70 kg in both places. (Bathroom scales show "kg" by quietly dividing your weight by 9.8.)

## Newton's third law: action and reaction

> When object A pushes on object B, object B pushes back on A with a force of equal size and opposite direction.

Forces always come in pairs, and the two forces in a pair **act on different objects**. When you push on a wall, the wall pushes back on you. When a rocket pushes exhaust gas backward, the gas pushes the rocket forward. When Earth pulls you down with your weight, you pull Earth up just as hard (Earth's huge mass means it barely accelerates).

**A common confusion:** the weight of a book on a table and the normal force from the table are **not** a third-law pair. They're both on the book, and they're equal only because the book isn't accelerating. The third-law partner of the book's weight is the book's gravitational pull on Earth.

## Free-body diagrams

The single most important problem-solving habit in mechanics: **draw a free-body diagram (FBD).** Isolate one object, and draw every force acting **on it** as an arrow starting from the object.

```circuit
BOX 0,0 1.6,1.1 "box"
ARROW 0,0.55 0,2.1
TEXT 0.15,1.75 "W = mg" anchor=start
ARROW 0,-0.55 0,-2.1
TEXT 0.15,-1.75 "N (normal)" anchor=start
ARROW 0.8,0 2.6,0
TEXT 1.7,-0.2 "F (push)"
ARROW -0.8,0 -2.4,0
TEXT -1.7,-0.2 "f (friction)"
LINE -3,0.6 3,0.6 dash
caption: Free-body diagram of a box pushed across a floor. Only forces acting on the box appear: its weight down, the floor's normal force up, the push, and friction opposing the sliding. The floor line is just for reference.
```

**The recipe:**
1. **Pick one object** and draw it as a simple box or dot.
2. **Draw every force on it.** Gravity always; then one contact force for everything touching it (floor, rope, hand, air). Don't include forces the object exerts on other things.
3. **Choose axes.** Usually one axis along the direction of acceleration.
4. **Write $\sum F = ma$ for each axis** and solve.

> **Example 3 (an elevator).** A 70 kg person stands on a scale in an elevator accelerating upward at 2.0 m/s². What does the scale read?
>
> FBD: weight $mg = 686$ N down, scale's normal force $N$ up. Take up as positive:
>
> $N - mg = ma \Rightarrow N = m(g + a) = 70(9.8 + 2.0) = 826$ N.
>
> The scale reads more than the person's weight: they feel heavier as the elevator starts upward. Accelerating downward at 2.0 m/s²: $N = 70(9.8 - 2.0) = 546$ N, lighter. In free fall ($a = g$ downward), $N = 0$: weightless.

> **Example 4 (two blocks).** A 3 kg block and a 5 kg block sit side by side on a frictionless surface. You push the 3 kg block with 24 N, so it pushes the 5 kg block along.
>
> Treat both together: $a = \dfrac{24}{3 + 5} = 3.0$ m/s².
>
> Now isolate the 5 kg block: the only horizontal force on it is the contact force from the 3 kg block, so $F_c = 5 \times 3.0 = 15$ N. By the third law, the 5 kg block pushes back on the 3 kg block with 15 N, which is why the 3 kg block's net force is only $24 - 15 = 9$ N $= 3 \times 3.0$. ✓

**Forces at angles** get split into components, exactly as in Lesson 1.1. A 12 N pull at 30° above the horizontal on a 4 kg sled on frictionless ice gives $F_x = 12\cos 30° = 10.4$ N, so $a = 10.4/4 = 2.6$ m/s².

### Common mistakes

- **Including forces the object exerts.** An FBD shows forces *on* the object only.
- **Putting "ma" on the diagram as a force.** $ma$ is the result of the forces, not another force.
- **Assuming the normal force always equals $mg$.** It equals whatever's needed; in an accelerating elevator, on a ramp, or with an angled push, it's different.
- **Pairing forces that act on the same object as action-reaction.** Third-law pairs act on different objects.

## Practice

```quiz
? A net force of 30 N acts on a 6.0 kg cart. What is its acceleration, in m/s²?
= 5 | 0.001 | m/s²
> $a = 30/6.0 = 5.0$ m/s².
```

```quiz
? What is the weight on Earth of a 2.5 kg laptop, in N? (Use $g = 9.8$ m/s².)
= 24.5 | 0.002 | N
> $W = mg = 2.5 \times 9.8 = 24.5$ N.
```

```quiz
? A 70 kg person rides an elevator accelerating downward at 2.0 m/s². What does a scale under them read, in N?
= 546 | 0.002 | N
> $N = m(g - a) = 70(9.8 - 2.0) = 546$ N.
```

```quiz
? A 2000 kg rocket's engine produces 30,000 N of upward thrust at liftoff. What is its acceleration, in m/s²?
= 5.2 | 0.005 | m/s²
> Net force: $30000 - 2000(9.8) = 10400$ N, so $a = 10400/2000 = 5.2$ m/s² upward.
```

```quiz
? You pull a 4.0 kg sled across frictionless ice with 12 N at 30° above horizontal. What is its acceleration, in m/s²?
= 2.598 | 0.005 | m/s²
> Horizontal component: $12\cos 30° = 10.39$ N, so $a = 10.39/4.0 = 2.60$ m/s².
```

```quiz
? A 5.0 kg lamp hangs at rest from a single cord. What is the tension in the cord, in N?
= 49 | 0.002 | N
> At rest, the net force is zero: $T = mg = 5.0 \times 9.8 = 49$ N.
```

```quiz
? A book rests on a table. What is the third-law partner of the book's weight (Earth pulling the book down)?
+ The book pulling Earth up
- The table pushing the book up
- The book pushing down on the table
- The table pushing down on the floor
> The weight is Earth's pull on the book; its partner is the book's equal pull on Earth. The table's normal force is a different interaction.
```

```quiz
? A hockey puck slides across frictionless ice at a steady 5 m/s. What net force acts on it?
+ Zero
- A forward force of about 5 N keeping it moving
- Its weight
- A backward force slowing it down
> Constant velocity means zero acceleration, so the net force is zero (Newton's first law). Weight and the normal force cancel.
```

## Key takeaways

- First law: without a net force, velocity stays constant. Mass measures inertia.
- Second law: $\sum \vec{F} = m\vec{a}$, applied in each direction separately. 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².
- Weight $W = mg$ is a force; mass is not.
- Third law: forces come in equal, opposite pairs acting on different objects.
- Always draw a free-body diagram of one object, with only the forces acting on it, then write $\sum F = ma$ along each axis.
