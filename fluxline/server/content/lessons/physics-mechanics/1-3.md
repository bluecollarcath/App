## The key idea: x and y don't interact

Throw a ball across a field and it follows a smooth curved arc. That curve looks complicated, but it hides a beautifully simple fact: **horizontal and vertical motion are independent.** Gravity pulls only downward, so it changes the vertical velocity and leaves the horizontal velocity alone.

That means a two-dimensional problem is really two one-dimensional problems you already know how to solve, linked by a shared clock: **time** is the same in both.

A famous demonstration: fire one bullet horizontally and drop another from the same height at the same instant. They hit the ground at the same time. The fired bullet travels far sideways, but its vertical motion is exactly the same free fall as the dropped one.

## Projectile motion

Launch something with speed $v_0$ at an angle $\theta$ above horizontal, and ignore air resistance. Split the launch velocity into components:

$$
v_{0x} = v_0\cos\theta \qquad v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta
$$

Then, with $+y$ up:

| | Horizontal ($x$) | Vertical ($y$) |
|---|---|---|
| acceleration | $0$ | $-g$ |
| velocity | $v_x = v_{0x}$ (constant) | $v_y = v_{0y} - gt$ |
| position | $x = v_{0x}t$ | $y = v_{0y}t - \tfrac{1}{2}gt^2$ |

**The recipe:** use the vertical equations to find the time (how long it's in the air, or when it reaches a height), then plug that time into the horizontal equation.

> **Example 1 (a kicked ball).** A soccer ball is kicked at 20 m/s, 35° above the ground. Find its time of flight, range and maximum height.
>
> Components: $v_{0x} = 20\cos 35° = 16.4$ m/s and $v_{0y} = 20\sin 35° = 11.5$ m/s.
>
> **Time of flight** (back to $y = 0$): $0 = 11.5t - 4.9t^2$, so $t = \dfrac{11.5}{4.9} = 2.34$ s.
>
> **Range:** $x = 16.4 \times 2.34 = 38.4$ m.
>
> **Max height** (where $v_y = 0$): $h = \dfrac{v_{0y}^2}{2g} = \dfrac{11.5^2}{19.6} = 6.7$ m.

> **Example 2 (rolling off a table).** A marble rolls off a 1.2 m high table at 3.0 m/s. How far from the table does it land?
>
> It leaves horizontally, so $v_{0y} = 0$. Vertical: $1.2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(9.8)t^2$ gives $t = 0.495$ s.
>
> Horizontal: $x = 3.0 \times 0.495 = 1.48$ m.

## The shape and the range

Eliminating $t$ from $x$ and $y$ shows the path is a **parabola**. For a projectile that lands at the same height it was launched from, the range is

$$
R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}
$$

The range is greatest at $\theta = 45°$, where $\sin 2\theta = 1$. Angles that add to 90° (like 30° and 60°) give the **same** range: the low one gets there fast and flat; the high one takes longer but goes higher.

```plot
x: 0, 45, horizontal distance (m)
y: 0, 16, height (m)
f: 0.5774*x - 0.016333*x^2 | 30°
f: x - 0.0245*x^2 | 45°: farthest
f: 1.732*x - 0.049*x^2 | 60°
caption: Projectiles launched at 20 m/s at three angles (no air resistance). 45° goes farthest (40.8 m); 30° and 60° land at the same spot (35.3 m), one low and fast, the other high and slow.
```

In real life, air resistance shortens every trajectory and makes the best angle a bit lower than 45°, but the no-air model is the right starting point and works well for dense, slow objects.

## Relative velocity

Velocities depend on who's measuring. If you walk at 1.5 m/s toward the front of a train moving at 30 m/s, you move at 31.5 m/s relative to the ground. In general, velocities add as vectors:

$$
\vec{v}_{\text{object rel. ground}} = \vec{v}_{\text{object rel. medium}} + \vec{v}_{\text{medium rel. ground}}
$$

> **Example 3 (crossing a river).** A boat points straight across an 80 m wide river and moves at 4.0 m/s relative to the water. The current flows at 3.0 m/s.
>
> Velocity relative to the shore: $(3.0,\ 4.0)$ m/s, or $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5.0$ m/s at $\tan^{-1}(3/4) = 36.9°$ downstream of straight across.
>
> Time to cross depends only on the across-river speed: $80/4.0 = 20$ s. In that time the current carries it $3.0 \times 20 = 60$ m downstream.

The same reasoning gives an airplane's ground speed in a crosswind, and it's how GPS receivers separate your motion from the satellites' motion.

### Common mistakes

- **Letting gravity affect the horizontal velocity.** $v_x$ stays constant (without air resistance).
- **Using the full launch speed in one direction.** Always split into $v_0\cos\theta$ and $v_0\sin\theta$ first.
- **Using the range formula when launch and landing heights differ.** It only applies to level ground; otherwise solve for time from the vertical equation.
- **Forgetting that time links the two directions.** Find $t$ from one, use it in the other.

```quiz
= 62.81 | 0.005 | m
> $R = \dfrac{25^2 \sin 100°}{9.8} = \dfrac{625 \times 0.9848}{9.8} = 62.8$ m.
```

```quiz
? A projectile is launched at 20 m/s at 45° on level ground. What is its range, in m?
= 40.82 | 0.003 | m
> $R = \dfrac{400 \times \sin 90°}{9.8} = 40.8$ m.
```

```quiz
? A ball is thrown horizontally at 12 m/s off a 30 m cliff. How far from the base of the cliff does it land, in m?
= 29.69 | 0.005 | m
> Fall time: $t = \sqrt{2(30)/9.8} = 2.47$ s. Horizontal distance: $12 \times 2.47 = 29.7$ m.
```

```quiz
? A marble rolls off a 1.2 m table at 3.0 m/s. How fast is it moving just before it hits the floor, in m/s?
= 5.703 | 0.005 | m/s
> $v_x = 3.0$ m/s and $v_y = gt = 9.8 \times 0.495 = 4.85$ m/s, so the speed is $\sqrt{9 + 23.5} = 5.70$ m/s.
```

```quiz
? A ball is kicked at 25 m/s, 50° above the ground. What is its maximum height, in m?
= 18.71 | 0.005 | m
> $v_{0y} = 25\sin 50° = 19.15$ m/s, and $h = \dfrac{19.15^2}{19.6} = 18.7$ m.
```

```quiz
? Two projectiles are launched at the same speed on level ground, one at 25° and one at 65°. Which lands farther away?
+ They land at the same distance.
- The 25° one
- The 65° one
- It depends on their masses.
> Complementary angles (adding to 90°) have the same $\sin 2\theta$, so the same range.
```

```quiz
? A plane heads due north at 200 km/h through still air, and a wind blows east at 50 km/h. What is its speed relative to the ground, in km/h?
= 206.2 | 0.005 | km/h
> $\sqrt{200^2 + 50^2} = 206$ km/h, about 14° east of north.
```

```quiz
? A bullet is fired horizontally while another is dropped from the same height at the same moment. Which hits the ground first (no air resistance)?
+ They land at the same time.
- The fired bullet
- The dropped bullet
- It depends on the bullet's speed.
> Vertical motion is independent of horizontal motion. Both start with zero vertical velocity and fall identically.
```

## Key takeaways

- Horizontal and vertical motion are independent; they share only time.
- Projectile: $v_x = v_0\cos\theta$ (constant); vertical is free fall with $v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta$ and $a = -g$.
- Find time from the vertical motion, then use it horizontally. The path is a parabola.
- Level ground: $R = v_0^2 \sin 2\theta / g$, maximum at 45°; complementary angles give equal ranges.
- Relative velocities add as vectors.
