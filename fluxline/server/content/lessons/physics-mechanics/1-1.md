## Why an EE needs mechanics

Electrical engineering is built on physics, and physics starts with motion. The ideas in this course come back constantly: a motor turns electrical energy into rotation, a speaker cone is a mass on a spring driven by a force, an accelerometer in your phone is a tiny mass whose motion is measured as a capacitance change, and the equations of a swinging pendulum are the same equations as an LC circuit. Electricity & Magnetism (PHYS 212) builds directly on the forces, energy and fields you learn here.

This first lesson sets up the language: units, and **vectors**, the tool for anything that has both a size and a direction.

## SI units

Physics uses the **SI** (metric) system. Every quantity in this course is built from a few base units:

| Quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| length | meter | m |
| mass | kilogram | kg |
| time | second | s |
| electric current | ampere | A |
| temperature | kelvin | K |

Everything else is derived from these. Speed is m/s; force is the **newton**, $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^2$; energy is the **joule**, $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N·m}$; power is the **watt**, $1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}$. The volt, ohm and farad you use in circuits are SI units too, built from the same base.

**Always convert to SI before calculating.** Mixing km, hours and minutes is the most common source of wrong answers.

> **Example 1.** Convert 100 km/h to m/s.
>
> $100\ \dfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{h}}{3600\ \text{s}} = 27.8\ \text{m/s}$.
>
> Shortcut: divide km/h by 3.6 to get m/s; multiply m/s by 3.6 to get km/h. (In US units, 1 mile = 1609 m, so 65 mph ≈ 29.1 m/s.)

**Significant figures.** Report answers with about as many significant figures as your least precise input, usually two or three. If a cart moves 4.2 m in 1.7 s, its speed is 2.5 m/s, not 2.470588 m/s. Carry extra digits in the middle of a calculation and round only at the end.

## Scalars and vectors

A **scalar** has only a size: mass (2 kg), temperature (300 K), time (5 s), energy (40 J).

A **vector** has a size *and* a direction: displacement (3 m east), velocity (20 m/s north), force (50 N downward). We write vectors in bold or with an arrow, $\vec{A}$, and the size, called the **magnitude**, as $A$ or $|\vec{A}|$.

**Distance vs. displacement.** Run one lap of a 400 m track: you've covered a **distance** of 400 m, but your **displacement** (the change in position, a vector from start to finish) is zero, because you ended where you began.

## Components: breaking a vector into x and y

Any vector in a plane can be split into an $x$-part and a $y$-part, its **components**. If $\vec{A}$ has magnitude $A$ and points at angle $\theta$ above the $+x$ axis:

$$
A_x = A\cos\theta \qquad A_y = A\sin\theta
$$

Going the other way, from components back to magnitude and direction, use Pythagoras and the inverse tangent:

$$
A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2} \qquad \theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{A_y}{A_x}\right)
$$

**Watch the quadrant.** $\tan^{-1}$ on a calculator only returns angles between −90° and +90°. If $A_x$ is negative, add 180° to the result. Sketching the vector first prevents this mistake.

> **Example 2.** A force of 10 N acts at 30° above the horizontal. Find its components.
>
> $F_x = 10\cos 30° = 8.66$ N, and $F_y = 10\sin 30° = 5.00$ N.

> **Example 3 (quadrant check).** A vector has components $(-6, 8)$. Find its magnitude and direction.
>
> $A = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10$. The calculator gives $\tan^{-1}(8/{-6}) = -53.1°$, but the vector points up and to the *left*, so the angle is $-53.1° + 180° = 126.9°$ from the $+x$ axis.

## Adding vectors

To add vectors, **add their components**:

$$
\vec{C} = \vec{A} + \vec{B} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_x = A_x + B_x, \quad C_y = A_y + B_y
$$

Graphically, that's placing the vectors tip to tail; the sum runs from the first tail to the last tip. Never add magnitudes directly unless the vectors point the same way: 3 m east plus 4 m north is 5 m, not 7 m.

> **Example 4 (a delivery drone).** A drone flies 200 m at 40° north of east, then 150 m due north. Where does it end up relative to its start?
>
> Leg 1: $(200\cos 40°,\ 200\sin 40°) = (153.2,\ 128.6)$ m.
>
> Leg 2: $(0,\ 150)$ m.
>
> Sum: $(153.2,\ 278.6)$ m. Magnitude $\sqrt{153.2^2 + 278.6^2} = 318$ m, at $\tan^{-1}(278.6/153.2) = 61.2°$ north of east.

Subtracting is the same with minus signs: $\vec{A} - \vec{B}$ has components $(A_x - B_x,\ A_y - B_y)$. Multiplying a vector by a number scales its magnitude (and flips its direction if the number is negative).

**Unit-vector notation.** Engineers often write vectors with $\hat{i}$ and $\hat{j}$, unit vectors (length 1) along $x$ and $y$: $\vec{A} = 3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j}$ means $A_x = 3$, $A_y = 4$. In three dimensions add $\hat{k}$ along $z$.

**You'll see this again in circuits.** In AC circuit analysis, sinusoidal voltages are represented as rotating vectors called **phasors**, and you add them by components exactly like this.

### Common mistakes

- **Adding magnitudes of vectors that point different ways.** Always add components.
- **Calculator in radian mode** when angles are in degrees (or the reverse).
- **Wrong quadrant from $\tan^{-1}$.** If $A_x < 0$, add 180°.
- **Mixing units.** Convert to meters, seconds and kilograms first.

## Key takeaways

- Work in SI units: meters, kilograms, seconds; newton = kg·m/s², joule = N·m, watt = J/s. Divide km/h by 3.6 for m/s.
- Scalars have size only; vectors have size and direction. Displacement is a vector; distance is a scalar.
- Components: $A_x = A\cos\theta$, $A_y = A\sin\theta$; back again with $A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}$ and $\tan^{-1}(A_y/A_x)$, checking the quadrant.
- Add vectors by adding components, never by adding magnitudes (unless they're parallel).
- The same component method returns as phasors in AC circuits.
