## Things that swing back and forth

Pluck a guitar string, tap a tuning fork, bump a car's suspension, or let a pendulum go: each one oscillates back and forth at its own natural frequency. The simplest and most important kind of oscillation is **simple harmonic motion** (SHM), and it's everywhere in electrical engineering too. The quartz crystal that keeps time in every computer and phone is a tiny mechanical oscillator, and the LC circuits in radios obey exactly the same equation as a mass on a spring.

## The mass-spring system

Attach a mass $m$ to a spring with constant $k$ on a frictionless surface. Pull it to the side and let go. The spring's force always pulls back toward the resting position, in proportion to the displacement:

$$
F = -kx
$$

(The minus sign means "back toward $x = 0$.") This is a **restoring force**. Newton's second law gives

$$
m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -kx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\frac{k}{m}\,x
$$

A function whose second derivative is a negative constant times itself: that's a sine or cosine, as you saw in Differential Equations. The solution is

$$
x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}
$$

where $A$ is the **amplitude** (the maximum displacement) and $\varphi$ is the phase, both set by how you start it. The period and frequency are

$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \qquad f = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}
$$

**Two remarkable facts:**
- **The frequency doesn't depend on the amplitude.** Pull it farther and it moves faster, but takes exactly the same time per cycle. That's what makes oscillators good clocks.
- A stiffer spring (bigger $k$) means faster oscillation; a heavier mass (bigger $m$) means slower.

> **Example 1.** A 0.50 kg mass on a spring with $k = 200$ N/m.
>
> $\omega = \sqrt{200/0.50} = 20$ rad/s, so $f = \dfrac{20}{2\pi} = 3.18$ Hz and $T = 0.314$ s.

## Velocity and acceleration

Differentiate $x = A\cos(\omega t)$:

$$
v = -A\omega\sin(\omega t) \qquad a = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t) = -\omega^2 x
$$


- **Maximum speed** $v_{\max} = A\omega$, as the mass passes through the center.
- **Maximum acceleration** $a_{\max} = A\omega^2$, at the turning points, where the spring is stretched most.

With $A = 0.05$ m and $\omega = 20$ rad/s: $v_{\max} = 1.0$ m/s and $a_{\max} = 20$ m/s².

## Energy in SHM

As the mass oscillates, energy sloshes between the spring and the motion:
- At the ends: all spring energy, $\tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$; the mass is momentarily still.
- At the center: all kinetic energy, $\tfrac{1}{2}mv_{\max}^2$; the spring is relaxed.

Without friction, the total stays constant:

$$
E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}mv_{\max}^2
$$

For the example: $E = \tfrac{1}{2}(200)(0.05)^2 = 0.25$ J.

## The pendulum

A mass on a string of length $L$, swinging through small angles (under about 15°), also undergoes SHM. Gravity's component along the arc is the restoring force, and

$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}
$$

The period depends only on the length and $g$: not on the mass, and (for small swings) not on the amplitude. A 1.0 m pendulum has $T = 2\pi\sqrt{1.0/9.8} = 2.0$ s. That's why pendulums kept the world's time for nearly 300 years.

## The LC circuit: the same equation

Here's the payoff for electrical engineers. In an LC circuit, an inductor $L$ and a capacitor $C$ are connected in a loop. Charge $q$ sloshes back and forth between the capacitor plates, through the inductor. Kirchhoff's voltage law gives

$$
L\frac{d^2q}{dt^2} = -\frac{1}{C}\,q
$$

That's exactly the mass-spring equation, with these substitutions:

| Mass on a spring | LC circuit |
|---|---|
| displacement $x$ | charge $q$ |
| velocity $v$ | current $i$ |
| mass $m$ (inertia) | inductance $L$ (resists changes in current) |
| spring constant $k$ | $1/C$ (inverse capacitance) |
| spring energy $\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$ | capacitor energy $\tfrac{q^2}{2C}$ |
| kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ | inductor energy $\tfrac{1}{2}Li^2$ |
| $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ | $\omega = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}$ |

Energy sloshes between the capacitor's electric field and the inductor's magnetic field, exactly as it sloshes between a spring and a moving mass. Everything you learn about one transfers to the other.

> **Example 2.** An LC circuit with $L = 10$ mH and $C = 100$ nF resonates at
>
> $f = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(0.010)(100 \times 10^{-9})}} = 5030$ Hz.

**Quartz crystals bridge both worlds.** A quartz crystal is a mechanical oscillator, a tiny vibrating slab, but quartz is **piezoelectric**: squeezing it makes a voltage, and a voltage squeezes it. So the crystal acts in a circuit like an extremely precise LC resonator. The 32,768 Hz crystal in a watch or a microcontroller's real-time clock ($2^{15}$ Hz, easy to divide down to exactly one tick per second) keeps time to within seconds per month.

### Common mistakes

- **Mixing $f$ and $\omega$.** $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ is in rad/s; divide by $2\pi$ for hertz.
- **Thinking a bigger amplitude changes the period.** For SHM, it doesn't.
- **Putting mass in the pendulum formula.** $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ has no mass in it.
- **Mixing up which energy is where.** Kinetic is maximum at the center; spring energy is maximum at the ends.

## Key takeaways

- A restoring force $F = -kx$ gives SHM: $x = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$ with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$.
- The frequency doesn't depend on the amplitude.
- $v_{\max} = A\omega$ at the center; $a_{\max} = A\omega^2$ at the ends; energy $E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2$ is constant.
- Small-angle pendulum: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, independent of mass.
- An LC circuit obeys the same equation: $L \leftrightarrow m$, $1/C \leftrightarrow k$, $q \leftrightarrow x$, $i \leftrightarrow v$, and $\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}$.
