## Spinning things

So far, objects have been treated as points that slide or fly. Real objects also **rotate**: wheels, motor shafts, turbines, hard-drive platters, doors on hinges. Rotation has its own versions of position, velocity, acceleration, force and mass, and once you see the pattern, everything from Units 1 to 3 carries straight over.

This unit matters a lot for EEs: electric motors and generators are rotating machines, rated by **torque** and **speed**, and Electric Machines & Drives builds directly on it.

## Rotational kinematics

For a rigid object spinning about a fixed axis, every point turns through the same angle. From Lesson 1.4:
- **angular position** $\theta$ (radians),
- **angular velocity** $\omega = d\theta/dt$ (rad/s),

and now:
- **angular acceleration** $\alpha = d\omega/dt$ (rad/s²).

With constant angular acceleration, the equations are exact copies of the linear ones, with $\theta$ for $x$, $\omega$ for $v$ and $\alpha$ for $a$:

| Linear | Rotational |
|---|---|
| $v = v_0 + at$ | $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$ |
| $x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$ | $\theta = \theta_0 + \omega_0t + \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^2$ |
| $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x$ | $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha\Delta\theta$ |

A point at radius $r$ has linear speed $v = \omega r$ and tangential acceleration $a_t = \alpha r$.

> **Example 1.** A grinding wheel spins up from rest to 300 rpm in 5.0 s. Find its angular acceleration and how many turns it makes.
>
> $\omega = 300 \times \dfrac{2\pi}{60} = 31.4$ rad/s, so $\alpha = \dfrac{31.4}{5.0} = 6.28$ rad/s².
>
> $\Delta\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha t^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(6.28)(25) = 78.5$ rad $= 12.5$ revolutions.

## Torque: the rotational force

Push on a door near its hinges and it barely moves; push at the handle and it swings easily. What matters for rotation isn't just the force, but **where** and **in which direction** it's applied. That combination is **torque** $\tau$ (Greek "tau"):

$$
\tau = rF\sin\theta
$$

where $r$ is the distance from the axis to where the force acts (the **lever arm** distance), $F$ is the force, and $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{F}$. Torque is measured in **N·m** (newton-meters).

```circuit

TEXT -0.25,0.1 "axis" anchor=end
caption: A force applied at distance $r$ from the axis, at angle $\theta$ to the lever. Only the part of the force perpendicular to the lever, $F\sin\theta$, makes it turn.
```

- Maximum torque comes from pushing **perpendicular** to the lever ($\theta = 90°$): $\tau = rF$.
- Pushing straight along the lever toward or away from the axis ($\theta = 0$) gives **no** torque. Try opening a door by pushing toward the hinges.
- Doubling the distance doubles the torque. That's why wrenches have long handles.

By convention, counterclockwise torques are positive and clockwise torques negative.

> **Example 2.** A mechanic pulls on a 0.25 m wrench with 40 N. Perpendicular to the handle: $\tau = 0.25 \times 40 = 10$ N·m. At 60° to the handle: $\tau = 0.25 \times 40 \times \sin 60° = 8.7$ N·m.

**Motors are rated by torque.** A small hobby motor might produce 0.05 N·m; an electric car's motor, several hundred N·m. Electric motors produce strong torque right from zero speed, which is why EVs accelerate so quickly from a stop.

## Equilibrium

An object at rest stays at rest only if **both** the net force and the net torque are zero:

$$
\sum \vec{F} = 0 \qquad \sum \tau = 0
$$

> **Example 3 (a seesaw).** A 30 kg child sits 2.0 m from the pivot. Where must a 40 kg child sit on the other side to balance?
>
> Torques must cancel: $30g(2.0) = 40g(d)$, so $d = 1.5$ m. The heavier child sits closer.

Static equilibrium is how engineers size beams, brackets, cable trays, and the mounts for heavy transformers and antennas.

## Moment of inertia: rotational mass

In $F = ma$, mass measures resistance to changes in motion. The rotational version is the **moment of inertia** $I$, which measures resistance to changes in rotation. It depends not just on the mass but on **how far it is from the axis**:

$$
I = \sum m_i r_i^2
$$

Mass far from the axis counts much more (the $r^2$). For common shapes:

| Shape (axis) | Moment of inertia |
|---|---|
| point mass at distance $r$ | $mr^2$ |
| thin hoop or ring (center) | $MR^2$ |
| solid disk or cylinder (center) | $\tfrac{1}{2}MR^2$ |
| solid sphere (center) | $\tfrac{2}{5}MR^2$ |
| thin rod, length $L$ (center) | $\tfrac{1}{12}ML^2$ |
| thin rod, length $L$ (one end) | $\tfrac{1}{3}ML^2$ |

> **Example 4.** A 2.0 kg solid disk has radius 0.10 m: $I = \tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)(0.10)^2 = 0.010$ kg·m². A 3.0 kg rod 1.0 m long is four times harder to spin about one end ($\tfrac{1}{3}ML^2 = 1.0$ kg·m²) than about its center ($\tfrac{1}{12}ML^2 = 0.25$ kg·m²).

A hoop and a disk of the same mass and radius behave differently: the hoop's mass is all at the rim, so it's twice as hard to spin up. **Flywheels** for energy storage put their mass at the rim for exactly this reason: more stored energy for the same weight (next lesson).

### Common mistakes

- **Using degrees in rotational kinematics.** $\theta$, $\omega$ and $\alpha$ must be in radians.
- **Using the full force for torque when it's at an angle.** Only $F\sin\theta$ counts.
- **Treating moment of inertia like mass.** It depends on the axis and on where the mass is.
- **Forgetting torque balance.** Zero net force isn't enough for equilibrium; the torques must cancel too.


```quiz
? You push perpendicular to a door 0.90 m from its hinges with 20 N. What torque do you apply, in N·m?

```

```quiz

= 8.66 | 0.003 | N·m
> $\tau = 0.25 \times 40 \times \sin 60° = 8.66$ N·m.
```

```quiz
? A 30 kg child sits 2.0 m from a seesaw's pivot. How far from the pivot must a 40 kg child sit to balance, in m?
= 1.5 | 0.001 | m
> $30(2.0) = 40d$, so $d = 1.5$ m.
```

```quiz
? What is the moment of inertia of a 2.0 kg solid disk of radius 0.10 m about its center, in kg·m²?
= 0.01 | 0.001 | kg·m²
> $I = \tfrac{1}{2}MR^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)(0.01) = 0.010$ kg·m².
```

```quiz
? A hoop and a solid disk have the same mass and radius. Which is harder to start spinning?
+ The hoop, because all its mass is at the rim.
- The disk, because it's solid.
- They're equally hard.
- It depends on how fast you spin them.
> $I_{\text{hoop}} = MR^2$ is twice $I_{\text{disk}} = \tfrac{1}{2}MR^2$: mass far from the axis counts more.
```

```quiz

+ It doesn't rotate, because the force has no lever arm (θ = 0).
- It rotates quickly.
- It rotates slowly.
- It rotates in the opposite direction.
> A force along the line through the axis gives $\tau = rF\sin 0° = 0$.
```

## Key takeaways

- Rotational kinematics mirrors linear: $\theta$, $\omega$, $\alpha$ in radians; $\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t$, and so on. A point at radius $r$ has $v = \omega r$, $a_t = \alpha r$.
- Torque $\tau = rF\sin\theta$ (N·m): bigger with more force, a longer lever, and a perpendicular push.
- Equilibrium needs both $\sum F = 0$ and $\sum \tau = 0$.
- Moment of inertia $I = \sum mr^2$ is rotational mass; mass far from the axis counts more. Disk $\tfrac{1}{2}MR^2$, hoop $MR^2$, rod about center $\tfrac{1}{12}ML^2$.
