## From bits to waveforms

A digital transmitter sends a stream of **symbols** at a rate $R_s$ symbols per second (baud), each one a pulse $p(t)$ scaled by a value $a_k$:

$$
s(t) = \sum_k a_k\,p(t - kT_s), \qquad T_s = \frac{1}{R_s}
$$

The pulse shape decides two things that pull against each other: how much **bandwidth** the signal occupies, and whether neighboring symbols interfere with one another.

## Intersymbol interference

A rectangular pulse is easy to generate but has a $\text{sinc}$-shaped spectrum that spreads far beyond $R_s$, wasting bandwidth and interfering with neighboring channels. Filtering it to save bandwidth smears each pulse in time, so it spills into the neighboring symbol slots: **intersymbol interference** (ISI). The receiver, sampling once per symbol, then sees each value contaminated by its neighbors' tails.

## The Nyquist criterion

ISI can be avoided **at the sampling instants** even though the pulses overlap. The condition: the pulse must be zero at every other symbol's sampling time,

$$
p(kT_s) = \begin{cases} 1 & k = 0 \\ 0 & k \ne 0 \end{cases}
$$

The narrowest-band pulse that does this is the $\text{sinc}$ pulse, $p(t) = \text{sinc}(t/T_s)$, whose spectrum is a perfect rectangle of width $R_s/2$. That's the **Nyquist bandwidth**, the absolute minimum baseband bandwidth for $R_s$ symbols per second:

$$
B_{min} = \frac{R_s}{2}
$$

```plot
caption: Three sinc pulses one symbol apart. They overlap heavily, yet at each sampling instant ($t/T_s = 0, 1, 2$) only one pulse is nonzero: zero ISI.
```

## Raised-cosine pulses

The ideal sinc is impractical: its brick-wall spectrum can't be built, and its tails decay so slowly that a tiny timing error causes large ISI. The **raised-cosine** family keeps the zero crossings but rolls the spectrum off smoothly, with a **roll-off factor** $\alpha$ between 0 and 1:

$$
B = \frac{(1 + \alpha)R_s}{2} \quad\text{(baseband)}, \qquad B_{RF} = (1 + \alpha)R_s \quad\text{(passband, double-sided)}
$$

Larger $\alpha$ means faster-decaying tails (more tolerance of timing error) but more bandwidth. Typical values: 0.2–0.35 in cellular and cable systems. In practice the filtering is split equally as a **root-raised-cosine** filter in the transmitter and another in the receiver; together they give the raised-cosine response, and the receive half is also the matched filter (Lesson 2.3).

> **Example.** 10 Mbaud with $\alpha = 0.25$: the baseband bandwidth is $1.25 \times 10/2 = 6.25$ MHz, and the modulated RF signal occupies 12.5 MHz.

## The eye diagram

Overlay many symbol-length slices of the received waveform on an oscilloscope triggered by the symbol clock, and you get an **eye diagram**. It shows signal quality at a glance:

- the **vertical opening** is the noise margin at the best sampling time;
- the **horizontal opening** is the tolerance to timing error;
- a closing eye means ISI or noise is eating the margin, and a closed eye means errors.

Eye diagrams and eye masks are the standard pass/fail test for serial links such as USB, PCIe and Ethernet, and for every high-speed PCB design.

## Key takeaways

- Filtering pulses causes ISI unless the pulse is zero at every other symbol's sampling instant (Nyquist criterion).
- Minimum bandwidth is $R_s/2$ (sinc pulse); raised cosine needs $(1 + \alpha)R_s/2$ baseband, $(1 + \alpha)R_s$ at RF.
- Root-raised-cosine filters are split between transmitter and receiver.
- Eye diagrams show noise and timing margins.
