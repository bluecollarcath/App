## The problem with fast data over radio

A radio signal reaches the receiver along many paths: directly, and reflected off buildings, walls and hills. Each copy arrives with a different delay. The spread of those delays, the **delay spread** $\tau_{rms}$, is 50 ns indoors and up to several microseconds in cities.

That's harmless when symbols are much longer than the delay spread. But at 100 Mb/s a single-carrier symbol might last 10 ns, so echoes smear each symbol across dozens of following ones: severe **intersymbol interference**. Equivalently, in the frequency domain, multipath makes the channel's frequency response bumpy, with deep notches (**frequency-selective fading**). Undoing that with one big equalizer is expensive.

## OFDM: many slow carriers instead of one fast one

**Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing** splits the data across $N$ narrow **subcarriers**, each carrying a slow stream of QAM symbols.

- Each subcarrier's symbol lasts $T_u = N$ times longer, much longer than the delay spread, so ISI becomes minor.
- Each subcarrier is so narrow that the channel looks **flat** across it: equalization is one complex multiplication per subcarrier.
- Spacing the subcarriers at exactly $\Delta f = 1/T_u$ makes them **orthogonal**: their spectra overlap, but at each subcarrier's center all the others pass through zero (the frequency-domain twin of the Nyquist zero-ISI pulse, Lesson 2.1).
- The whole bank of modulators is just an **inverse FFT** in the transmitter, and the demodulators are an **FFT** in the receiver, so OFDM is cheap to build.

**Cyclic prefix.** Before each OFDM symbol, the transmitter copies the last part of the symbol to its front. As long as the prefix is longer than the channel's delay spread, all echoes of the previous symbol die out within it, and the receiver discards it. The cost is overhead: the prefix carries no new data.

> **Example: Wi-Fi (802.11a/g/n).** 20 MHz channel, 64-point FFT: $\Delta f = 20\ \text{MHz}/64 = 312.5$ kHz, so $T_u = 3.2\ \mu$s. Cyclic prefix 0.8 µs, total symbol 4.0 µs, so 20% overhead. 48 of the subcarriers carry data, 4 carry pilots for tracking the channel, and the rest are guards.

> **LTE and 5G NR** use 15 kHz spacing ($T_u = 66.7\ \mu$s) in LTE, and in 5G a family of spacings (15 × $2^\mu$ kHz) so shorter symbols can serve fast-moving users and millimeter-wave bands.

The price of OFDM: a sum of many independent subcarriers has a high **peak-to-average power ratio**, so the transmitter's power amplifier must stay linear with a lot of headroom, which costs efficiency. LTE uplink uses a variant (SC-FDMA) to soften this for phone batteries.

## MIMO: using space as well

With multiple antennas at both ends, multipath stops being an enemy and becomes a resource.

- **Diversity:** with several receive antennas, it's unlikely that all of them are in a fade at once. Combining them makes the link far more reliable.
- **Beamforming:** adjusting the phase of each transmit antenna steers energy toward the user; essential at 5G millimeter-wave frequencies, where path loss is high.
- **Spatial multiplexing:** with $N_t$ transmit and $N_r$ receive antennas in a rich multipath environment, the channel supports up to $\min(N_t, N_r)$ independent data streams **in the same bandwidth at the same time**. Capacity grows linearly with the number of antennas, not just logarithmically with power:
$$C \approx \min(N_t, N_r)\,B\log_2(1 + \text{SNR})$$

Wi-Fi 6 supports up to 8 spatial streams, and 5G base stations use **massive MIMO** with 64 or more antenna elements, serving many users at once on the same frequencies.

```quiz
- It must be shorter than 2 µs

```

```quiz
? Why are OFDM subcarriers spaced at exactly $1/T_u$?
+ So they are orthogonal: each one's spectrum is zero at every other subcarrier's center
- To minimize the cyclic prefix
- To reduce the peak-to-average power ratio
- So that the FFT size is a power of 2
> Overlapping yet orthogonal subcarriers make OFDM spectrally efficient.
```

## Key takeaways

- Multipath delay spread causes ISI and frequency-selective fading at high data rates.
- OFDM splits data over many orthogonal subcarriers ($\Delta f = 1/T_u$), built with IFFT/FFT; a cyclic prefix longer than the delay spread removes ISI.
- MIMO adds diversity, beamforming and spatial multiplexing, with capacity growing as $\min(N_t, N_r)$.
