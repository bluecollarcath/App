## Putting the message in the angle

AM varies a carrier's **amplitude**, so any noise or interference that changes the amplitude ends up in the output. **Angle modulation** keeps the amplitude constant and puts the message in the carrier's **phase** or **frequency** instead. The receiver can then clip off amplitude noise entirely. That's why FM radio sounds cleaner than AM, and why it was used for analog TV sound, two-way radio and early cellular phones.

A general angle-modulated signal is

$$
s(t) = A_c\cos\big(2\pi f_ct + \phi(t)\big)
$$

- **Phase modulation (PM):** $\phi(t) = k_pm(t)$; the phase follows the message.
- **Frequency modulation (FM):** the **instantaneous frequency** follows the message:
$$f_i(t) = f_c + k_fm(t), \qquad \phi(t) = 2\pi k_f\int_0^t m(\tau)\,d\tau$$

The two are closely related: FM is PM of the integrated message.

## Deviation and modulation index

For a tone message $m(t) = A_m\cos2\pi f_mt$, the carrier frequency swings by a **peak deviation** $\Delta f = k_fA_m$, and

$$
s(t) = A_c\cos\big(2\pi f_ct + \beta\sin2\pi f_mt\big), \qquad \beta = \frac{\Delta f}{f_m}
$$

The **modulation index** $\beta$ is the peak phase swing in radians.

- **Narrowband FM** ($\beta \ll 1$): only the first pair of sidebands matters; the bandwidth is about $2f_m$, the same as AM.
- **Wideband FM** ($\beta \gg 1$): many sidebands at $f_c \pm nf_m$, with amplitudes given by Bessel functions $J_n(\beta)$.

## Carson's rule

About 98% of an FM signal's power lies within

$$
B_T \approx 2(\Delta f + f_m) = 2f_m(\beta + 1)
$$

> **Broadcast FM.** Peak deviation 75 kHz, audio up to 15 kHz: $\beta = 5$ and $B_T \approx 2(75 + 15) = 180$ kHz. Stations are spaced 200 kHz apart. An AM station needs only 10 kHz; FM spends bandwidth to buy noise immunity.

## The FM trade: bandwidth for SNR

Above a threshold carrier-to-noise ratio (roughly 10 dB), a wideband FM receiver's output SNR improves over a baseband system by about

$$
\frac{\text{SNR}_{FM}}{\text{SNR}_{baseband}} \approx 3\beta^2(\beta + 1)
$$

(for a tone; the exact factor depends on the message). For $\beta = 5$, the core $3\beta^2 = 75$ factor alone is 18.8 dB. Doubling the deviation buys about 6 dB more SNR, at roughly double the bandwidth. Broadcast FM adds **pre-emphasis** (boosting high audio frequencies before transmission, then cutting them in the receiver) because FM noise rises with audio frequency.

Below threshold, the advantage collapses suddenly, which is why an FM station fades out abruptly ("crackle, then nothing") rather than gradually like AM.

## Demodulation

FM receivers first pass the signal through a **limiter**, which strips off amplitude variations, then convert frequency back to voltage:

- A **frequency discriminator** (a slope detector): a circuit whose output voltage is proportional to frequency.
- A **phase-locked loop** (PLL): a voltage-controlled oscillator is locked to the incoming signal; the control voltage that keeps it locked **is** the message.
- In software radios, the message is the time derivative of the phase of the complex baseband signal: $m(t) \propto \frac{d}{dt}\arg\big(I + jQ\big)$.

## Key takeaways

- PM puts the message in the phase, FM in the instantaneous frequency; the amplitude stays constant.
- Modulation index $\beta = \Delta f/f_m$; Carson's rule $B_T \approx 2(\Delta f + f_m)$.
- Wideband FM trades bandwidth for SNR (about $3\beta^2$), above a threshold.
- Demodulate with a limiter plus a discriminator or PLL.
