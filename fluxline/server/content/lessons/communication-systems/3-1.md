## How much information is in a message?

Claude Shannon's 1948 paper *A Mathematical Theory of Communication* answered two questions that seemed impossible to answer precisely: how far can data be compressed, and how fast can it be sent reliably over a noisy channel? The answers, **entropy** and **channel capacity**, are the hard limits every compression format and every modem is measured against.

## Entropy

An event of probability $p$ carries $\log_2(1/p)$ bits of information: rare events are more informative. The **entropy** of a source is the average information per symbol:

$$
H = -\sum_i p_i\log_2p_i \quad \text{bits/symbol}
$$

- $H$ is largest when all $M$ symbols are equally likely: $H = \log_2M$.
- $H = 0$ when the outcome is certain.
- A binary source with $P(1) = p$ has $H_b(p) = -p\log_2p - (1 - p)\log_2(1 - p)$: 1 bit for a fair coin, only 0.47 bits for $p = 0.9$.

**Source coding theorem:** a source can be compressed losslessly to an average of $H$ bits per symbol, and no further.

## Huffman coding

A practical code that approaches $H$: give frequent symbols short codewords and rare ones long codewords. Repeatedly merge the two least likely symbols into a tree; each branch adds a bit.

> **Example.** Four symbols with probabilities $\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18$.
>
> $H = \tfrac12(1) + \tfrac14(2) + \tfrac18(3) + \tfrac18(3) = 1.75$ bits/symbol.
>
> Huffman code: A = 0, B = 10, C = 110, D = 111. Average length $= 1.75$ bits, exactly the entropy (because the probabilities are powers of 1/2), versus 2 bits for a fixed-length code.

ZIP, PNG and the lossless stages of JPEG and MP3 all use Huffman or arithmetic coding.

## Channel capacity

Every channel has a **capacity** $C$: below it, data can be sent with arbitrarily low error probability (with good enough coding); above it, errors are unavoidable. For a channel of bandwidth $B$ with additive white Gaussian noise, the **Shannon–Hartley theorem** gives

$$
C = B\log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right)\ \text{bits/s}
$$

> **Example: a telephone line.** $B = 3.1$ kHz, SNR = 30 dB (1000): $C = 3100\log_2(1001) = 30.9$ kb/s. Dial-up modems topped out at 33.6 kb/s on analog lines, right at this limit.

Two regimes:

- **Bandwidth-limited** (high SNR): capacity grows only logarithmically with power. Each extra bit/s/Hz needs about 3 dB more SNR.
- **Power-limited** (low SNR, wide bandwidth): capacity grows almost linearly with power.

Taking $B \to \infty$ gives the ultimate limit on energy per bit: reliable communication is possible only if

$$
\frac{E_b}{N_0} > \ln2 = -1.59\ \text{dB}
$$

(the **Shannon limit**). Uncoded BPSK needs 9.6 dB for $10^{-5}$, so there's 11 dB of potential improvement. Error-correcting codes recover most of it (next lesson).

## Key takeaways

- Entropy $H = -\sum p\log_2p$ is the minimum average bits per symbol for lossless compression; Huffman codes approach it.
- Shannon–Hartley: $C = B\log_2(1 + S/N)$. Below $C$, error-free transmission is possible with coding.
- At high SNR, each extra bit/s/Hz costs ~3 dB; the absolute floor is $E_b/N_0 = -1.59$ dB.
