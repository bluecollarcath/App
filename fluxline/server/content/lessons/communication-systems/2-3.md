## Deciding what was sent

The receiver sees the transmitted symbol plus noise. Its job: decide which symbol was sent, making as few mistakes as possible. Two questions: what's the best way to process the waveform, and how often will it still be wrong?

## The matched filter

For a known pulse $p(t)$ in white Gaussian noise of PSD $N_0/2$, the linear filter that maximizes the SNR at the sampling instant is the **matched filter**, with impulse response

$$
h(t) = p(T - t)
$$

(the pulse reversed in time). Its peak output SNR depends only on the pulse **energy** $E$, not its shape:

$$
\text{SNR}_{max} = \frac{2E}{N_0}
$$

Equivalently, the receiver **correlates** the received signal with a copy of the expected pulse, $\int r(t)p(t)\,dt$, and samples the result. Every digital receiver does this, often as a root-raised-cosine filter (Lesson 2.1) or a digital correlator; GPS receivers correlate against known spreading codes to pull signals out from 20 dB **below** the noise.

## Bit-error rate and $E_b/N_0$

After the matched filter, the decision is a comparison against a threshold, and the error probability is a Gaussian tail (Probability, Lesson 1.3). The natural measure of signal strength is the energy per bit relative to the noise density, $E_b/N_0$:

| Scheme | Bit-error probability |
|---|---|
| BPSK, QPSK (Gray coded) | $P_b = Q\!\left(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0}\right)$ |
| Coherent OOK, orthogonal BFSK | $P_b = Q\!\left(\sqrt{E_b/N_0}\right)$ |

OOK and FSK need **3 dB more** $E_b/N_0$ than BPSK for the same error rate, because their two symbols are less far apart for the same average energy. Higher-order PSK and QAM need more still.


> **Example.** At $E_b/N_0 = 9.6$ dB ($= 9.12$), BPSK gives $P_b = Q(\sqrt{18.2}) = Q(4.27) \approx 10^{-5}$: one error in 100,000 bits. Coherent FSK at the same $E_b/N_0$ gives $1.3\times10^{-3}$, a hundred times worse.

## Connecting $E_b/N_0$ to the link

$E_b/N_0$ comes from quantities you can measure:

$$
E_b = \frac{P_r}{R_b}, \qquad \frac{E_b}{N_0} = \frac{P_r}{N_0R_b} = \text{SNR}\cdot\frac{B}{R_b}
$$

with $N_0 = kT_{sys}$ (Probability, Lesson 3.3). So halving the bit rate doubles $E_b/N_0$ (3 dB): slower links reach farther, which is how deep-space probes still return data from beyond Pluto, at a few hundred bits per second.

> **Example.** Received power $10^{-12}$ W (−90 dBm) at a 1 Mb/s rate gives $E_b = 10^{-18}$ J. With $N_0 = kT = 4\times10^{-21}$ W/Hz (room temperature, no excess receiver noise), $E_b/N_0 = 250$ (24 dB), comfortably reliable even for 64-QAM.

## Key takeaways

- The matched filter $h(t) = p(T - t)$ maximizes output SNR to $2E/N_0$.
- BPSK/QPSK: $P_b = Q(\sqrt{2E_b/N_0})$, about $10^{-5}$ at 9.6 dB; OOK/FSK need 3 dB more.
- $E_b/N_0 = P_r/(N_0R_b)$: lower data rates reach farther.
