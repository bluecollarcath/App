## One receiver, any station

Filtering one station out of hundreds with sharp, tunable filters at radio frequency is nearly impossible: a filter that must be 10 kHz wide at 1 MHz and also at 1.6 MHz would need extremely high Q, retuned as the station changes. Edwin Armstrong's 1918 solution, the **superheterodyne** receiver, is still in nearly every radio, TV, phone and radar. The trick: **move** each station to one fixed **intermediate frequency** (IF), and build all the hard filtering at that one frequency.

```circuit

BOX 3.6,1.7 1.6,0.9 LO
ARROW 3.6,1.25 3.6,0.28
```

## Mixing

A **mixer** multiplies the received signal by a **local oscillator** (LO) at $f_{LO}$. By the product-to-sum identity, a signal at $f_{RF}$ produces components at

$$
f_{RF} + f_{LO} \quad\text{and}\quad |f_{RF} - f_{LO}|
$$

The IF filter keeps the difference. To tune the radio, you change only $f_{LO}$ so that the wanted station always lands on $f_{IF}$. Common choices:

- AM broadcast: $f_{IF} = 455$ kHz.
- FM broadcast: $f_{IF} = 10.7$ MHz.

With **high-side injection** ($f_{LO} = f_{RF} + f_{IF}$):

> **AM example.** To receive 1000 kHz, set the LO to 1455 kHz; the difference is 455 kHz.

## The image problem

The mixer only sees the **difference** frequency, so a second input frequency also lands on the IF: the one on the other side of the LO,

$$
f_{image} = f_{RF} + 2f_{IF} \quad\text{(high-side LO)}
$$

> In the AM example, a station at $1455 + 455 = 1910$ kHz also produces a 455 kHz difference. If it isn't removed **before** the mixer, it's heard on top of the wanted station, and no IF filter can separate them.

That's the job of the RF **preselector** filter before the mixer. It doesn't need to be sharp, only good enough to reject frequencies $2f_{IF}$ away. That drives the choice of IF:

- **High IF:** the image is far away and easy to reject, but the IF filter must be narrow relative to a high frequency, which is harder.
- **Low IF:** sharp channel filtering is easy, but image rejection is hard.

Many receivers resolve this with **double conversion**: a high first IF for image rejection, then a second mixer down to a low IF for selectivity. FM radios use 10.7 MHz so the image ($f_{RF} + 21.4$ MHz) falls outside the 88–108 MHz band's preselector passband.

Modern **zero-IF** (direct-conversion) and software-defined radios mix straight to 0 Hz with two LOs 90° apart, producing I and Q signals. The image then becomes the negative-frequency side of the spectrum, and it's cancelled by I/Q processing rather than by filtering.

## Noise in the receiver chain

The first stage dominates a receiver's sensitivity. With noise factors $F_i$ (linear) and gains $G_i$, the **Friis formula** for a cascade gives

$$
F_{total} = F_1 + \frac{F_2 - 1}{G_1} + \frac{F_3 - 1}{G_1G_2} + \cdots
$$

> **Example.** A low-noise amplifier with NF = 1 dB ($F = 1.26$) and 20 dB gain, followed by a mixer with NF = 10 dB ($F = 10$):
> $$
> F = 1.26 + \frac{9}{100} = 1.35 \quad (1.30\ \text{dB})
> $$
> The noisy mixer barely matters, because the LNA's gain comes first. That's why every sensitive receiver starts with a low-noise amplifier.

## Key takeaways

- A superhet mixes every station to one fixed IF, where the sharp filtering and gain are done.
- Mixing produces $f_{RF} \pm f_{LO}$; the image at $f_{RF} + 2f_{IF}$ (high-side LO) must be filtered out before the mixer.
- High IF eases image rejection; low IF eases selectivity; double conversion gets both.
- Friis: the first stage's noise figure and gain dominate the receiver's sensitivity.
