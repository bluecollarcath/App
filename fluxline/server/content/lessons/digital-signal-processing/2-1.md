## A Fourier transform a computer can calculate

The discrete-time Fourier transform of Signals & Systems is a continuous function of frequency computed from an infinitely long sequence. A computer has neither. The **discrete Fourier transform** (DFT) takes $N$ samples and returns $N$ frequency values:

$$
X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/N}, \qquad
x[n] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k]\,e^{j2\pi kn/N}
$$

for $k = 0, 1, \ldots, N - 1$. Each $X[k]$ measures how much of a complex sinusoid with exactly $k$ cycles per $N$ samples the signal contains.

## What the bins mean

With sampling rate $f_s$, bin $k$ corresponds to the frequency

$$
f_k = k\,\frac{f_s}{N}, \qquad \Delta f = \frac{f_s}{N} = \frac{1}{T_{record}}
$$

The **bin spacing** $\Delta f$ is the inverse of the record length: to see finer frequency detail, record **longer**, not faster.

- $X[0]$ is the sum of the samples ($N$ times the DC value).
- Bins above $N/2$ are the negative frequencies: bin $N - k$ is frequency $-f_k$.
- For a **real** signal, $X[N - k] = X^*[k]$, so only bins 0 to $N/2$ carry new information.

> **Example.** $f_s = 8$ kHz, $N = 256$: $\Delta f = 31.25$ Hz, and a 1 kHz tone lands exactly in bin $k = 1000/31.25 = 32$ (and its mirror in bin 224). A 1 kHz tone with amplitude $A$ gives $|X[32]| = AN/2 = 128A$.

> **Small example.** $x = [1, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0]$ ($N = 8$): $X[0] = 4$; at the even bins $k = 2, 4, 6$ the four ones sum to zero; the odd bins are nonzero. It's a sampled version of the sinc-shaped spectrum of a rectangular pulse.

## The DFT assumes periodicity

The DFT treats the $N$ samples as **one period** of an infinitely repeating signal. Two consequences:

**Circular convolution.** Multiplying two DFTs corresponds to **circular** convolution of the sequences, where the tail of the result wraps around to the beginning. To get ordinary (linear) convolution, the basis of fast filtering, both sequences must be **zero-padded** to at least $N_1 + N_2 - 1$ points so nothing wraps.

**Leakage.** If the record doesn't contain a whole number of cycles of a tone, the assumed periodic extension has a jump, and the tone's energy spreads into many bins. That's the subject of Lesson 2.3.

## Zero padding

Appending zeros before the DFT gives more bins, sampling the underlying spectrum more densely. It makes plots smoother and peaks easier to locate, but it adds **no new resolution**: two tones closer than about $1/T_{record}$ still can't be separated. Only more data does that.

## Key takeaways

- $X[k] = \sum x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}$; bin $k$ is at $kf_s/N$; spacing $\Delta f = 1/T_{record}$.
- For real signals, bins above $N/2$ mirror the lower ones.
- DFT multiplication is circular convolution; zero-pad to $N_1 + N_2 - 1$ for linear convolution.
- Zero padding interpolates; only longer records improve resolution.
