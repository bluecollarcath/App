## Sharp filters, cheaply

An FIR filter with a sharp cutoff may need hundreds of taps. An IIR filter uses feedback, so its poles can do the work: a sixth-order IIR filter (about a dozen multiplies per sample) can match the sharpness of a 100-tap FIR. The price is nonlinear phase and the need to check stability. IIR filters are designed by borrowing a century of **analog** filter theory and translating it to discrete time.

## Analog prototypes

| Type | Passband | Stopband | Rolloff |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Butterworth** | maximally flat | monotonic | gentlest |
| **Chebyshev I** | equiripple | monotonic | sharper |
| **Chebyshev II** | flat | equiripple | sharper |
| **Elliptic** | equiripple | equiripple | sharpest for a given order |

The **Butterworth** filter of order $N$ and cutoff $\Omega_c$ has

$$
|H(j\Omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + (\Omega/\Omega_c)^{2N}}
$$

It's 3 dB down at $\Omega_c$ and falls at $20N$ dB/decade beyond. To get attenuation $A$ dB at frequency $\Omega_s$:

$$
N \ge \frac{\log_{10}\big(10^{A/10} - 1\big)}{2\log_{10}(\Omega_s/\Omega_c)}
$$

> **Example.** −3 dB at 1 kHz and at least 40 dB down at 4 kHz: $N \ge \log_{10}(9999)/(2\log_{10}4) = 3.32$, so order 4.

## The bilinear transform

To turn an analog $H(s)$ into a digital $H(z)$, substitute

$$
s = \frac{2}{T}\cdot\frac{z - 1}{z + 1}
$$

It maps the whole left half of the s-plane inside the unit circle, so **stable analog filters become stable digital filters**, and the order is preserved. There's no aliasing, because the entire analog frequency axis is squeezed onto the unit circle. That squeezing is nonlinear (**frequency warping**):

$$
\Omega = \frac{2}{T}\tan\frac{\omega}{2}
$$

so the critical frequencies must be **pre-warped**: design the analog prototype at $\Omega$ computed from the desired digital frequency $\omega$, and after the transform the cutoff lands exactly where you want it.

> **Example.** For a 1 kHz digital cutoff at $f_s = 8$ kHz: $\omega = 2\pi(1000/8000) = \pi/4$, so $\Omega = 2(8000)\tan(\pi/8) = 6627$ rad/s, or 1055 Hz. Design the analog prototype at 1055 Hz; the digital filter's cutoff comes out at exactly 1 kHz.

In practice, `scipy.signal.butter(4, 1000, fs=8000)` does all of this in one call.

## IIR vs. FIR

| | IIR | FIR |
|---|---|---|
| Sharpness per multiply | high | low |
| Phase | nonlinear (worst near the cutoff) | exactly linear possible |
| Stability | must keep poles inside the unit circle | always stable |
| Sensitivity to coefficient rounding | can be high | low |

Use IIR when phase doesn't matter and computation is tight (audio equalizers, sensor smoothing, control loops); FIR when phase matters or for multirate systems.

## Key takeaways

- IIR filters reuse analog prototypes: Butterworth (flat), Chebyshev (ripple), elliptic (sharpest).
- Butterworth: $|H|^2 = 1/(1 + (\Omega/\Omega_c)^{2N})$, rolling off at $20N$ dB/decade.
- The bilinear transform preserves stability and order but warps frequency; pre-warp with $\Omega = (2/T)\tan(\omega/2)$.
- IIR is efficient but not linear-phase.
