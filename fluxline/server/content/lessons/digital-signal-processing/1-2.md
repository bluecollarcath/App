## Systems as recipes

A digital filter is an algorithm: each output sample is computed from current and past inputs and past outputs. That recipe is a **linear constant-coefficient difference equation**:

$$
y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{M} b_k\,x[n-k] - \sum_{k=1}^{N} a_k\,y[n-k]
$$

- If all $a_k = 0$, the output depends only on inputs: a **finite impulse response** (FIR) filter, whose impulse response ends after $M + 1$ samples.
- If any $a_k \ne 0$, outputs feed back: an **infinite impulse response** (IIR) filter, whose impulse response can ring on forever.

## The transfer function

Taking z-transforms (Signals & Systems, Lesson 4.2), with $z^{-1}$ standing for a one-sample delay:

$$
H(z) = \frac{Y(z)}{X(z)} = \frac{b_0 + b_1z^{-1} + \cdots + b_Mz^{-M}}{1 + a_1z^{-1} + \cdots + a_Nz^{-N}}
$$

The roots of the numerator are the **zeros**, the roots of the denominator the **poles**. A causal filter is **stable** if and only if every pole lies **inside the unit circle**. FIR filters have no poles except at $z = 0$, so they're always stable.

## Frequency response: walking the unit circle

Evaluating $H(z)$ on the unit circle, $z = e^{j\omega}$, gives the frequency response, where $\omega$ runs from 0 (DC) to $\pi$ (half the sampling rate, the Nyquist frequency):

$$
H(e^{j\omega}) = H(z)\big|_{z = e^{j\omega}}, \qquad f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}f_s
$$

**Geometric picture.** $|H(e^{j\omega})|$ is proportional to the product of distances from the point $e^{j\omega}$ to the zeros, divided by the product of distances to the poles. So:

- a **pole near the unit circle** creates a peak at that angle (a resonance);
- a **zero on the unit circle** creates a null (complete rejection of that frequency).

> **Example 1: two-point average.** $y[n] = \tfrac12(x[n] + x[n-1])$, so $H(z) = \tfrac12(1 + z^{-1})$, with a zero at $z = -1$. Then $|H(e^{j\omega})| = |\cos(\omega/2)|$: gain 1 at DC, a perfect null at $f_s/2$. A crude lowpass.

> **Example 2: one-pole smoother.** $y[n] = 0.9\,y[n-1] + 0.1\,x[n]$, so $H(z) = \dfrac{0.1}{1 - 0.9z^{-1}}$, with a pole at $z = 0.9$.
> - DC ($z = 1$): $0.1/0.1 = 1$.
> - Nyquist ($z = -1$): $0.1/1.9 = 0.053$ (−25.6 dB).
>
> A much sharper lowpass than the average, using only one multiply-add more. This is the **exponential moving average** used everywhere from sensor smoothing to stock charts. Moving the pole closer to 1 makes it smoother and slower.

```plot
x: 0, 1, ω / π
y: 0, 1.1, |H(e^{jω})|
f: abs(cos(pi*x/2)) | two-point average
f: 0.1/sqrt(1 - 1.8*cos(pi*x) + 0.81) | one-pole, pole at 0.9 | dash
caption: Magnitude responses from DC ($\omega = 0$) to the Nyquist frequency ($\omega = \pi$). The pole near $z = 1$ makes a much narrower passband than the single zero at $z = -1$.
```

## Key takeaways

- Difference equations define FIR (no feedback) and IIR (feedback) filters; $H(z) = B(z)/A(z)$.
- Causal stability: every pole inside the unit circle. FIR filters are always stable.
- $H(e^{j\omega})$ is $H(z)$ on the unit circle; poles near it make peaks, zeros on it make nulls.
