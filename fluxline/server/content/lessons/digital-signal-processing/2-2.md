## $N^2$ is too slow

Computing an $N$-point DFT directly takes $N$ complex multiply-adds for each of $N$ outputs: $N^2$ operations. For $N = 1024$, that's a million; for a one-second audio clip at 48 kHz, two billion. The **fast Fourier transform** (FFT), published by Cooley and Tukey in 1965 (and known to Gauss in 1805), computes exactly the same result in about $\tfrac{N}{2}\log_2N$ multiplications. It's one of the most important algorithms ever devised: it makes spectrum analyzers, MP3, JPEG, OFDM radios, MRI and fast convolution possible.

## Divide and conquer

Let $W_N = e^{-j2\pi/N}$ and $N$ be a power of 2. Split the input into its even- and odd-indexed samples. Each half is itself an $N/2$-point DFT:

$$
X[k] = \underbrace{\sum_{m} x[2m]\,W_{N/2}^{mk}}_{E[k]} + W_N^k\underbrace{\sum_{m} x[2m+1]\,W_{N/2}^{mk}}_{O[k]}
$$

Since $E[k]$ and $O[k]$ repeat with period $N/2$, and $W_N^{k + N/2} = -W_N^k$, each pair of outputs comes from one multiplication:

$$
X[k] = E[k] + W_N^kO[k], \qquad X[k + N/2] = E[k] - W_N^kO[k]
$$

That two-in, two-out step is the **butterfly**. Apply the split again to each half, and again, until only 2-point DFTs remain. This is the **radix-2 decimation-in-time** FFT.

## Counting the work

- $\log_2N$ stages, each with $N/2$ butterflies, each needing one complex multiplication.
- Total: $\tfrac{N}{2}\log_2N$ complex multiplications, versus $N^2$.

| $N$ | Direct DFT | FFT | Speed-up |
|---|---|---|---|
| 64 | 4,096 | 192 | 21× |
| 1,024 | 1,048,576 | 5,120 | 205× |
| 65,536 | $4.3\times10^9$ | 524,288 | 8,192× |

## Bit reversal

Repeatedly splitting into even and odd samples scrambles the input order: the FFT takes its inputs (or produces its outputs) in **bit-reversed** order. Sample 1 (binary 001 for $N = 8$) goes to position 4 (100); sample 3 (011) to position 6 (110). Hardware FFTs and DSP chips have special addressing modes for it.

## Fast convolution

Filtering a long signal with a long FIR filter takes $N_h$ multiplications per output sample. With FFTs, convolution becomes multiplication: transform blocks of the signal, multiply by the filter's DFT, and transform back (**overlap-add** or **overlap-save**). For filters longer than about 64 taps, this is far cheaper. It's how audio reverb with 100,000-tap room responses runs in real time.

Variants abound: radix-4 and split-radix FFTs save more multiplications; FFTs for any $N$ (not just powers of 2) exist; libraries like FFTW pick the fastest plan for each size and machine.

## Key takeaways

- The FFT splits an $N$-point DFT into two $N/2$-point DFTs, recursively, combined with butterflies.
- Cost: $\tfrac N2\log_2N$ complex multiplications instead of $N^2$.
- Radix-2 DIT uses bit-reversed ordering.
- FFT-based (overlap-add/save) convolution makes long filters cheap.
