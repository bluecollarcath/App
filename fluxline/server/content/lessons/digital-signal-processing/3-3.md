## Same filter, different arithmetic

A transfer function $H(z)$ can be built many ways. In exact arithmetic they're identical; in a real processor, with finite word lengths and fixed-point numbers, they behave very differently. Picking the right **structure** is what separates a filter that works on paper from one that works on a $2 microcontroller.

## Direct forms

**Direct form I** implements the difference equation literally: one delay line for past inputs, one for past outputs. **Direct form II** shares a single delay line between the feedback and feedforward parts, using the minimum number of delays (equal to the filter order). **Transposed** versions reverse the signal flow, which often improves numerical behavior in floating point.

For an $N$th-order filter, all of them need about $2N + 1$ multiplications per sample.

## Why high-order direct forms fail

The poles of a high-order filter are the roots of a long polynomial, and polynomial roots are **extremely sensitive** to small changes in the coefficients. Rounding the coefficients of a 10th-order narrowband filter to 16 bits can move its poles a lot, or even outside the unit circle, making the filter unstable. The sensitivity is worst when poles are clustered near the unit circle, which is exactly the case for sharp filters.

## Cascade of biquads

The standard fix: factor $H(z)$ into **second-order sections** ("biquads"), each with one pair of complex-conjugate poles and zeros:

$$
H(z) = G\prod_{k=1}^{N/2}\frac{b_{0k} + b_{1k}z^{-1} + b_{2k}z^{-2}}{1 + a_{1k}z^{-1} + a_{2k}z^{-2}}
$$

Each section's poles depend on only two coefficients, so rounding errors stay local, and each section can be checked and scaled independently. An 8th-order filter becomes four biquads. Libraries like `scipy.signal` output filters as second-order sections (`sos`) precisely for this reason, and audio DSP chips are built around biquad engines.

## Fixed-point arithmetic

Low-cost processors use integers interpreted as fractions. In **Q15** format, a 16-bit signed integer represents values from $-1$ to $1 - 2^{-15}$, with a resolution of $2^{-15} = 3.05\times10^{-5}$. Three effects to manage:

- **Coefficient quantization:** coefficients are rounded to the nearest representable value, moving poles and zeros (above).
- **Round-off noise:** each multiplication's result is rounded, adding a small noise source of about $\Delta^2/12$ (Probability, Lesson 1.3) at each multiplier. Feedback in IIR filters can amplify it.
- **Overflow:** sums that exceed the range wrap around from $+1$ to $-1$, a huge error. Prevent it by **scaling** the signal into each section (so it can't overflow for any input), by using **saturating** arithmetic, and by ordering the sections sensibly.

IIR filters in fixed point can also show **limit cycles**: small persistent oscillations caused by rounding, even with zero input. Careful rounding modes and wider accumulators (most DSPs accumulate in 32 or 40 bits) suppress them.

## Key takeaways

- Direct forms implement the difference equation directly; DF-II uses the minimum number of delays.
- High-order filters are numerically fragile; use cascades of biquads (second-order sections).
- Fixed point (e.g. Q15): manage coefficient quantization, round-off noise, overflow (scaling, saturation) and limit cycles.
