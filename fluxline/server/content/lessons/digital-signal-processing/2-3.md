## When a pure tone smears

Take the DFT of a pure sine wave and you expect one sharp spike. You usually get a spike with "skirts" spreading across the whole spectrum. That's **spectral leakage**, and it happens whenever the record doesn't hold a whole number of the sine's cycles.

The cause: taking $N$ samples is the same as multiplying an infinitely long signal by a rectangular **window**, which is 1 for the samples you kept and 0 elsewhere. Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency, so every spectral line gets replaced by a copy of the window's spectrum. For a rectangular window that's a sinc, whose sidelobes fall off slowly: the first is only 13 dB below the peak. Unless the tone sits exactly on a bin (where the sinc's zeros line up with the other bins), its energy leaks everywhere, and a weak tone near a strong one disappears under the strong one's sidelobes.

## Window functions

The cure is to taper the data smoothly to zero at both ends before the DFT, so there's no jump when the record repeats. Tapering lowers the sidelobes at the cost of a **wider main lobe**, which means poorer resolution of closely spaced tones.

| Window | Highest sidelobe | Main-lobe width (bins, null to null) | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rectangular | −13 dB | 2 | tones exactly on bins; transients |
| Hann | −31 dB | 4 | general-purpose default |
| Hamming | −43 dB | 4 | speech, filter design |
| Blackman | −58 dB | 6 | high dynamic range |
| Flat-top | −93 dB | 10 | accurate amplitude measurement |

The Hann window is $w[n] = 0.5\big(1 - \cos\frac{2\pi n}{N - 1}\big)$.

## Resolution vs. dynamic range

Choosing a window is choosing a trade-off:

- **Resolution:** two tones are distinguishable only if they're separated by more than about a main-lobe half-width. With a Hann window that's roughly 2 bins, or $2f_s/N$. The only way to improve resolution with a given window is a longer record.
- **Dynamic range:** a weak tone is visible only if it rises above the strong tones' sidelobes. Low-sidelobe windows win here.

Windows also scale amplitudes: tapering removes signal, so a window's **coherent gain** (the average of $w[n]$, 0.5 for Hann) must be divided out to read tone amplitudes correctly. Spectrum analyzers do this automatically, and their "resolution bandwidth" setting is the window's main-lobe width.

> **Example.** $f_s = 1$ kHz, $N = 1000$: bins are 1 Hz apart. With a Hann window, tones at 100 Hz and 101.5 Hz are hard to separate (1.5 bins apart, inside the main lobe), but tones at 100 Hz and 103 Hz are clearly resolved. A 10 Hz tone 60 dB weaker than a 100 Hz tone is invisible with a rectangular window when the strong tone falls between bins, but easily seen with Blackman.

## Key takeaways

- Truncating a record multiplies by a window; leakage is the window's spectrum smearing each line.
- Tapered windows (Hann, Hamming, Blackman) lower sidelobes but widen the main lobe.
- Resolution ~ main-lobe width ($\propto f_s/N$); dynamic range ~ sidelobe level.
- Correct amplitudes for the window's coherent gain.
