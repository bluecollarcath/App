## Filters with no feedback

An FIR filter is a weighted sum of the last $N$ inputs:

$$
y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} h[k]\,x[n-k]
$$

The coefficients $h[k]$ **are** the impulse response, and the filter has $N$ **taps**. FIR filters dominate in DSP because they're always stable, simple to implement, and can have exactly **linear phase**.

## Linear phase

If the coefficients are symmetric, $h[k] = h[N - 1 - k]$, the frequency response is

$$
H(e^{j\omega}) = A(\omega)\,e^{-j\omega(N-1)/2}
$$

with $A(\omega)$ real. Every frequency is delayed by the same **group delay** of $(N - 1)/2$ samples, so the waveform's shape passes through undistorted, just late. That matters for audio, for pulses in communications (where phase distortion causes ISI), and for biomedical signals where the waveshape carries the diagnosis. IIR filters can't do this exactly.

> A 31-tap symmetric FIR delays every frequency by 15 samples; at 48 kHz that's 0.31 ms.

## The window method

1. Start from the ideal lowpass with cutoff $\omega_c$, whose impulse response is an infinitely long sinc: $h_d[n] = \dfrac{\omega_c}{\pi}\,\text{sinc}\!\left(\dfrac{\omega_c n}{\pi}\right)$.
2. Shift it by $M = (N - 1)/2$ to make it causal, and truncate to $N$ taps.
3. Multiply by a window $w[n]$ to tame the ripples that truncation causes (the Gibbs phenomenon).

$$
h[n] = h_d[n - M]\,w[n]
$$

The window plays the same role as in spectral analysis (Lesson 2.3): its sidelobe level sets the stopband attenuation (about 44 dB with Hamming, 74 dB with Blackman), and its main-lobe width sets the transition band, which narrows as $N$ grows.

```plot
samples: 0.5*sinc(0.5*(x - 10))*(0.54 - 0.46*cos(2*pi*x/20)) | 1 | h[n]
caption: A 21-tap Hamming-windowed lowpass FIR with cutoff at $f_s/4$ ($\omega_c = \pi/2$). The coefficients are symmetric about $n = 10$, so the phase is linear with a 10-sample delay.
```

## How many taps?

A useful rule of thumb (fred harris's) estimates the length for stopband attenuation $A$ (in dB) and transition width $\Delta f$:

$$
N \approx \frac{A}{22}\cdot\frac{f_s}{\Delta f}
$$

> **Example.** 60 dB rejection, $f_s = 48$ kHz, a 2 kHz transition band: $N \approx (60/22)(24) = 65$ taps. Halving the transition band doubles the length. Sharp FIR filters are long.

## Optimal design: Parks–McClellan

The window method is simple but not optimal: its error is largest near the band edge and wasted elsewhere. The **Parks–McClellan** (Remez exchange) algorithm finds the filter of a given length whose maximum error is as small as possible, spreading the error evenly as **equiripple** in the passband and stopband. For the same specification it typically needs 10–20% fewer taps. It's what `firpm` (MATLAB) and `scipy.signal.remez` do.

## Key takeaways

- FIR: $y = \sum h[k]x[n - k]$; always stable; symmetric taps give exact linear phase with delay $(N - 1)/2$.
- Window method: truncated, shifted sinc times a window; the window sets stopband depth and transition width.
- Length $\approx (A/22)(f_s/\Delta f)$; Parks–McClellan gives optimal equiripple designs.
