## Three basic converters

Volt-second balance (Lesson 1.1) gives the conversion ratio of any switching converter in a few lines. The three basic non-isolated topologies use the same parts (one switch, one diode, one inductor, one capacitor) arranged differently:

| Converter | $V_o/V_{in}$ in CCM | Output | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Buck** | $D$ | lower, same polarity | point-of-load regulators, CPU cores, chargers |
| **Boost** | $\dfrac{1}{1 - D}$ | higher, same polarity | battery-to-LED, PFC, solar |
| **Buck-boost** | $-\dfrac{D}{1 - D}$ | either, inverted | negative rails, wide input ranges |

## The buck converter in detail

**Inductor ripple.** While the switch is on, $V_{in} - V_o$ is across the inductor for a time $DT_s$:

$$
\Delta i_L = \frac{(V_{in} - V_o)D}{Lf_s}
$$

**Output ripple.** The ripple current flows into the output capacitor; its charge produces a small triangular-parabolic voltage ripple:

$$
\Delta v_o = \frac{\Delta i_L}{8Cf_s}
$$

(plus the ripple current times the capacitor's ESR, often the bigger term with electrolytic capacitors).

> **Example: 12 V to 3.3 V at 2 A**, $f_s = 500$ kHz, $L = 4.7\ \mu$H, $C = 22\ \mu$F (ceramic, negligible ESR).
>
> $D = 3.3/12 = 0.275$.
>
> $\Delta i_L = (8.7)(0.275)/(4.7\times10^{-6} \times 5\times10^5) = 1.02$ A peak-to-peak, about 50% of the load current (designers typically aim for 20–40%).
>
> $\Delta v_o = 1.02/(8 \times 22\times10^{-6} \times 5\times10^5) = 11.6$ mV, 0.35% of the output.

Higher switching frequency shrinks $L$ and $C$ proportionally. That's the main reason converters keep moving to higher $f_s$ (with GaN, Lesson 3.1), limited by switching losses.

## The boost converter

Switch on: the inductor charges from $V_{in}$. Switch off: the inductor's voltage adds to $V_{in}$ to push current through the diode into the higher output. Volt-second balance gives $V_o = V_{in}/(1 - D)$.

Because the output current only flows during the off time, the inductor (input) current is larger than the output current:

$$
I_{in} = \frac{I_o}{1 - D}, \qquad \Delta i_L = \frac{V_{in}D}{Lf_s}
$$

> **Example: 5 V to 12 V at 1 A**, $L = 10\ \mu$H, $f_s = 1$ MHz: $D = 0.583$, input current 2.4 A, ripple $5(0.583)/10 = 0.29$ A.

The output capacitor of a boost sees pulsed current (zero during the on time), so boost output ripple is larger than a buck's for the same parts.

## CCM and DCM

The formulas above assume **continuous conduction mode** (CCM): the inductor current never falls to zero. At light load, the average current drops below half the ripple, and the current hits zero and stays there for part of each cycle: **discontinuous conduction mode** (DCM). For the buck, the boundary is

$$
I_o = \frac{\Delta i_L}{2}
$$

(0.51 A in the example). In DCM, the conversion ratio depends on the load, not just $D$, and the dynamics change, so the controller must cope with both. Many modern controllers deliberately switch to DCM or **pulse-skipping** at light load to save switching losses, which keeps efficiency high in standby.

## Key takeaways

- Buck $D$, boost $1/(1 - D)$, buck-boost $-D/(1 - D)$ (ideal, CCM).
- Buck ripple $\Delta i_L = (V_{in} - V_o)D/(Lf_s)$, $\Delta v_o = \Delta i_L/(8Cf_s)$; higher $f_s$ shrinks $L$ and $C$.
- Boost input current is $I_o/(1 - D)$; its output capacitor sees pulsed current.
- Below $I_o = \Delta i_L/2$ the converter enters DCM, where the ratio depends on load.
