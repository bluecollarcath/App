## From the grid to DC

Almost every electronic device starts by rectifying AC from the grid. At low power that's a diode bridge and a capacitor (Microelectronics I, Lesson 1.2); at industrial power, three-phase bridges feed motor drives, DC links, electrolysis plants and HVDC transmission. Power electronics adds two concerns: **controlling** the DC voltage, and the **quality** of the current drawn from the grid.

## Diode rectifiers

**Single-phase full-wave bridge** (resistive or inductive load): the average output is

$$
V_{dc} = \frac{2V_m}{\pi} = 0.637V_m = 0.9V_{rms}
$$

> 120 V RMS: $V_m = 169.7$ V and $V_{dc} = 108$ V.

**Three-phase six-pulse bridge**: six diodes, each pair conducting for 60° per cycle, so the output follows the highest line-to-line voltage, with ripple at 6 times line frequency:

$$
V_{dc} = \frac{3\sqrt2}{\pi}V_{LL} = 1.35V_{LL}
$$

> 480 V line-to-line gives $V_{dc} = 648$ V, with ripple at 360 Hz and only about 4% peak-to-peak even before filtering. That's why industrial drives use three-phase input.

## Controlled rectifiers

Replace the diodes with **thyristors** (SCRs): a thyristor conducts only after it's triggered by a gate pulse, and turns off when its current falls to zero. Delaying the gate pulses by a **firing angle** $\alpha$ after the natural conduction point lowers the average output:

$$
V_{dc} = 1.35V_{LL}\cos\alpha \quad\text{(three-phase, continuous current)}
$$

> With $\alpha = 30°$: $648\cos30° = 561$ V.

For $\alpha > 90°$ the average voltage goes **negative** while current still flows the same way, so power flows from the DC side back to the AC grid: **inverter mode**. That's how DC motors regenerate when braking, and how classic HVDC links transmit power in either direction.

## Power quality

A rectifier with a large filter capacitor draws current only near the peaks of the sine, in short, tall pulses. That current is rich in harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th...), which overheat transformers and neutral conductors, distort the grid voltage, and waste distribution capacity. Two measures:

**Total harmonic distortion** of the current, $\text{THD} = \sqrt{\sum_{h>1}I_h^2}/I_1$.

**Power factor**, the ratio of real power to apparent power. With sinusoidal voltage, only the fundamental current carries real power:

$$
\text{PF} = \underbrace{\frac{I_1}{I_{rms}}}_{\text{distortion factor}}\cdot\underbrace{\cos\phi_1}_{\text{displacement factor}}, \qquad \frac{I_1}{I_{rms}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \text{THD}^2}}
$$

> A capacitor-input rectifier with 80% current THD and no phase shift has $\text{PF} = 1/\sqrt{1.64} = 0.78$, even though its current is perfectly in phase.

**Power-factor correction (PFC).** Regulations (IEC 61000-3-2) limit harmonics for equipment above 75 W. The standard fix is a **boost converter** right after the bridge, controlled so that its input current follows the shape of the rectified voltage. The line current becomes almost sinusoidal (PF above 0.99), and the boost also delivers a regulated ~400 V DC bus to the next stage. Every laptop charger above 75 W, server supply and LED streetlight driver contains one.

## Key takeaways

- Single-phase bridge: $V_{dc} = 0.9V_{rms}$. Three-phase bridge: $1.35V_{LL}$, with 6× line-frequency ripple.
- Thyristor rectifiers: $V_{dc} = 1.35V_{LL}\cos\alpha$; $\alpha > 90°$ inverts power flow.
- $\text{PF} = \cos\phi_1/\sqrt{1 + \text{THD}^2}$; capacitor-input rectifiers have high THD.
- Boost PFC makes the input current sinusoidal and regulates the DC bus.
