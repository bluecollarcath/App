## Closing the loop

A converter running at a fixed duty cycle drifts whenever the input voltage, the load or the components change. Every practical supply measures its output and adjusts $D$ continuously. Designing that feedback loop means applying Control Systems (Units 2 and 3) to a circuit that **switches**, which first requires a model that doesn't.

## Averaging

Over one switching period, a switch and diode deliver an average voltage or current determined by $D$. Replacing them with that average, and treating $D$ as a slowly varying control input, gives an **averaged model** that's continuous and accurate up to about a fraction of the switching frequency. Linearizing it around the operating point, $d(t) = D + \hat d(t)$, gives small-signal transfer functions.

## The buck's control-to-output response

For the buck, the averaged switch network is just $dV_{in}$ driving the output LC filter and load $R$:

$$
G_{vd}(s) = \frac{\hat v_o}{\hat d} = \frac{V_{in}}{LCs^2 + \dfrac{L}{R}s + 1}
$$

A **double pole** at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, with quality factor $Q = R\sqrt{C/L}$. Above $f_0$ the gain falls at 40 dB/decade, and the phase swings by 180° near $f_0$, more sharply at light load (high $Q$).

> **Example**, the Lesson 1.2 buck: $L = 4.7\ \mu$H, $C = 22\ \mu$F, $V_{in} = 12$ V, $R = 3.3/2 = 1.65\ \Omega$: $f_0 = 15.7$ kHz, $Q = 3.6$, low-frequency gain 12 (21.6 dB), and a resonant peak of about $12Q = 43$ (32.6 dB).

## Voltage-mode compensation

The controller is an op-amp compensator $G_c(s)$ comparing the divided-down output with a reference. Design targets, from Control Systems Lesson 3.3:

- **crossover frequency** $f_c$ around $f_s/10$ to $f_s/5$ (fast response, but well below the switching ripple);
- **phase margin** 45–60°;
- an **integrator** for zero steady-state error.

Above $f_0$ the plant already has $-180°$ of phase, so the compensator must add **phase boost** near $f_c$: a **type III** compensator (an integrator plus two zeros and two poles) places two zeros near $f_0$ to cancel the double pole. In the example, with $f_s = 500$ kHz, a crossover near 50 kHz is a reasonable target.

## Boost and buck-boost: the right-half-plane zero

Their control-to-output responses have a **right-half-plane zero**. Increasing $D$ to raise the output first **reduces** the energy delivered to the output (the switch stays on longer, so the diode conducts for less time), before the inductor current has had time to build up. That initial wrong-way response, at

$$
\omega_{RHPZ} = \frac{R(1 - D)^2}{L} \quad\text{(boost)}
$$

adds phase lag that no compensator can cancel, so the crossover must stay well below it (typically under a third).

> **Example**, the Lesson 1.2 boost (12 Ω load, $D = 0.583$, $L = 10\ \mu$H): $f_{RHPZ} = 12(0.417)^2/(2\pi \times 10\times10^{-6}) = 33$ kHz, so crossover should be below about 10 kHz, much slower than a comparable buck.

## Current-mode control

Most modern controller ICs use **peak current-mode control**: an inner loop turns the switch off when the inductor current reaches a level set by the outer voltage loop. The inductor then behaves like a controlled current source, removing one pole of the double pole: the plant looks first-order, easy to compensate with a simple **type II** network, and it gains cycle-by-cycle current limiting for free. Above 50% duty cycle it needs **slope compensation** to avoid subharmonic oscillation.

## Key takeaways

- Averaging replaces switches with $D$-weighted averages; linearize for small-signal transfer functions.
- Buck $G_{vd} = V_{in}/(LCs^2 + (L/R)s + 1)$: double pole at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, $Q = R\sqrt{C/L}$.
- Voltage mode needs a type III compensator; aim for $f_c \approx f_s/10$ and 45–60° PM.
- Boost and buck-boost have an RHP zero at $R(1 - D)^2/L$ that caps bandwidth.
- Current-mode control simplifies compensation (type II) and limits current.
