## From DC to AC

An **inverter** turns DC into AC of controllable frequency and amplitude. Inverters drive every variable-speed motor (from washing machines to electric cars and locomotives), connect solar panels and batteries to the grid, and run UPS systems. The building block is a **half-bridge leg**: two switches in series across the DC bus, with the output taken from their midpoint. Turning one or the other on connects the output to the top or bottom rail; they must never both be on (that would short the bus, called **shoot-through**), so a short **dead time** separates their switching.

## Square-wave operation

Switching a **full bridge** (two legs) so that the load sees $+V_{dc}$ for half a cycle and $-V_{dc}$ for the other half gives a square wave. Its fundamental has peak

$$
V_{1,peak} = \frac{4}{\pi}V_{dc}
$$

> 400 V DC gives a 509 V peak fundamental.

It's simple and efficient, but its amplitude is fixed and it's full of low-order harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th at 1/3, 1/5, 1/7 of the fundamental) that a motor turns into heat and torque ripple.

## Sinusoidal PWM

Instead, switch each leg rapidly (kHz to tens of kHz) and vary the duty cycle sinusoidally: compare a sinusoidal **reference** at the desired output frequency with a high-frequency triangular **carrier**. The leg is high whenever the reference exceeds the carrier.

```plot
x: 0, 1, time (fundamental periods)
y: -1.3, 1.3, normalized
f: 0.8*sin(2*pi*x) | reference (m_a = 0.8)
f: 2*abs(2*mod(12*x, 1) - 1) - 1 | carrier (m_f = 12) | dot
f: 0.95*(2*u(0.8*sin(2*pi*x) - (2*abs(2*mod(12*x, 1) - 1) - 1)) - 1) | leg output (scaled) | dash
caption: Sinusoidal PWM: the leg switches high whenever the reference exceeds the carrier, so the local average of the pulses follows the sine.
```

The **local average** of the switched output follows the reference. The load's inductance (or an LC filter) smooths away the high-frequency switching harmonics, which sit around multiples of the carrier frequency, far from the fundamental, where they're easy to filter.

Two parameters set the output:

- **Amplitude modulation index** $m_a = \hat V_{ref}/\hat V_{carrier}$: the fundamental scales linearly with it, up to $m_a = 1$. Each leg's fundamental (relative to the DC midpoint) is $m_a\dfrac{V_{dc}}{2}$ peak.
- **Frequency modulation ratio** $m_f = f_{carrier}/f_1$: sets where the switching harmonics land.

**Three-phase inverters** use three legs with references 120° apart. The line-to-line RMS fundamental is

$$
V_{LL,rms} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2\sqrt2}m_aV_{dc} = 0.612\,m_aV_{dc}
$$

> 400 V DC at $m_a = 0.9$: $V_{LL} = 220$ V RMS, enough for a 208/230 V motor.

## Going further

- **Overmodulation** ($m_a > 1$): more output, but low-order harmonics return; the limit is the square wave.
- **Third-harmonic injection and space-vector PWM** (SVPWM): adding a common-mode component that cancels between phases lets the line voltages reach $2/\sqrt3 = 1.155$ times the SPWM limit, 15.5% more output from the same DC bus. SVPWM, which treats the eight switch states as vectors in a plane, is the standard in motor drives.
- **Multilevel inverters** (NPC, cascaded H-bridge) use more voltage levels to cut harmonics and device stress at medium voltage.
- **Grid-tied inverters** must synchronize to the grid with a PLL, control their current, and disconnect safely when the grid fails (anti-islanding).

## Key takeaways

- An inverter leg switches its output between the DC rails; dead time prevents shoot-through.
- Square wave: $V_1 = (4/\pi)V_{dc}$, fixed amplitude, strong low-order harmonics.
- SPWM: compare a sine reference with a triangular carrier; the fundamental scales with $m_a$; harmonics move near the carrier frequency.
- Three-phase: $V_{LL,rms} = 0.612m_aV_{dc}$; SVPWM adds 15.5%.
