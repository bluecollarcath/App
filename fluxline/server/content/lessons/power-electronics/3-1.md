## The switches that do the work

A converter is only as good as its switches. An ideal switch has zero voltage when on, zero current when off, and changes state instantly. Real devices lose power in two ways: **conduction loss** while on, and **switching loss** during each transition, when voltage and current overlap.

## The device families

| Device | Strengths | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Si MOSFET** | fast, easy to drive, resistive on-state | below ~200 V: chargers, DC-DC, low-voltage motor drives |
| **Si IGBT** | low conduction loss at high current and voltage; slower switching | 600–6500 V: motor drives, traction, welders, wind |
| **SiC MOSFET** | 10× higher breakdown field than Si; fast; runs hot | 650–1700 V: EV inverters and chargers, solar |
| **GaN HEMT** | extremely fast, tiny capacitances, no reverse recovery | 100–650 V: compact chargers, data-center supplies |
| **Thyristor** | cheapest per kVA; latches on, can't turn off by the gate | grid-frequency rectifiers, HVDC, soft starters |

Wide-bandgap devices (SiC, GaN; Semiconductor Devices, Lesson 1.1) are why a 100 W phone charger now fits in a pocket and why EV inverters are more efficient: they switch faster with less loss, allowing higher frequencies and smaller magnetics.

## Conduction loss

- **MOSFET:** a resistance when on, so $P_{cond} = I_{rms}^2R_{DS(on)}$. $R_{DS(on)}$ roughly doubles from 25 °C to 125 °C, so use the hot value.
- **IGBT:** a roughly constant voltage drop plus a small resistance, so $P_{cond} \approx V_{CE(sat)}I_{avg}$. At high current a 1.8 V drop beats a MOSFET's $I^2R$, which is why IGBTs rule at hundreds of amps.

## Switching loss

During each hard-switched transition, the device briefly carries the full current while supporting the full voltage. With a linear approximation:

$$
P_{sw} \approx \frac12V_{DS}I_D(t_r + t_f)f_s
$$

plus the energy in the device's output capacitance ($\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2$ per cycle) and the diode's **reverse recovery** charge. Switching loss rises **linearly with frequency**, which is the main limit on how fast converters can switch.

> **Example.** A MOSFET in a 400 V converter carries 10 A (RMS and average about equal), with $R_{DS(on)} = 20$ mΩ (hot), $t_r + t_f = 40$ ns and $f_s = 100$ kHz.
>
> Conduction: $10^2 \times 0.02 = 2$ W. Switching: $\tfrac12(400)(10)(40\times10^{-9})(10^5) = 8$ W. Total 10 W.
>
> Switching dominates; moving to GaN with 5 ns transitions would cut it to 1 W, or allow 8× the frequency at the same loss.

**Soft switching** (ZVS or ZCS, Lesson 1.3) arranges for the voltage or the current to be zero at the moment of switching, eliminating most of this loss.

## Thermal design

All that loss becomes heat, and the junction temperature must stay below its limit (150–175 °C, with margin). Heat flows through a chain of **thermal resistances** (°C/W): junction to case, case to heatsink (through a thermal pad or grease), heatsink to air:

$$
T_j = T_a + P\left(\theta_{jc} + \theta_{cs} + \theta_{sa}\right)
$$

> The 10 W device above, with a total of 3 °C/W, in 50 °C ambient air: $T_j = 50 + 30 = 80$ °C, comfortably safe.

It's exactly Ohm's law with temperature for voltage and power for current, and engineers draw thermal circuits the same way.

## Practice

```quiz
? A MOSFET with $R_{DS(on)} = 8$ mΩ carries 25 A RMS. What is its conduction loss, in W?
= 5 | 0.001 | W
> $25^2 \times 0.008$.
```

```quiz
? A switch hard-switches 48 V and 20 A with $t_r + t_f = 20$ ns at 500 kHz. What is the switching loss, in W?
= 4.8 | 0.001 | W
> $\tfrac12(48)(20)(20\times10^{-9})(5\times10^5)$.
```

```quiz
? An IGBT with $V_{CE(sat)} = 1.6$ V carries an average of 100 A. What is its conduction loss, in W?
= 160 | 0.001 | W
> $1.6 \times 100$.
```

```quiz
? A device dissipates 25 W with $\theta_{jc} = 0.8$, $\theta_{cs} = 0.4$ and $\theta_{sa} = 1.8$ °C/W in 40 °C air. What is $T_j$, in °C?
= 115 | 0.001 | °C
> $40 + 25(3.0)$.
```

```quiz
? Doubling the switching frequency of a hard-switched converter (everything else fixed) roughly:
+ doubles the switching loss but allows smaller inductors and capacitors
- halves the switching loss
- doubles the conduction loss
- has no effect on losses
> Switching loss is proportional to $f_s$.
```

```quiz
? Why are SiC MOSFETs preferred over Si MOSFETs for 800 V EV inverters?
+ SiC's high breakdown field gives low on-resistance at high voltage with fast switching
- SiC is cheaper than silicon
- SiC devices have no switching loss
- Silicon cannot be used above 50 V
> Wide-bandgap material breaks the silicon trade-off between blocking voltage and on-resistance.
```

## Key takeaways

- MOSFETs (fast, resistive), IGBTs (low drop at high current), SiC and GaN (fast and efficient at high voltage), thyristors (line frequency).
- Conduction loss: $I_{rms}^2R_{DS(on)}$ or $V_{CE(sat)}I_{avg}$. Switching loss $\approx \tfrac12VI(t_r + t_f)f_s$, proportional to frequency.
- Junction temperature $T_j = T_a + P\sum\theta$.
