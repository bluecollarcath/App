## Inductors and transformers you design yourself

Magnetic components are usually the largest, heaviest and most custom parts of a power converter, and often a major source of loss. Unlike resistors or capacitors, they're frequently designed for each converter: choose a core, a number of turns, a gap and a wire.

## Magnetic circuits

A core with a winding of $N$ turns behaves like a resistive circuit (Electromagnetics, Lesson 2.3):

- **magnetomotive force** $\mathcal F = NI$ plays the role of voltage;
- **flux** $\Phi$ plays the role of current;
- **reluctance** $\mathcal R = \ell/(\mu A)$ plays the role of resistance.

$$
\Phi = \frac{NI}{\mathcal R}, \qquad L = \frac{N^2}{\mathcal R}
$$

## Designing an inductor

A ferrite core alone has a huge permeability, so it saturates at a tiny current. Power inductors add an **air gap**: its reluctance, $\mathcal R_g = \ell_g/(\mu_0A_c)$, dwarfs the core's, so the inductance is set by the gap and is stable over temperature and core variations. The energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ is stored almost entirely in the gap.

The design must satisfy two constraints:

1. **No saturation.** The peak flux density, from $N\Phi = LI$:
$$B_{peak} = \frac{LI_{peak}}{NA_c} \le B_{max}$$
with $B_{max}$ about 0.3 T for ferrite (keeping margin below ~0.4 T saturation), more for powdered iron.
2. **The turns fit** in the core's window, with wire thick enough to keep $I_{rms}^2R$ loss acceptable.

> **Example.** $L = 100\ \mu$H, $I_{peak} = 5$ A, a core with $A_c = 1$ cm², $B_{max} = 0.3$ T.
>
> $N \ge LI_{peak}/(B_{max}A_c) = (100\times10^{-6})(5)/(0.3 \times 10^{-4}) = 16.7$, so 17 turns.
>
> Gap for the required inductance: $\ell_g = \mu_0N^2A_c/L = (4\pi\times10^{-7})(289)(10^{-4})/(10^{-4}) = 0.36$ mm.

## Designing a transformer

A transformer ideally stores no energy (no gap), and its flux is set by the **applied voltage**, not the current. From Faraday's law, a winding with $N$ turns driven by a voltage $V$ for a time $t$ swings the flux density by $\Delta B = Vt/(NA_c)$. For a symmetric square wave at frequency $f$ (as in a full bridge):

$$
V = 4fNB_{peak}A_c \qquad \text{(sine wave: } V_{rms} = 4.44fNB_{peak}A_c)
$$

Higher frequency means fewer turns or a smaller core for the same voltage, which is the whole reason switching converters are small.

> **Example.** A 48 V square wave at 100 kHz on a core with $A_c = 0.5$ cm² and $B_{peak} = 0.1$ T: $N = 48/(4 \times 10^5 \times 0.1 \times 0.5\times10^{-4}) = 24$ turns on the primary.

At high frequency the limit is usually **core loss**, not saturation, so $B_{peak}$ is set lower (0.05–0.15 T for ferrite at 100+ kHz).

## Losses

- **Core loss** (hysteresis and eddy currents) follows the empirical **Steinmetz equation**, $P_v = kf^\alpha B^\beta$, with $\alpha \approx 1.2$–$1.8$ and $\beta \approx 2.5$–$3$ for ferrites. Because of the steep $B$ dependence, halving the flux swing cuts core loss by 6–8×.
- **Copper loss** is $I_{rms}^2R$, but at high frequency **skin effect** (Electromagnetics, Lesson 3.2) and **proximity effect** (currents in neighboring turns pushing each other to the surface) raise the AC resistance far above DC. At 100 kHz copper's skin depth is 0.2 mm, so designers use litz wire, foil or PCB windings.

Optimal designs roughly balance core and copper loss.

## Key takeaways

- Magnetic circuits: $\Phi = NI/\mathcal R$, $L = N^2/\mathcal R$; an air gap sets $L$ and stores the energy.
- Inductor design: $N \ge LI_{peak}/(B_{max}A_c)$, then choose the gap.
- Transformers: $V = 4fNB_{peak}A_c$ (square wave); higher frequency means smaller cores.
- Losses: Steinmetz core loss $kf^\alpha B^\beta$; copper loss raised by skin and proximity effects.
