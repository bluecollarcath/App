## Why isolate?

Many power supplies need a **transformer** between input and output:

- **Safety:** an AC adapter's output must be galvanically isolated from the mains; touching the USB cable must never connect you to 230 V.
- **Large ratios:** converting 400 V to 1 V with a buck would need $D = 0.0025$, a 25 ns on-time at 100 kHz. A transformer does most of the ratio, so $D$ stays reasonable.
- **Multiple outputs:** extra windings give extra outputs (+5 V, +12 V, −12 V) from one converter.
- **Polarity:** outputs can float or be referenced anywhere.

The transformer must be driven with high-frequency AC, so isolated converters are built around a switching stage that chops the DC input, the transformer, and a rectifier and filter on the secondary. At 100 kHz the transformer is a tiny fraction of the size a 50/60 Hz one would be, which is why modern chargers are so small.

With turns ratio $n = N_s/N_p$:

| Converter | $V_o/V_{in}$ | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Flyback** | $n\dfrac{D}{1 - D}$ | derived from the buck-boost; fewest parts; up to ~100–150 W |
| **Forward** | $nD$ | derived from the buck; needs core reset, so $D \le 0.5$ for a 1:1 reset winding |
| **Push-pull, half- and full-bridge** | $2nD$ (per half cycle) | drive the core both ways; hundreds of watts to kilowatts |

## The flyback

The flyback's "transformer" is really a **coupled inductor**: during the on time, the primary stores energy in the core (the secondary diode is reverse biased); during the off time, the energy "flies back" through the secondary to the output. Its gapped core stores energy just like an inductor.

> **Example: 48 V to 5 V**, $n = 1/4$.
> $$5 = 48 \times \frac14 \times \frac{D}{1 - D} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{D}{1 - D} = 0.417, \quad D = 0.294$$
> While the switch is off, the primary sees the output reflected back, $V_o/n = 20$ V, on top of the input, so the switch must block $48 + 20 = 68$ V, plus a **leakage-inductance spike** that a snubber or clamp circuit must absorb.

Flybacks power nearly every phone charger, standby supply and LED driver: one switch, one magnetic part, one diode, and inherent isolation.

## The forward converter

A forward converter transfers energy **directly** through the transformer while the switch is on, followed by a buck-type output inductor. It's a buck with a transformer: $V_o = nDV_{in}$. Each cycle, the core's magnetizing current must be returned to zero (**reset**), or the flux ratchets up until the core saturates. With a reset winding of equal turns, reset takes as long as the on time, which limits $D \le 0.5$.

> 48 V to 5 V with $n = 1/4$: $D = 5/(48 \times 0.25) = 0.417$.

## Bridge converters

**Half-bridge** and **full-bridge** converters drive the transformer primary with a square wave of alternating polarity, so the core flux swings both ways, using the core fully and needing no reset winding. The full-bridge applies $\pm V_{in}$ to the primary using four switches. With **phase-shift** control and **soft switching** (zero-voltage switching, ZVS), the switches turn on with no voltage across them, eliminating most switching loss: the standard for kilowatt server supplies and EV chargers. The **LLC resonant converter** goes further and dominates high-efficiency supplies from laptop chargers to data centers.

## Key takeaways

- Isolation gives safety, large ratios, multiple outputs and floating outputs; high frequency keeps transformers small.
- Flyback: $nD/(1 - D)$, energy stored in a gapped coupled inductor; switch stress $V_{in} + V_o/n$ plus leakage spike.
- Forward: $nD$, needs core reset ($D \le 0.5$ with a 1:1 reset winding).
- Bridges drive the core both ways; soft switching (ZVS, LLC) gives top efficiency at high power.
