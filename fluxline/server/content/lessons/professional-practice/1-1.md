## Design is not analysis

Coursework mostly asks you to *analyze*: given a circuit, find the current. Engineering practice asks you to *design*: given a need, create something that meets it — with incomplete information, conflicting constraints, and a deadline. A disciplined process makes this repeatable.

## The process

1. **Define the problem.** Who is the customer? What do they actually need? Gather constraints: cost, size, power, safety standards, schedule, manufacturing volume.
2. **Write requirements.** Each should be *specific, measurable, and verifiable*, in "shall" form:
   - Weak: "The charger should be efficient."
   - Strong: "The charger **shall** achieve ≥ 90% efficiency from 20% to 100% rated load at 120 V AC input."
3. **Generate concepts.** Brainstorm several architectures before committing — a buck converter, a linear regulator, an off-the-shelf module.
4. **Evaluate and select** with a trade study (below).
5. **Detailed design**: schematics, calculations, simulation, PCB layout, firmware.
6. **Prototype and test.**
7. **Verify and validate**, then iterate.

## Verification vs. validation

- **Verification**: *Did we build the thing right?* Does the design meet each written requirement? (Tests, analysis, inspection.)
- **Validation**: *Did we build the right thing?* Does it meet the user's real need in the real environment?

A product can pass every verification test and still fail validation, if the requirements were wrong. The **V-model** pairs each design level (system → subsystem → component) with a corresponding test level.

## Weighted decision matrices

To compare concepts objectively, assign each criterion a weight (summing to 1), score each concept (e.g., 1–10), and compute the weighted sum:

| Criterion | Weight | Buck module | Custom buck | LDO |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Efficiency | 0.5 | 8 | 9 | 3 |
| Cost | 0.3 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
| Dev time | 0.2 | 9 | 4 | 10 |
| **Total** | | **7.6** | **7.7** | **6.5** |

The two buck options are nearly tied, which is itself useful information: refine the criteria or the scores, or let a secondary factor (supply-chain risk, team experience) decide. The matrix structures judgment; it doesn't replace it.

## Key takeaways

- Design is iterative: define → require → conceive → select → detail → test.
- Good requirements are specific, measurable, and verifiable.
- Verification checks the spec; validation checks the need.
