## From schematic to working board

Nearly every electronic product is built on a **printed circuit board** (PCB): layers of copper traces on fiberglass (FR-4), carrying components and connecting them. A circuit that works in simulation can fail on a board because of layout: noise, ground bounce, crosstalk, overheating traces or reflections. This lesson is an overview of the workflow and the rules that matter most.

## The workflow

1. **Schematic capture:** draw the circuit with symbols in an EDA tool (KiCad, Altium, OrCAD...), each symbol tied to a **footprint** (the copper pads the part solders to). Annotate reference designators (R1, C3, U2), values and ratings.
2. **Electrical rule check (ERC):** catch unconnected pins, outputs shorted together, missing power.
3. **Board setup:** outline, **layer stackup** (2, 4, 6+ layers), design rules from the fabricator (minimum trace width and spacing, drill sizes).
4. **Placement:** the most important step. Place connectors and mechanically constrained parts first, then group circuits by function, keeping critical loops short.
5. **Routing:** draw traces; power and critical signals first.
6. **Design rule check (DRC)**, review, then generate **Gerber** files (one per layer), drill files and a **bill of materials** (BOM) for fabrication and assembly.
7. **Bring-up:** power up carefully (current-limited supply), check rails, then test function by function.

## Grounding and return paths

Current flows in loops. Every signal's current returns to its source through the ground, and at high frequencies (above roughly 100 kHz) the return current flows in the ground **directly underneath** the signal trace, where the loop inductance is lowest.

- Use a **solid ground plane** (a full copper layer) in all but the simplest boards. A 4-layer stackup of signal / ground / power / signal is the usual minimum for anything with fast edges.
- **Don't cut the plane** under fast signals: a slot forces the return current around it, creating a large loop that radiates and picks up noise.
- Keep **loop areas small**: an antenna's coupling is proportional to its loop area.
- Separate noisy circuits (switching regulators, motor drivers) from sensitive ones (ADC inputs, oscillators) by **placement**, keeping their currents in their own regions of a common ground plane, rather than by splitting the ground.

## Decoupling

Digital chips draw current in sharp spikes on every clock edge. A **decoupling capacitor** next to each power pin supplies those spikes locally, so they don't travel through the long, inductive power path.

- Typical: 100 nF ceramic at each power pin, plus a few µF of bulk capacitance per chip or rail.
- **Placement matters more than value:** the capacitor's mounting inductance (traces and vias, about 1 nH) limits it. A 100 nF capacitor with 1 nH of total inductance self-resonates at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}) = 16$ MHz; above that it behaves like an inductor. Put it right at the pin, with vias straight down to the planes.

## Trace sizing and impedance

**Current capacity.** A trace heats up with $I^2R$. A 1 oz copper layer (35 µm thick) has a sheet resistance of about 0.5 mΩ/square. IPC-2221 charts give the width for a given current and temperature rise: about 30 mil (0.8 mm) for 2 A with a 10 °C rise on an outer layer.

> A 10 mil (0.25 mm) trace 1 inch long is 100 squares: $100 \times 0.5$ mΩ $= 50$ mΩ. Fine for signals; a problem for a 5 A supply.

**Controlled impedance.** Fast digital and RF traces are transmission lines (Electromagnetics, Unit 1). Their impedance depends on width, dielectric thickness and $\varepsilon_r$; a common approximation for a microstrip is

$$
Z_0 \approx \frac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r + 1.41}}\ln\frac{5.98h}{0.8w + t}
$$

> On FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r = 4.3$) with $h = 0.2$ mm and $w = 0.35$ mm (1 oz copper), $Z_0 \approx 49\ \Omega$. Fabricators calculate exact widths for your stackup. Route USB, Ethernet and DDR as impedance-controlled **differential pairs**, with length matching.

## Design for manufacturing and safety

- Follow the fabricator's minimums with margin; keep components a few millimeters from the board edge.
- Add **test points**, fiducials and clear silkscreen labels; check footprints against datasheets (the most common prototype error).
- Respect **creepage and clearance** distances between high-voltage and low-voltage circuits (several millimeters for mains; see Lesson 2.2).
- Manage heat: copper pours and thermal vias under hot parts.


## Key takeaways

- Workflow: schematic → ERC → stackup and rules → placement → routing → DRC → Gerbers and BOM → bring-up.
- Solid ground planes, unbroken return paths and small loops prevent most noise and EMI problems.
- Decouple every power pin locally; placement beats value.
- Size traces for current; route fast signals as controlled-impedance lines; design for manufacturing and safety.
