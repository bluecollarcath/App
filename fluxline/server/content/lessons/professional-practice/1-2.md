## Engineering inside a budget and a deadline

A brilliant design delivered a year late or 50% over budget is often a failed project. Project management gives engineers the tools to plan work, see which tasks drive the schedule, anticipate what could go wrong, and know early whether the project is on track.

## Breaking down the work

Start with a **work breakdown structure** (WBS): divide the project into deliverables, then into tasks small enough to estimate (days to a couple of weeks each). For each task, record its duration, its predecessors (what must finish first) and who does it. Engineers notoriously underestimate; integration, testing, documentation and rework are the usual forgotten tasks.

## The critical path

Draw the tasks as a network and find the **longest** path from start to finish: the **critical path**. Its length is the project's minimum duration. Any delay to a critical task delays the whole project; tasks off the critical path have **float** (slack) and can slip a little without harm.

> **Example.**
>
> | Task | Duration (weeks) | Predecessors |
> |---|---|---|
> | A: requirements | 3 | — |
> | B: circuit design | 5 | A |
> | C: enclosure design | 2 | A |
> | D: prototype and test | 4 | B, C |
>
> Paths: A–B–D = 12 weeks; A–C–D = 9 weeks. The critical path is A–B–D (12 weeks), and task C has 3 weeks of float. Adding engineers to the enclosure won't help; speeding up the circuit design will.

A **Gantt chart** shows the same plan as bars on a timeline, the standard way to communicate a schedule.

## Estimating with uncertainty

When durations are uncertain, **PERT** uses three estimates (optimistic $o$, most likely $m$, pessimistic $p$):

$$
t_e = \frac{o + 4m + p}{6}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{p - o}{6}
$$

> A task estimated at 2, 4 and 12 days has $t_e = 5$ days and $\sigma = 1.7$ days. The long pessimistic tail pulls the expectation above the "most likely" value, which is exactly why naive estimates run late.

## Risk management

List what could go wrong, then rank risks by **exposure**:

$$
\text{risk exposure} = \text{probability} \times \text{impact}
$$

> A 30% chance that a key chip goes out of stock, costing \$50,000 to redesign around: exposure \$15,000, which justifies buying a second source or stockpiling parts.

Responses: **avoid** (change the plan), **mitigate** (reduce probability or impact: prototypes, early testing, second sources), **transfer** (contracts, insurance) or **accept** (with a contingency reserve). Tackle the riskiest technical unknowns **first**, with quick prototypes, while changes are still cheap.

## Tracking progress: earned value

Comparing money spent with the plan is misleading unless you also measure work completed. **Earned value** management uses three numbers at a given date:

- **PV** (planned value): budgeted cost of work scheduled so far;
- **EV** (earned value): budgeted cost of work actually completed;
- **AC** (actual cost): what was actually spent.

$$
\text{CPI} = \frac{\text{EV}}{\text{AC}}, \qquad \text{SPI} = \frac{\text{EV}}{\text{PV}}, \qquad \text{EAC} = \frac{\text{BAC}}{\text{CPI}}
$$

> A \$100k project has PV = \$45k, EV = \$40k, AC = \$50k. CPI = 0.80 (getting 80 cents of work per dollar) and SPI = 0.89 (behind schedule). If the trend continues, the estimate at completion is $100/0.8 = 125$ thousand dollars.

## Key takeaways

- Build a WBS; the critical path (longest path) sets the duration; other tasks have float.
- PERT: $t_e = (o + 4m + p)/6$, $\sigma = (p - o)/6$.
- Rank risks by probability × impact; retire technical risks early with prototypes.
- Earned value: CPI = EV/AC, SPI = EV/PV, EAC = BAC/CPI.
