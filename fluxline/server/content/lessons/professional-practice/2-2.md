## Electricity can kill

It's not voltage that harms people, but **current** through the body, especially through the heart. Approximate effects of 60 Hz current, hand to hand or hand to foot, for about a second:

| Current | Effect |
|---|---|
| 1 mA | threshold of perception |
| 5 mA | maximum "harmless" current |
| 10–20 mA | "let-go" threshold: muscles clamp and you can't release the conductor |
| 50–100 mA | ventricular fibrillation possible: the heart stops pumping effectively |
| > 1 A | burns, cardiac arrest |

Body resistance varies from about 100 kΩ (dry skin) down to around 1 kΩ (wet skin, broken skin, or internal resistance once the skin is breached). So 120 V across wet hands can drive 120 mA, well into the fibrillation range. **Mains voltage is lethal.** DC is somewhat less dangerous at the same current, but high-voltage DC (EV batteries, solar strings) is just as deadly.

## Protection: grounding, bonding and GFCIs

- **Equipment grounding:** exposed metal parts are connected to earth through the green or bare grounding conductor. If a live wire touches the case, a large fault current flows and trips the breaker, instead of leaving the case live waiting for a person to touch it.
- **Bonding:** metal parts that could become energized are connected together so no dangerous voltage can appear between them.
- **Double insulation:** two independent layers of insulation (the "square in a square" symbol), used for tools with two-prong plugs.
- **GFCI / RCD:** compares the current in the hot and neutral conductors; a difference means current is leaking somewhere, possibly through a person. It trips at 4–6 mA (US GFCI) or 30 mA (typical RCD) within a few tens of milliseconds, fast enough to prevent fibrillation. Required in bathrooms, kitchens, outdoors and wet locations.
- **Arc-fault breakers (AFCI):** detect the signature of arcing in damaged wiring, a major cause of house fires.

## Working safely

- **Lockout/tagout (LOTO):** before working on equipment, de-energize it, lock the disconnect, tag it, and **verify** it's dead with a tested meter ("test before touch").
- **Arc flash:** a fault in high-energy equipment can produce an explosion of plasma at thousands of degrees. NFPA 70E requires arc-flash risk assessment, labels and appropriate protective equipment.
- **Capacitors** can stay charged long after power is removed; discharge and verify them.
- On the bench: one hand in your pocket around high voltage, isolated supplies or isolation transformers, current-limited supplies for bring-up, and never work alone on hazardous circuits.

## Codes and standards

| Body / document | Covers |
|---|---|
| **NEC** (NFPA 70) | U.S. building wiring: conductor ampacity, overcurrent protection, grounding, GFCI/AFCI requirements |
| **NFPA 70E** | electrical safety in the workplace, arc flash |
| **NESC** | utility power lines and substations |
| **IEC** (e.g. IEC 62368-1) | international product safety for electronics; creepage and clearance distances |
| **UL** | U.S. product safety testing and certification |
| **IEEE** standards | technical practice (e.g. 802.11 Wi-Fi, 1547 grid interconnection, 519 harmonics) |
| **FCC Part 15 / CE marking** | electromagnetic emissions and conformity for products |

Codes are legal requirements where adopted; standards represent agreed best practice. Products sold to the public need safety certification (UL, CE) and EMC compliance.

## Voltage drop: a code-driven calculation

The NEC recommends limiting voltage drop to about 3% on a branch circuit (5% including the feeder). The round-trip resistance of a run is $2LR'$.

> A 120 V, 20 A load at the end of a 100 ft run of 12 AWG copper (1.59 Ω per 1000 ft): $R = 2(100)(0.00159) = 0.318\ \Omega$, so the drop is $20 \times 0.318 = 6.4$ V, which is 5.3%: too much. Upsize to 10 AWG (1.0 Ω per 1000 ft) for 4.0 V, 3.3%.

## Key takeaways

- Current kills: 10–20 mA prevents let-go, 50–100 mA can cause fibrillation; mains voltage is lethal.
- Grounding, bonding, double insulation, GFCIs (4–6 mA) and AFCIs protect people and property.
- Lockout/tagout, test before touch, arc-flash precautions, discharge capacitors.
- Know the codes (NEC, 70E, NESC) and product standards (IEC/UL, FCC/CE); limit voltage drop to about 3%.
