## Engineering is a writing job

Engineers spend a large share of their time communicating: requirements, design reviews, test reports, datasheets, emails that decide what gets built. Excellent work that can't be understood, checked or reproduced has little value. Clear technical writing is a skill, and it can be learned.

## Know your reader and lead with the answer

Before writing, ask who will read it and what they need to **decide or do**. A manager needs the conclusion, cost and risk; a reviewing engineer needs the method and data; a technician needs step-by-step instructions.

Put the most important information **first**. Busy readers may read only the first paragraph:

> **Weak:** "This report describes tests performed on the regulator in the lab over three weeks using several loads..."
>
> **Strong:** "The new regulator meets every requirement except load-transient response, which overshoots 8% (spec: 5%). Increasing the output capacitor to 47 µF fixes it at a cost of \$0.12 per board."

## The technical report

The standard structure:

1. **Title and abstract** (or executive summary): the problem, what was done, the key results and recommendation, in one paragraph.
2. **Introduction:** background, objectives and requirements.
3. **Method:** design approach or test setup, in enough detail that someone else could reproduce it: equipment, conditions, procedure.
4. **Results:** data, as tables and figures, with uncertainty.
5. **Discussion:** what the results mean, how they compare with requirements and predictions, limitations.
6. **Conclusions and recommendations.**
7. **References and appendices** (raw data, code, derivations).

## Figures, tables and numbers

- Every figure needs labeled axes **with units**, a legend if there's more than one trace, and a caption that says what to notice.
- Choose the plot that answers the question: Bode plots on log-frequency axes, time-domain responses on linear ones.
- Report numbers with sensible **significant figures** that reflect the measurement's precision: "3.3 V ± 2%", not "3.29874 V".
- Always state units and use SI prefixes consistently (mA, kΩ, µF).
- Compare measured with expected values and give the error:

$$
\text{percent error} = \frac{|\text{measured} - \text{expected}|}{\text{expected}} \times 100\%
$$

> A regulator designed for 5.00 V measures 4.85 V: a 3.0% error. Is that within the requirement? The report must say.

## Writing clearly

- Short sentences, active voice, concrete words: "We measured the ripple" rather than "Measurements of the ripple were performed."
- Define acronyms on first use; be consistent with terms.
- Separate **facts** (what you measured), **inferences** (what you think it means) and **recommendations** (what should be done).
- Don't hide bad news; state it plainly with its impact and a plan.

## Presentations and documentation

- **Design reviews:** one message per slide, a title that states the conclusion, minimal text, large readable plots. Practice, and finish on time. Anticipate questions with backup slides.
- **Engineering notebooks** and version-controlled repositories record decisions and data with dates; they're essential for debugging, team handoffs and patent disputes.
- **Requirements and specifications** must be testable (Lesson 1.1). Datasheets and user manuals are read by people under time pressure: tables, clear limits, and conditions for every number.

## Practice

```quiz
? A filter's measured cutoff is 1.04 kHz; the design value is 1.00 kHz. What is the percent error?
= 4 | 0.001 | %
> $0.04/1.00 \times 100\%$.
```

```quiz
? A resistor measures 4.65 kΩ against a nominal 4.70 kΩ. What is the percent error?
= 1.064 | 0.005 | %
> $0.05/4.70 \times 100\%$.
```

```quiz
? What belongs in the first paragraph of a test report?
+ the key results and recommendation
- a detailed list of the test equipment
- the history of the project
- raw data tables
> Lead with the answer; details follow.
```

```quiz
? Which is the best way to report a measured voltage of 3.29874 V from a meter accurate to ±1%?
+ 3.30 V ± 1%
- 3.29874 V
- about 3 V
- 3.3 V exactly
> Significant figures should reflect the measurement's precision.
```

```quiz
? A good figure caption:
+ tells the reader what the figure shows and what to notice
- repeats the axis labels
- is left out if the figure is clear
- contains only a figure number
> Readers often skim figures and captions first.
```

## Key takeaways

- Write for a specific reader and lead with the conclusion.
- Report structure: abstract, introduction, method, results, discussion, conclusions.
- Figures need labeled axes with units and informative captions; use sensible significant figures and state errors.
- Be clear and honest: active voice, defined terms, facts separated from inferences, bad news stated plainly.
