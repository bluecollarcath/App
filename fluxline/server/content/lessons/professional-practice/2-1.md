## Why engineers have a code of ethics

Engineers' decisions affect people who never see the drawings: the passengers in an elevator, the families near a substation, the users of a medical device. Society grants engineering its authority on the understanding that engineers will protect the public. Professional codes of ethics spell out that duty.

## The NSPE Code: the fundamental canons

The National Society of Professional Engineers' Code of Ethics opens with six canons. Engineers, in fulfilling their professional duties, shall:

1. **Hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public.**
2. Perform services only in areas of their competence.
3. Issue public statements only in an objective and truthful manner.
4. Act for each employer or client as faithful agents or trustees.
5. Avoid deceptive acts.
6. Conduct themselves honorably, responsibly, ethically and lawfully, so as to enhance the honor, reputation and usefulness of the profession.

The ordering matters: the duty to the **public comes first**. When a client's or employer's interests conflict with public safety, safety wins. The IEEE Code of Ethics makes similar commitments (including to disclose factors that might endanger the public, avoid conflicts of interest, and reject bribery).

## Licensure

In the United States, engineering that affects public safety (building systems, power distribution, many public works) must be approved by a licensed **Professional Engineer** (PE). The usual path:

1. an ABET-accredited engineering degree;
2. passing the **Fundamentals of Engineering** (FE) exam (Unit 3), becoming an Engineer in Training (EIT);
3. about **four years** of progressive experience under a PE;
4. passing the **Principles and Practice of Engineering** (PE) exam in a discipline, such as Electrical and Computer: Power.

A PE's seal on a drawing is a personal, legal statement of responsibility. Many electronics and software engineers work under an "industrial exemption" without a license, but the ethical duties apply to everyone.

## Lessons from failures

- **Hyatt Regency walkway collapse (1981):** a late change to the hanger-rod design doubled the load on a connection; it wasn't properly checked. 114 people died, and the engineers of record lost their licenses. Lesson: changes need the same review as the original design.
- **Space Shuttle Challenger (1986):** engineers warned that the O-rings were unsafe at the forecast low temperature; managers overrode them. Lesson: engineering judgment about safety must not be overruled by schedule pressure, and engineers must escalate.
- **Citicorp Center (1978):** after completion, the structural engineer, William LeMessurier, discovered that the building could fail in a strong wind. He reported it himself, and the joints were secretly reinforced before hurricane season. Lesson: owning your mistakes protects the public, and it is the honorable path.
- **Boeing 737 MAX (2018–19):** a flight-control system relied on a single sensor, and its behavior was not fully disclosed to pilots or regulators. Two crashes killed 346 people. Lesson: safety-critical design needs redundancy, honest disclosure and independent review.

## Working through a dilemma

A practical approach:

1. Get the facts. What exactly is the risk, and how sure are you?
2. Identify the stakeholders and the obligations to each, starting with the public.
3. Consider the options, including raising the issue internally, documenting it, and escalating.
4. Act, and record what you did and why.

If internal channels fail and the public is at risk, codes direct engineers to notify the proper authorities: **whistleblowing**, which is protected by law in many cases.

Everyday ethics matter too: honest test reports, crediting others' work, refusing gifts that could influence a specification, protecting confidential information, and not signing off on work you didn't check.

## Key takeaways

- Public safety, health and welfare come first; then competence, honesty, faithful service and honorable conduct.
- PE licensure: degree, FE exam, ~4 years' experience, PE exam; the seal means personal responsibility.
- Failures like Hyatt Regency, Challenger and the 737 MAX show the cost of unreviewed changes, schedule pressure and poor disclosure.
- Resolve dilemmas with facts, stakeholders, options and documented action; escalate when the public is at risk.
