## Delivering power

Gain stages handle tiny currents into high-resistance loads. A speaker, motor or antenna needs **amps** into a few ohms. The output stage's job is to deliver that current with low output resistance, low distortion, and as little wasted power as possible. Efficiency matters twice: wasted power drains batteries, and it all becomes heat in the transistors.

$$
\eta = \frac{P_L}{P_S} = \frac{\text{power delivered to the load}}{\text{power drawn from the supplies}}
$$

## Class A

An emitter (or source) follower biased with a constant current larger than the peak load current. The transistor conducts over the **whole** cycle, so the output is clean, but the bias current flows all the time, signal or no signal.

The maximum efficiency of a class A follower (with a current-source bias and full-swing output) is only **25%**, and at idle it's 0%: all the supply power becomes heat. Class A is used where linearity matters more than power, such as in small-signal stages and audiophile amplifiers.

## Class B: push-pull

Use two complementary transistors, each handling half the waveform. The NPN **pushes** current into the load on positive half-cycles; the PNP **pulls** it out on negative ones. At zero signal both are off and draw nothing.

```circuit
LINE 2.6,-0.2 3.4,-0.2
TEXT 3,-0.5 +V_{CC}
W 3,-0.2 3,0
NPN 3,1 Q_N
PNP 3,3 Q_P
W 3,4 3,4.2
LINE 2.6,4.2 3.4,4.2
TEXT 3,4.55 −V_{CC}
W 2,1 2,3
DOT 2,2
W 0.6,2 2,2
T 0.6,2 v_{in}
DOT 3,2
W 3,2 4.6,2
R 4.6,2 4.6,4 R_L
GND 4.6,4
TEXT 4.9,1.7 v_{out} anchor=start color=anno size=13
caption: A class B complementary push-pull stage. Each transistor is an emitter follower for one half of the waveform.
```

**Efficiency.** For a sine output of peak $V_p$ with supplies $\pm V_{CC}$:

$$
P_L = \frac{V_p^2}{2R_L}, \qquad
P_S = \frac{2V_{CC}V_p}{\pi R_L}, \qquad
\eta = \frac{\pi}{4}\,\frac{V_p}{V_{CC}}
$$

At full swing ($V_p \approx V_{CC}$) the maximum is $\pi/4 = 78.5\%$, three times better than class A.

**Heat.** The total transistor dissipation $P_S - P_L$ is **not** largest at full output. It peaks at $V_p = 2V_{CC}/\pi$ (64% of full swing), where

$$
P_{D,max} = \frac{2V_{CC}^2}{\pi^2R_L} \quad\text{(both transistors together)}
$$

That's the number heatsinks are sized for.

> **Worked example.** $\pm15$ V supplies, an 8 Ω speaker, a 12 V peak sine.
>
> $P_L = 144/16 = 9.0$ W; $P_S = 2(15)(12)/(8\pi) = 14.3$ W; $\eta = 62.8\%$ (check: $\frac{\pi}{4}\cdot\frac{12}{15}$).
>
> Worst-case dissipation: $2(225)/(\pi^2 \cdot 8) = 5.70$ W total, 2.85 W per transistor.

## Crossover distortion

Each transistor needs about $0.7$ V of $V_{BE}$ before it conducts. Near zero, for $|v_{in}| < 0.7$ V, **neither** conducts: the output sits at 0 while the input passes through. The result is a notch at every zero crossing, called crossover distortion. It's especially audible at low volume, where the dead zone is a large fraction of the signal.

```plot
x: 0, 2, t (ms)
y: -2.2, 2.2, voltage (V)
f: 2*sin(pi*x) | input | dot
f: (2*sin(pi*x) - 0.7)*u(2*sin(pi*x) - 0.7) + (2*sin(pi*x) + 0.7)*u(-2*sin(pi*x) - 0.7) | class B output
caption: Crossover distortion with a 2 V peak input: the output is stuck at zero for $|v_{in}| < 0.7$ V.
```

Placing the stage inside an op-amp's feedback loop reduces the notch by the loop gain, but it can't fully remove it at high frequencies.

## Class AB

The standard fix: bias both transistors **slightly on** at idle, by inserting about $2V_{BE}$ between the bases, usually with two diodes or a "$V_{BE}$ multiplier." Now, around zero, both conduct a small quiescent current and hand over smoothly. The efficiency is barely lower than class B, and the distortion is far lower. Nearly every audio amplifier and op-amp output stage is class AB.

Small emitter resistors (often 0.1–0.5 Ω) are added to stabilize the idle current against **thermal runaway**: as transistors heat up, $V_{BE}$ falls by about 2 mV/°C, which would raise the current and the heating even further. Mounting the bias diodes on the same heatsink provides thermal tracking.

Beyond AB, **class D** amplifiers switch the transistors fully on or off at hundreds of kilohertz (pulse-width modulation) and filter the result, reaching 90%+ efficiency. That's covered in Power Electronics.

## Key takeaways

- Class A: always conducting, linear, at most 25% efficient.
- Class B push-pull: $\eta = \frac{\pi}{4}V_p/V_{CC}$, up to 78.5%; worst dissipation $2V_{CC}^2/(\pi^2R_L)$ at $V_p = 2V_{CC}/\pi$.
- Class B suffers crossover distortion; class AB biases both devices slightly on to remove it.
- Emitter resistors and thermal tracking prevent thermal runaway.
