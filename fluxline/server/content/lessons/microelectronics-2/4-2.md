## Amplifiers that are supposed to oscillate

The last two lessons fought to keep feedback loops from oscillating. An **oscillator** does it on purpose: positive feedback at exactly one frequency turns noise into a steady sine wave. Every radio, microcontroller clock and phase-locked loop starts with one.

## The Barkhausen criterion

An amplifier $A$ with a frequency-selective feedback network $\beta(j\omega)$, connected so the feedback **adds** to the input, sustains an oscillation at the frequency $\omega_0$ where

$$
A\beta(j\omega_0) = 1
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
|A\beta| = 1 \ \text{ and } \ \angle A\beta = 0°\ (\text{or } 360°)
$$

In practice:

- **Start-up:** make $|A\beta|$ slightly **greater** than 1, so a tiny noise component at $\omega_0$ grows.
- **Limiting:** some nonlinearity must reduce the effective gain to exactly 1 as the amplitude grows. Otherwise the output grows until it clips hard against the supply rails. Good oscillators use gentle, controlled limiting for a clean sine.

## The Wien-bridge oscillator

```circuit
OA 4,1
W 3,0.5 2.6,0.5
DOT 2.6,0.5
W 2.6,0.5 2.6,-0.6
R 2.6,-0.6 5.6,-0.6 R_2
W 5.6,-0.6 5.6,3.4
W 5,1 5.6,1
DOT 5.6,1
W 5.6,1 6.4,1
T 6.4,1 v_o
W 2.6,0.5 1.6,0.5
R 1.6,0.5 1.6,2.2 R_1 side=-1
GND 1.6,2.2
R 5.6,3.4 4.1,3.4 R side=-1
C 4.1,3.4 2.4,3.4 C side=-1
DOT 2.4,3.4
W 2.4,3.4 2.4,1.5 3,1.5
R 2.4,3.4 2.4,5 R
W 2.4,3.4 1.2,3.4
C 1.2,3.4 1.2,5 C side=-1
W 1.2,5 2.4,5
DOT 1.8,5
GND 1.8,5
caption: A Wien-bridge oscillator. The series and parallel RC arms (bottom) feed back to the + input; $R_1$ and $R_2$ set the gain.
```

The feedback network is a series RC followed by a parallel RC. Its transfer function to the + input is

$$
\beta(j\omega) = \frac{1}{3 + j\left(\omega RC - \dfrac{1}{\omega RC}\right)}
$$

At $\omega_0 = 1/RC$ the imaginary part vanishes: zero phase shift and $\beta = 1/3$. So

$$
f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}, \qquad A = 1 + \frac{R_2}{R_1} = 3 \ \Rightarrow\ R_2 = 2R_1
$$

> **Example.** $R = 10$ kΩ and $C = 15.9$ nF give $f_0 = 1.00$ kHz. Choose $R_1 = 10$ kΩ and $R_2$ slightly above 20 kΩ to start reliably.

Hewlett and Packard's first product (1939) was a Wien-bridge audio oscillator that used a small incandescent **lamp** as $R_1$. As the amplitude grew, the filament warmed, its resistance rose, and the gain settled at exactly 3. Modern versions use back-to-back diodes across part of $R_2$, or a JFET as a voltage-controlled resistor.

The **RC phase-shift oscillator** is a cousin: an inverting amplifier plus three RC sections, each contributing $60°$ at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt6RC)$, where the network attenuates by 29, so the amplifier needs a gain of at least 29.

## LC oscillators

Above about 1 MHz, RC networks and op-amps run out of steam, and **LC tanks** take over. A tank resonates at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, with low loss and a sharp phase change. A single transistor supplies the gain.

- **Colpitts:** the tank capacitance is split into $C_1$ and $C_2$, with the tap providing the feedback. $C_{eq} = \dfrac{C_1C_2}{C_1 + C_2}$ and $f_0 = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC_{eq}}}$.
- **Hartley:** the inductance is split instead ($L_{eq} = L_1 + L_2$ plus mutual inductance).

> **Example.** A Colpitts oscillator with $L = 10\ \mu$H and $C_1 = C_2 = 2$ nF: $C_{eq} = 1$ nF, $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{10^{-14}}) = 1.59$ MHz.

## Crystal oscillators

A quartz crystal is a piezoelectric resonator: mechanically, it vibrates; electrically, it behaves like a series RLC (the "motional" arm, with a huge $L$ and a tiny $C$) in parallel with its holder capacitance. Its **Q** is 10,000 to 1,000,000, versus about 100 for an LC tank, so its frequency is pinned to within parts per million. The series resonance is

$$
f_s = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_mC_m}}
$$

and the parallel resonance lies just slightly above it. In a **Pierce oscillator**, the crystal replaces the inductor of a Colpitts circuit, with a CMOS inverter as the amplifier. That's the clock circuit inside nearly every microcontroller. A 32.768 kHz watch crystal divides down to exactly 1 Hz after 15 halvings.

> **Example.** Motional parameters $L_m = 10$ mH, $C_m = 25$ fF give $f_s = 10.07$ MHz.

## Ring oscillators

An odd number $N$ of inverters in a loop has no stable state, so it oscillates with period $2Nt_d$ (each edge travels around the ring twice per period):

$$
f = \frac{1}{2Nt_d}
$$

Ring oscillators are cheap, tunable and fully on-chip, but noisy, so they're used inside PLLs that lock them to a crystal reference. Five stages with 20 ps delay each run at 5 GHz.

## Key takeaways

- Barkhausen: oscillation at the frequency where $A\beta = 1$ (unit magnitude, zero phase); start with $|A\beta|$ slightly above 1.
- Wien bridge: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi RC)$, $\beta = 1/3$, so the gain must be 3.
- LC (Colpitts, Hartley) for RF; crystals for ppm-level accuracy; ring oscillators for on-chip clocks inside PLLs.
