## The input stage of every op-amp

The **differential pair** amplifies the *difference* between two inputs and ignores what they have in common. That one property makes it the input stage of nearly every op-amp, comparator, and high-speed serial receiver. Noise picked up equally on both wires (hum, ground bounce, supply ripple) is rejected; only the signal you want gets through.

Write the inputs as a **differential** part and a **common-mode** part:

$$
v_{id} = v_{G1} - v_{G2}, \qquad v_{icm} = \frac{v_{G1} + v_{G2}}{2}
$$

## Large-signal behavior

At balance ($v_{id} = 0$) each transistor carries $I_{SS}/2$. As $v_{id}$ grows, current steers from one side to the other, until one transistor takes all of $I_{SS}$ at

$$
|v_{id}| = \sqrt2\,V_{ov}
$$

where $V_{ov}$ is the overdrive at balance. Beyond that the pair is fully switched, which is exactly how it works in a comparator or a current-mode logic gate. For linear amplification, keep $|v_{id}|$ well below this.

## Differential gain

For small signals, $v_{id}/2$ appears across each transistor's $v_{gs}$, with opposite signs, and the tail node (the common source) stays still: a **virtual ground** for differential signals. Each half is then just a common-source stage:

$$
A_d = \frac{v_{o2} - v_{o1}}{v_{id}} = g_mR_D \quad\text{(differential output)}, \qquad
\frac{v_{o2}}{v_{id}} = \frac{g_mR_D}{2} \quad\text{(single-ended)}
$$

with $g_m = 2I_D/V_{ov} = I_{SS}/V_{ov}$.

## Common-mode gain and CMRR

A common-mode input moves both gates together. The tail source has a finite output resistance $R_{SS}$, so the tail node moves too, and a little current flows. Splitting $R_{SS}$ into two $2R_{SS}$ halves, each side is a degenerated CS stage:

$$
A_{cm} = \frac{v_{o1}}{v_{icm}} \approx -\frac{R_D}{2R_{SS}}
$$

The **common-mode rejection ratio** compares the gain you want to the gain you don't:

$$
\text{CMRR} = \left|\frac{A_d}{A_{cm}}\right| \approx g_mR_{SS} \quad\text{(single-ended output)}
$$

With a differential output and perfectly matched halves, the common-mode outputs cancel and CMRR is infinite in theory; in practice, mismatch limits it to 60–100 dB.

> **Worked example.** $I_{SS} = 0.4$ mA, $V_{ov} = 0.2$ V, $R_D = 10$ kΩ, $R_{SS} = 100$ kΩ.
>
> $I_D = 0.2$ mA, $g_m = 2(0.2)/0.2 = 2$ mS.
>
> $A_d = g_mR_D = 20$ (differential output), or 10 single-ended.
>
> $A_{cm} = -10/(2 \times 100) = -0.05$, so $\text{CMRR} = 10/0.05 = 200$ (46 dB), equal to $g_mR_{SS}$.
>
> The pair stays linear for $|v_{id}| \ll \sqrt2(0.2) = 0.28$ V.

Raising $R_{SS}$ (a cascode tail source) is the main way to improve CMRR.

## The active load

Resistor loads waste gain and headroom. Replace them with a **current-mirror load** ($M_3$–$M_4$, PMOS): the mirror copies $M_1$'s signal current to the output side, where it adds to $M_2$'s. The result is a differential-to-single-ended conversion with **no** loss of half the signal:

$$
A_d = \frac{v_{out}}{v_{id}} = g_{m1}\,(r_{o2} \parallel r_{o4})
$$

With $g_m = 2$ mS and $r_o = 100$ kΩ for both devices, $A_d = 2\ \text{mS} \times 50\ \text{k}\Omega = 100$ (40 dB) from a single stage. This actively loaded pair is the first stage of the classic two-stage op-amp (next lesson).

## Key takeaways

- A differential pair amplifies $v_{id}$ and rejects $v_{icm}$; the tail is a virtual ground for differential signals.
- $A_d = g_mR_D$ (differential out); $A_{cm} \approx -R_D/2R_{SS}$; $\text{CMRR} \approx g_mR_{SS}$.
- The pair switches fully at $|v_{id}| = \sqrt2V_{ov}$.
- A current-mirror load gives $A_d = g_m(r_{o2} \parallel r_{o4})$ with a single-ended output.
