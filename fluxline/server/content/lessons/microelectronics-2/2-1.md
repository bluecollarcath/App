## Why gain falls at high frequencies

Microelectronics I explained the **low**-frequency rolloff: coupling and bypass capacitors that stop being short circuits. At the **high** end, the limit comes from inside the transistor itself. Every junction and every gate stores charge, and charge storage means capacitance. Those tiny capacitors, femtofarads to picofarads, eventually short the signal away.

## The MOSFET at high frequency

Add three capacitances to the small-signal model:

- $C_{gs}$, gate to source: mostly the channel charge. In saturation $C_{gs} \approx \tfrac23WLC_{ox}$, plus overlap.
- $C_{gd}$, gate to drain: only the small overlap capacitance in saturation, but it straddles the gain and gets multiplied by it (the **Miller effect**, next lesson).
- $C_{db}$ (and $C_{sb}$): reverse-biased junctions from drain and source to the substrate.

The BJT has the same structure with different names: $C_\pi$ (base–emitter, mostly the diffusion capacitance of the forward-biased junction, which grows with $I_C$) in place of $C_{gs}$, and $C_\mu$ (the collector–base junction) in place of $C_{gd}$, plus a small base resistance $r_x$.

## Transition frequency $f_T$

The standard figure of merit for a transistor's speed is its **unity-current-gain frequency** $f_T$. Short the output to AC ground and drive the gate with a current $i_i$. The input current splits into $C_{gs}$ and $C_{gd}$, and the output current is $g_mv_{gs}$, so

$$
\frac{i_o}{i_i} \approx \frac{g_m}{j\omega(C_{gs} + C_{gd})}
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
\boxed{f_T = \frac{g_m}{2\pi(C_{gs} + C_{gd})}}
$$

$f_T$ is the frequency at which the current gain falls to 1. For a BJT it's the same formula with $C_\pi + C_\mu$.

> **Worked example.** A MOSFET has $g_m = 2$ mS, $C_{gs} = 20$ fF and $C_{gd} = 5$ fF:
> $$
> f_T = \frac{2\times10^{-3}}{2\pi(25\times10^{-15})} = 12.7\ \text{GHz}
> $$

For a long-channel MOSFET, using $g_m = \mu_nC_{ox}(W/L)V_{ov}$ and $C_{gs} = \tfrac23WLC_{ox}$:

$$
f_T \approx \frac{3\mu_nV_{ov}}{4\pi L^2}
$$

Speed rises with overdrive and falls with the **square** of the channel length. That $1/L^2$ is a big reason the industry kept shrinking transistors: modern RF CMOS devices exceed 300 GHz.

## The BJT's current gain vs. frequency

A BJT's $\beta$ is flat at low frequency, then rolls off at 20 dB/decade above the **beta cutoff** $f_\beta$, reaching 1 at $f_T$:

$$
\beta(f) = \frac{\beta_0}{1 + jf/f_\beta}, \qquad f_T \approx \beta_0f_\beta
$$

```plot
caption: Current gain of a BJT with $\beta_0 = 100$ and $f_T = 10$ GHz. It falls 3 dB at $f_\beta = 100$ MHz, then 20 dB/decade, crossing 0 dB at $f_T$.
```

$f_T$ is a ceiling, not a working bandwidth: a real amplifier, with gain and loads, has a bandwidth far below $f_T$. The next lesson shows how to estimate it.

## Key takeaways

- High-frequency rolloff comes from device capacitances: $C_{gs}$, $C_{gd}$, $C_{db}$ (BJT: $C_\pi$, $C_\mu$).
- $f_T = g_m/[2\pi(C_{gs} + C_{gd})]$ is the frequency where current gain reaches 1.
- Long-channel $f_T \propto \mu V_{ov}/L^2$; BJT $f_T \approx \beta_0f_\beta$.
