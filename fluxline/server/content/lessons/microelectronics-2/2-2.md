## Finding the upper corner $f_H$

An amplifier with several internal capacitors has several poles. Solving the full circuit by hand is painful. Two shortcuts make it easy: the **Miller theorem**, which untangles a capacitor that bridges input and output, and the **open-circuit time-constant** (OCTC) method, which estimates $f_H$ from simple resistance calculations.

## The Miller effect

An impedance $Z$ connected between two nodes whose voltages are related by $v_2 = Av_1$ can be replaced by an impedance from each node to ground:

$$
Z_1 = \frac{Z}{1 - A}, \qquad Z_2 = \frac{Z}{1 - 1/A}
$$

For a capacitor across an **inverting** gain $A = -|A_v|$, the input-side capacitance becomes

$$
C_M = C_{gd}\,(1 + |A_v|)
$$

A tiny $C_{gd}$ across a gain of 20 looks 21 times larger from the input. The reason: as the input rises by $v$, the output falls by $20v$, so the capacitor sees $21v$ across it and draws 21 times the current.

```circuit

R 0,0 2,0 R_{sig}
W 2,0 2.8,0
C 2.8,0 2.8,2 "C_{gs} + C_M" side=-1
DOT 2.8,0
W 2.8,0 3.6,0
T 3.6,0 v_{gs}
W 0,2 3.6,2
DOT 2.8,2
caption: The input side of a common-source stage after the Miller transformation. $R_{sig}$ and the total input capacitance form a low-pass corner.
```

> **Worked example: common-source stage.** $R_{sig} = 10$ kΩ, $g_m = 2$ mS, $R_L' = 10$ kΩ (so $A_v = -20$), $C_{gs} = 20$ fF, $C_{gd} = 5$ fF.
>
> $C_M = 5(1 + 20) = 105$ fF; $C_{in} = 20 + 105 = 125$ fF.
>
> $$f_H \approx \frac{1}{2\pi R_{sig}C_{in}} = \frac{1}{2\pi(10\ \text{k}\Omega)(125\ \text{fF})} = 127\ \text{MHz}$$
>
> The 5 fF $C_{gd}$ contributes 84% of the input capacitance.

The Miller approximation uses the **midband** gain, so it ignores the output-side pole and the way the gain itself falls with frequency. It works well when the input pole clearly dominates.

## Open-circuit time constants

A more general estimate. For each capacitor $C_j$, set all **other** capacitors to open circuits, find the resistance $R_j$ that $C_j$ sees, and add the time constants:

$$
\tau_{total} = \sum_j R_jC_j, \qquad f_H \approx \frac{1}{2\pi\tau_{total}}
$$

The estimate is usually a little **low** (conservative), and very good when one pole dominates.

For the common-source stage, the three resistances are:

| Capacitor | Resistance it sees |
|---|---|
| $C_{gs}$ | $R_{sig}$ |
| $C_{gd}$ | $R_{sig}(1 + g_mR_L') + R_L'$ |
| $C_L$ (load, plus $C_{db}$) | $R_L'$ |

> **Example, continued,** now with a 50 fF load capacitance:
>
> - $C_{gs}$: $10\ \text{k}\Omega \times 20\ \text{fF} = 0.20$ ns
> - $C_{gd}$: $\big(10\ \text{k}(21) + 10\ \text{k}\big) \times 5\ \text{fF} = 220\ \text{k}\Omega \times 5\ \text{fF} = 1.10$ ns
> - $C_L$: $10\ \text{k}\Omega \times 50\ \text{fF} = 0.50$ ns
>
> $\tau_{total} = 1.80$ ns, so $f_H \approx 88.4$ MHz. (The exact dominant pole of this circuit is 92.4 MHz, so the estimate is within 5%.)

The table also shows **which capacitor to attack**. Here $C_{gd}$'s term is 61% of the total. To speed up the stage, reduce its effective multiplication.

## Beating Miller: the cascode

A **cascode** stacks a common-gate transistor on top of the common-source device. The CS transistor now drives the CG transistor's low input resistance $1/g_{m2}$, so its own drain gain is only about $-g_{m1}/g_{m2} \approx -1$. The Miller multiplication of $C_{gd1}$ drops from $1 + |A_v|$ to about 2, while the overall gain is preserved (or increased, thanks to the cascode's high output resistance). Cascodes are everywhere in wideband and RF amplifiers.

The other cures are a low source resistance $R_{sig}$ (drive the stage from a follower), or a stage with no inverting gain across a bridging capacitor, such as common-gate or common-drain.

## Key takeaways

- Miller: a capacitor across inverting gain $A_v$ appears as $C(1 + |A_v|)$ at the input.
- OCTC: $f_H \approx 1/(2\pi\sum R_jC_j)$, with each $R_j$ found with the other capacitors open.
- For a CS stage, $C_{gd}$ sees $R_{sig}(1 + g_mR_L') + R_L'$, usually the dominant term.
- A cascode (or a low $R_{sig}$) suppresses the Miller effect.
