## Trading gain for everything else

Transistor gain is large but unreliable: it drifts with temperature, varies 2:1 from chip to chip, and changes with signal level (that's distortion). **Negative feedback** trades some of that abundant gain for precision, linearity, bandwidth and controlled impedances. Harold Black's 1927 insight, sketched on a newspaper on the ferry to Bell Labs, is why op-amp circuits work.

```circuit

SUM 1,0 l=+ b=-
ARROW 1.28,0 2.6,0 x_i
BOX 3.6,0 2,1 A

DOT 5.6,0
LINE 5.6,0 5.6,1.6
ARROW 5.6,1.6 4.6,1.6
BOX 3.6,1.6 2,1 β
LINE 2.6,1.6 1,1.6
ARROW 1,1.6 1,0.28 x_f
caption: The general feedback structure: an amplifier $A$ and a feedback network $\beta$ that returns a fraction of the output.
```

## The feedback equation

With $x_i = x_s - \beta x_o$ and $x_o = Ax_i$:

$$
A_f = \frac{x_o}{x_s} = \frac{A}{1 + A\beta}
$$

The product $T = A\beta$ is the **loop gain**, and $1 + A\beta$ is the **amount of feedback**. When $T \gg 1$:

$$
A_f \approx \frac{1}{\beta}
$$

The gain is set by the feedback network (usually precise resistors) and hardly at all by the amplifier.

## What feedback buys, and what it costs

Each benefit is a factor of $1 + A\beta$; the cost is the same factor of gain.

- **Gain desensitivity:** $\dfrac{dA_f}{A_f} = \dfrac{1}{1 + A\beta}\,\dfrac{dA}{A}$.
- **Bandwidth extension:** for a single-pole amplifier, $f_{Hf} = f_H(1 + A_0\beta)$, so the **gain–bandwidth product stays constant**.
- **Less distortion:** nonlinearity inside the loop is reduced by $1 + A\beta$.
- **Noise and disturbances** injected inside the loop (but not at the input) are reduced by $1 + A\beta$.
- **Impedances** are multiplied or divided by $1 + A\beta$, depending on the topology (below).

> **Worked example.** $A = 10^4$, $\beta = 0.01$: $A_f = 10^4/101 = 99.0$. If $A$ drops 10% (to 9000), $A_f$ changes by only $10\%/101 = 0.099\%$.

> **Bandwidth example.** An op-amp with $A_0 = 10^5$ and a single pole at 10 Hz has a gain–bandwidth product of 1 MHz. With $\beta = 0.01$, the closed-loop gain is $10^5/1001 = 99.9$ and the bandwidth becomes $10 \times 1001 = 10$ kHz.

## The four topologies

Feedback can **sample** the output voltage (shunt connection at the output) or the output current (series), and **mix** at the input as a voltage (series) or a current (shunt). The name lists the input mixing first, then the output sampling:

| Topology | Amplifier type | $R_{in}$ | $R_{out}$ | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Series–shunt | voltage amp (V/V) | $\times(1 + T)$ | $\div(1 + T)$ | non-inverting op-amp |
| Shunt–series | current amp (A/A) | $\div(1 + T)$ | $\times(1 + T)$ | current mirror with feedback |
| Series–series | transconductance (A/V) | $\times(1 + T)$ | $\times(1 + T)$ | CE stage with emitter resistor |
| Shunt–shunt | transresistance (V/A) | $\div(1 + T)$ | $\div(1 + T)$ | inverting op-amp, TIA |

The rule to remember: **series** connections multiply resistance by $1 + T$, **shunt** connections divide it. Feedback pushes each impedance toward the ideal for that amplifier type.

> **Example.** A voltage amplifier with $A = 1000$, $R_i = 10$ kΩ and $R_o = 100\ \Omega$ gets series–shunt feedback with $\beta = 0.1$. Then $1 + T = 101$: $A_f = 9.90$, $R_{if} = 1.01$ MΩ, $R_{of} = 0.99\ \Omega$.

## Key takeaways

- $A_f = A/(1 + A\beta) \approx 1/\beta$ when the loop gain $T = A\beta$ is large.
- Feedback divides sensitivity, distortion and in-loop disturbances by $1 + T$, and multiplies bandwidth by it (GBW constant).
- Series connections multiply resistance by $1 + T$; shunt connections divide it.
