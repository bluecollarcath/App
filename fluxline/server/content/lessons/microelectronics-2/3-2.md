## When negative feedback turns positive

Every real amplifier has more than one pole, and each pole adds up to $90°$ of phase lag. With enough lag, the signal fed back arrives **in phase** with the input: negative feedback becomes positive, and the amplifier oscillates. Compensation is the art of keeping the loop stable while giving up as little speed as possible.

## The stability condition

The closed-loop gain $A/(1 + A\beta)$ blows up when $A\beta = -1$: loop gain magnitude 1 with $-180°$ of phase. As in Control Systems (Lesson 3.3), measure the safety margin at the frequency $f_u$ where $|A\beta| = 1$:

$$
\text{PM} = 180° + \angle A\beta(f_u)
$$

- $\text{PM} \ge 45°$ is the minimum for a usable amplifier; $60°$ is the usual target, giving a clean step response with about 9% overshoot.
- A single-pole loop always has $\text{PM} \ge 90°$ and can never oscillate. Two poles can approach $180°$ but never reach it; **three** poles can exceed it.

The **worst case is $\beta = 1$** (a unity-gain buffer), because the loop gain is largest and $f_u$ is highest, where the most phase lag has accumulated. An op-amp described as "unity-gain stable" is compensated for that case, so it's stable for any resistive feedback.

## Dominant-pole compensation

The simplest fix: add a pole at a very low frequency so the gain falls to 1 **before** the second pole contributes much phase. The loop then behaves like a single-pole system up to $f_u$. The cost is bandwidth: the open-loop gain starts falling at a few hertz or kilohertz.

## Miller compensation and pole splitting

In the two-stage op-amp (Lesson 1.3), connect a capacitor $C_c$ across the second (inverting) stage. The Miller effect multiplies it by the second-stage gain $A_2$, creating a huge effective capacitance at the first-stage output: a **low-frequency dominant pole** from a small, on-chip capacitor. At the same time, at high frequency $C_c$ acts as a short that diode-connects the second stage, pushing its output pole **higher**. The two poles move apart, which is called **pole splitting**.

The key results:

$$
\omega_u = \text{GBW} \approx \frac{g_{m1}}{C_c}, \qquad
p_2 \approx \frac{g_{m6}}{C_L}, \qquad
z_{RHP} = \frac{g_{m6}}{C_c}
$$

For about $60°$ phase margin, place the second pole at $p_2 \approx 2.2\,\omega_u$, which gives

$$
C_c \ge 2.2\,\frac{g_{m1}}{g_{m6}}\,C_L
$$

> **Worked example.** $g_{m1} = 1$ mS, $g_{m6} = 10$ mS, $C_L = 10$ pF, $A_0 = 10^4$.
>
> $C_c = 2.2(1/10)(10\ \text{pF}) = 2.2$ pF.
>
> $f_u = g_{m1}/(2\pi C_c) = 72.3$ MHz; the dominant pole sits at $f_u/A_0 = 7.2$ kHz; $p_2$ is at $g_{m6}/(2\pi C_L) = 159$ MHz.
>
> Phase margin $\approx 90° - \tan^{-1}(72.3/159) = 65.6°$. The right-half-plane zero at $g_{m6}/(2\pi C_c) = 723$ MHz, ten times $f_u$, costs another $\tan^{-1}(0.1) = 5.7°$, leaving about $60°$.

## The right-half-plane zero

$C_c$ also provides a direct feedforward path from the first stage to the output. It produces a **zero in the right half-plane**, which raises the gain like an ordinary zero but **lags** the phase like a pole: the worst of both. Two cures:

- A **nulling resistor** $R_z$ in series with $C_c$. With $R_z = 1/g_{m6}$, the zero moves to infinity; with a larger $R_z$, it moves into the left half-plane, where its phase *lead* can cancel $p_2$.
- A buffer (or a cascode node) that blocks the feedforward current.

## Key takeaways

- Oscillation happens where $|A\beta| = 1$ and $\angle A\beta = -180°$; aim for $\text{PM} \approx 60°$.
- The unity-gain buffer is the worst case for stability.
- Miller compensation splits poles: $\omega_u = g_{m1}/C_c$, $p_2 = g_{m6}/C_L$; choose $C_c \ge 2.2(g_{m1}/g_{m6})C_L$.
- The RHP zero $g_{m6}/C_c$ is tamed with a nulling resistor.
