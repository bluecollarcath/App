## Currents make magnetic fields

Ørsted discovered in 1820 that a current deflects a compass needle: **moving charges create magnetic fields**. Two laws compute those fields, with the constant $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ T·m/A.

## The Biot–Savart law

Each small piece of current-carrying wire $d\boldsymbol\ell$ contributes a field

$$
d\mathbf B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}
$$

Add (integrate) the contributions of the whole circuit. Two classic results:

- **Center of a circular loop** of radius $R$: $B = \dfrac{\mu_0I}{2R}$ (every piece is the same distance away and contributes in the same direction).
- **Long straight wire**: $B = \dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}$.

> A 5 cm-radius loop carrying 2 A: $B = (4\pi\times10^{-7})(2)/(0.1) = 25\ \mu$T at its center, about half the Earth's field.

The field lines circle the wire (right-hand rule: thumb along the current, fingers curl with the field). Magnetic field lines never begin or end; they always form closed loops.

## Ampère's law

For symmetric situations, a shortcut: around any closed path, the circulation of $\mathbf B$ equals $\mu_0$ times the current passing through it:

$$
\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = \mu_0I_{enc}
$$

**Long wire:** a circle of radius $r$ gives $B \cdot 2\pi r = \mu_0I$, the result above in one line.

**Solenoid** (a long coil with $n$ turns per meter): the field inside is uniform and the field outside nearly zero, so a rectangular path half inside gives

$$
B = \mu_0nI
$$

> 1000 turns over 50 cm ($n = 2000$/m) carrying 1 A: $B = 2.5$ mT.

**Toroid** ($N$ turns on a doughnut): the field stays entirely inside the core, $B = \dfrac{\mu_0NI}{2\pi r}$. Toroidal inductors and transformers leak very little field, which is why they're used where electromagnetic interference matters.

With an iron or ferrite core, replace $\mu_0$ by $\mu = \mu_r\mu_0$, multiplying the field by hundreds or thousands (until saturation).

## Forces between currents

Each wire sits in the other's field, so parallel wires carrying currents $I_1$ and $I_2$, a distance $d$ apart, exert a force per unit length

$$
\frac FL = \frac{\mu_0I_1I_2}{2\pi d}
$$

**attracting** when the currents are in the same direction and **repelling** when opposite. This force once defined the ampere. In power systems, short-circuit currents of tens of kiloamperes make busbars and transformer windings slam together, so they're braced mechanically.

> Two busbars 10 cm apart, each carrying 20 kA in a fault: $F/L = (2\times10^{-7})(4\times10^8)/0.1 = 800$ N/m.

## Key takeaways

- Biot–Savart: $d\mathbf B = (\mu_0/4\pi)I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2$; loop center $\mu_0I/2R$.
- Ampère: $\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = \mu_0I_{enc}$; wire $\mu_0I/2\pi r$, solenoid $\mu_0nI$, toroid $\mu_0NI/2\pi r$.
- Parallel currents attract with $F/L = \mu_0I_1I_2/2\pi d$; opposite currents repel.
