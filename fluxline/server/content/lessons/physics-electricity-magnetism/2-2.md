## Charge in motion

**Current** is the rate at which charge passes a point:

$$
I = \frac{dQ}{dt}
$$

in amperes (C/s). By convention it flows in the direction positive charges would move, opposite to the actual motion of electrons in a metal.

## Drift velocity

In a metal, free electrons zip around randomly at about $10^6$ m/s, colliding constantly with the lattice. An applied field adds a tiny average **drift** along the wire. With $n$ free electrons per cubic meter, each of charge $q$, drifting at $v_d$ through a cross-section $A$:

$$
I = nqAv_d
$$

> Copper has $n = 8.5\times10^{28}$ m⁻³. In a 1 mm² wire carrying 1 A:
> $$v_d = \frac{1}{(8.5\times10^{28})(1.6\times10^{-19})(10^{-6})} = 7.3\times10^{-5}\ \text{m/s}$$
> about a quarter of a meter per hour. Yet a light turns on instantly, because the **field** (the signal) travels along the wire at nearly the speed of light, setting all the electrons moving at once.

## Current density and Ohm's law

The **current density** $J = I/A$ (A/m²) is related to the local field by the material's **conductivity** $\sigma$ (or its inverse, **resistivity** $\rho$):

$$
\mathbf J = \sigma\mathbf E = \frac{\mathbf E}{\rho}
$$

This is Ohm's law at a point. For a uniform wire of length $L$ and cross-section $A$, it becomes the familiar circuit form:

$$
V = IR, \qquad R = \frac{\rho L}{A}
$$

| Material | $\rho$ (Ω·m) at 20 °C |
|---|---|
| Silver | $1.59\times10^{-8}$ |
| Copper | $1.68\times10^{-8}$ |
| Aluminum | $2.65\times10^{-8}$ |
| Nichrome | $1.1\times10^{-6}$ |
| Silicon (pure) | ~$2.3\times10^3$ |
| Glass | $10^{10}$–$10^{14}$ |

> 10 m of 1 mm² copper: $R = (1.68\times10^{-8})(10)/10^{-6} = 0.168\ \Omega$. At 10 A it drops 1.7 V and dissipates 17 W, which is why wire gauges are chosen for the current.

## Temperature dependence

For metals, resistance rises roughly linearly with temperature:

$$
R = R_0\big[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)\big]
$$

with $\alpha \approx 0.0039$ /°C for copper: a motor winding 50 °C above ambient has about 20% more resistance. Platinum resistance thermometers (RTDs) use this effect; semiconductors behave the opposite way (Semiconductor Devices, Lesson 1.3).

## Power

Charges falling through a potential difference $V$ give up energy $qV$, so a current $I$ delivers power

$$
P = IV = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}
$$

In a resistor it all becomes heat (Joule heating): useful in heaters and fuses, wasteful in transmission lines.

## Key takeaways

- $I = dQ/dt = nqAv_d$; drift velocities are tiny, but signals travel at nearly light speed.
- $\mathbf J = \sigma\mathbf E$; $R = \rho L/A$; metal resistance rises about 0.4%/°C.
- $P = IV = I^2R = V^2/R$.
