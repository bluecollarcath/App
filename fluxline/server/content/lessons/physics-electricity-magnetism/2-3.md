## Where circuits get their energy

A battery, generator or solar cell does work on charges to move them "uphill" against the field, from low potential to high. That work per unit charge is the **electromotive force** (emf), $\mathcal E$, measured in volts. (It's not a force; the name is historical.) In a battery, chemical reactions provide the energy; in a generator, mechanical work through magnetic induction (Lesson 3.3).

## Internal resistance

Every real source has **internal resistance** $r$, so its terminal voltage sags under load:

$$
V_{terminal} = \mathcal E - Ir, \qquad I = \frac{\mathcal E}{R + r}
$$

> A 9 V battery with 1.5 Ω internal resistance drives a 10 Ω load: $I = 9/11.5 = 0.78$ A and $V_{terminal} = 7.8$ V. The 1.2 V "missing" heats the battery.

As batteries age or get cold, $r$ rises, which is why a weak car battery reads 12 V on a meter but collapses when you crank the engine. The maximum power the source can deliver is $\mathcal E^2/4r$, into a load $R = r$ (Calculus, Lesson 1.4): 13.5 W for this battery.

## Kirchhoff's laws from physics

Circuit analysis rests on two conservation laws:

- **Kirchhoff's current law** (KCL): charge is conserved, and in steady state none piles up at a junction, so the currents entering a node sum to zero.
- **Kirchhoff's voltage law** (KVL): the electrostatic field is conservative (Lesson 1.4), so the potential changes around any closed loop sum to zero: emfs supply what resistors drop.

KVL holds in circuits because their fields are essentially electrostatic; with rapidly changing magnetic fields it needs Faraday's correction (Lesson 3.3), which is exactly what an inductor's voltage $L\,di/dt$ accounts for.

## Where the energy actually flows

Intuition says energy flows **through** the wires, carried by the electrons. Physics says otherwise: the energy flows through the **space around** the wires, carried by the electric and magnetic fields, and enters the resistor through its surface. The flow is described by the **Poynting vector** $\mathbf S = \mathbf E\times\mathbf H$ (Electromagnetics, Lesson 3.2). For a resistor carrying current $I$ with voltage $V$, the inward flux of $\mathbf S$ through its surface is exactly $VI$. The wires guide the energy; they don't carry it inside. This viewpoint becomes essential for transmission lines, antennas and PCB signal integrity, where fields clearly do the work.

## Batteries in practice

Battery capacity is quoted in ampere-hours (A·h) or milliampere-hours; the stored energy is capacity times average voltage.

> A 3000 mA·h, 3.7 V lithium-ion cell stores $3 \times 3.7 = 11.1$ W·h $= 40$ kJ. At an average draw of 500 mA it lasts about 6 hours.

Batteries in series add voltages (and internal resistances); in parallel they add capacity, but must be matched to avoid circulating currents.

## Key takeaways

- emf is work per charge done by a source; real sources have internal resistance: $V = \mathcal E - Ir$.
- KCL = charge conservation; KVL = conservative electrostatic field.
- Energy flows through the fields around wires (Poynting vector), entering loads through their surfaces.
- Battery energy = capacity × voltage.
