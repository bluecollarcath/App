## Counting field lines

The **electric flux** through a surface measures how much field passes through it. For a uniform field and a flat area $A$ whose normal makes an angle $\theta$ with the field, $\Phi_E = EA\cos\theta$; in general,

$$
\Phi_E = \int\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A
$$

## Gauss's law

The total flux out of **any closed surface** depends only on the charge inside it:

$$
\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A = \frac{Q_{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

Field lines start and end on charges, so a surface with no charge inside has as many lines entering as leaving. Gauss's law is always true, but it's **useful** for calculating fields only when symmetry makes $E$ constant over a cleverly chosen surface, so it comes out of the integral.

## Three symmetric cases

**Point charge (spherical symmetry).** Choose a sphere of radius $r$: $E \cdot 4\pi r^2 = Q/\varepsilon_0$, so $E = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}$: Coulomb's law falls out in one line.

**Line charge (cylindrical symmetry).** Choose a cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$; only its curved side has flux: $E \cdot 2\pi rL = \lambda L/\varepsilon_0$, so $E = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r}$.

**Infinite sheet (planar symmetry).** Choose a "pillbox" straddling the sheet; flux leaves both faces: $2EA = \sigma A/\varepsilon_0$, so $E = \dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$.

> A sheet with $\sigma = 1\ \mu$C/m² produces $E = 10^{-6}/(2 \times 8.85\times10^{-12}) = 56.5$ kV/m on each side.

**Parallel plates.** Two sheets with $+\sigma$ and $-\sigma$: their fields add between the plates and cancel outside, giving a uniform $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ between them. That's the capacitor of Lesson 2.1.

## Conductors in equilibrium

In a conductor, free charges move until they feel no force, so in electrostatic equilibrium:

- $\mathbf E = 0$ **inside** the conductor;
- any excess charge sits on the **surface** (Gauss's law with a surface just inside encloses nothing);
- just outside, the field is **perpendicular** to the surface, with $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$;
- charge concentrates where the surface is sharply curved, so fields are strongest at points (why lightning rods are pointed, and why high-voltage hardware has smooth, rounded edges to prevent corona).

A closed conducting shell shields its interior from outside fields: a **Faraday cage**. That's why cars protect occupants from lightning, why microwave ovens have a metal mesh, and why sensitive circuits sit inside metal enclosures.

## Key takeaways

- Gauss's law: closed-surface flux $= Q_{enc}/\varepsilon_0$; use symmetric Gaussian surfaces.
- Point $kQ/r^2$; line $\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0r$; sheet $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$; parallel plates $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$.
- Conductors: $E = 0$ inside, charge on the surface, $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ just outside, perpendicular; Faraday cages shield.
