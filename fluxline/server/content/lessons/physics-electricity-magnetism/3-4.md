## A circuit that opposes its own changes

A coil carrying current produces flux through itself. If the current changes, the flux changes, and Faraday's law induces an emf in the same coil, opposing the change (Lenz). The **self-inductance** $L$ measures this:

$$
L = \frac{N\Phi_B}{I}, \qquad v = L\frac{di}{dt}
$$

in henries (V·s/A). The second equation is the inductor law of circuit analysis: an inductor resists changes in current, the way mass resists changes in velocity.

## Inductance of a solenoid

With $B = \mu_0nI$ inside a solenoid of $N$ turns, length $\ell$ and area $A$:

$$
L = \frac{\mu_0N^2A}{\ell}
$$

Inductance grows with the **square** of the turns. A magnetic core multiplies it by $\mu_r$.

> 1000 turns, 1 cm² area, 50 cm long (air core): $L = (4\pi\times10^{-7})(10^6)(10^{-4})/0.5 = 0.25$ mH.

Inductance also appears where you don't want it: every wire and PCB trace has roughly 1 nH per millimeter, which is why fast current changes ($L\,di/dt$) produce voltage spikes and ground bounce in digital circuits, and why decoupling capacitors must sit right at chip pins.

## Energy in the magnetic field

Building up current in an inductor takes work against the induced emf, stored as

$$
U = \frac12LI^2
$$

> The 0.25 mH solenoid at 2 A stores $\tfrac12(0.25\times10^{-3})(4) = 0.5$ mJ.

That energy resides in the magnetic field, with density

$$
u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}
$$

(compare $u_E = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ for electric fields). A 1 T field stores about 400 kJ/m³. When the current is suddenly interrupted, this energy has to go somewhere: the voltage $L\,di/dt$ spikes until something arcs or breaks down. That's the kick that fires a car's spark plug, and the reason relay coils and motor windings need **flyback diodes** across them to protect the transistors that switch them.

## Mutual inductance

When two coils share flux, a changing current in one induces an emf in the other:

$$
v_2 = M\frac{di_1}{dt}
$$

$M$ is the **mutual inductance**, at most $\sqrt{L_1L_2}$ (for perfect coupling). Mutual inductance is the principle of the **transformer** (tightly coupled coils on a shared core; Electric Machines, Lesson 1.2), wireless phone chargers and RFID tags (loosely coupled coils), and current sensors. It's also the mechanism of **crosstalk** between neighboring wires and traces.

## RL circuits

Inductors make circuits respond with a time constant $\tau = L/R$ (Calculus, Lesson 1.3): switch on a voltage $V$ across a series RL, and the current rises as $I = (V/R)(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$. Together with capacitors (which store energy in electric fields), inductors make the resonant LC circuits of Differential Equations, Lesson 2.3: energy sloshing between the electric and magnetic fields.

## Key takeaways

- $L = N\Phi/I$, $v = L\,di/dt$; solenoid $L = \mu_0N^2A/\ell$ (∝ $N^2$).
- Energy $\tfrac12LI^2$, stored in the field at $B^2/2\mu_0$; interrupted currents spike, so use flyback diodes.
- Mutual inductance $v_2 = M\,di_1/dt$: transformers, wireless charging, crosstalk.
- RL time constant $L/R$; L and C together resonate.
