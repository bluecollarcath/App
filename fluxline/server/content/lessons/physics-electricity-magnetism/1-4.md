## Energy instead of force

Forces and fields are vectors; energy is a scalar, which makes it easier to work with. The **electric potential** $V$ is the potential energy per unit charge:

$$
\Delta U = q\,\Delta V, \qquad V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
$$

measured in volts (J/C). Only **differences** in potential matter physically; we choose a reference (ground, or infinity) where $V = 0$. This "voltage" is exactly what a voltmeter measures and what circuit analysis is built on.

## Potential of point charges

With $V = 0$ at infinity:

$$
V = \frac{kQ}{r}
$$

Potentials of several charges simply **add** as numbers, with no vector components: a big advantage over fields.

> Two charges, $+4$ nC and $-2$ nC, are each 0.2 m from point P: $V_P = (8.99\times10^9)(4 - 2)\times10^{-9}/0.2 = 89.9$ V.

## The electron-volt

Accelerating a charge through a potential difference gives it kinetic energy $q\Delta V$. The **electron-volt** (eV) is the energy an electron gains crossing 1 V: $1.602\times10^{-19}$ J. Semiconductor bandgaps (1.12 eV for silicon) and photon energies are quoted in eV.

> An electron accelerated through 100 V gains 100 eV $= 1.6\times10^{-17}$ J, reaching $v = \sqrt{2K/m} = 5.9\times10^6$ m/s, about 2% of the speed of light.

## Field from potential

Going the other way, the field is the **downhill slope** of the potential:

$$
\mathbf E = -\nabla V, \qquad E_x = -\frac{dV}{dx}
$$

Fields point from high to low potential and are strongest where the potential changes fastest. Between parallel plates separated by $d$ with voltage $V$, the field is uniform:

$$
E = \frac Vd
$$

> 12 V across plates 3 mm apart gives 4000 V/m. A MOSFET's 1 V gate voltage across a 2 nm oxide gives $5\times10^8$ V/m, close to silicon dioxide's breakdown strength.

## Equipotentials

**Equipotential surfaces** connect points of equal potential. The field is always perpendicular to them, and moving a charge along one takes no work. A conductor in equilibrium is an equipotential (Lesson 1.3): every point of a wire in an ideal circuit sits at the same voltage.

## Key takeaways

- $\Delta U = q\Delta V$; $V = kQ/r$ for point charges, adding as scalars.
- 1 eV $= 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J, the energy gained crossing 1 V.
- $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$; parallel plates: $E = V/d$.
- Equipotentials are perpendicular to field lines; conductors are equipotentials.
