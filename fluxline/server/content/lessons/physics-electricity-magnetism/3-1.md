## A force that depends on motion

A magnetic field $\mathbf B$ (in teslas) exerts a force only on **moving** charges, and the force is perpendicular to both the velocity and the field:

$$
\mathbf F = q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B, \qquad F = qvB\sin\theta
$$

Combined with the electric force, this is the **Lorentz force** $\mathbf F = q(\mathbf E + \mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$. Use the right-hand rule for direction (fingers along $\mathbf v$, curl toward $\mathbf B$, thumb gives the force on a positive charge; reverse it for electrons).

Typical field strengths: Earth, 50 µT; a fridge magnet, 5 mT; a neodymium magnet's surface, about 1 T; an MRI scanner, 1.5–7 T.

## Circular motion

Because the force is always perpendicular to the velocity, a magnetic field does **no work**: it changes a charge's direction but not its speed. A charge moving perpendicular to a uniform field travels in a circle, with radius and frequency

$$
r = \frac{mv}{qB}, \qquad f_c = \frac{qB}{2\pi m}
$$

The **cyclotron frequency** doesn't depend on speed or radius.

> An electron at $10^6$ m/s in a 1 mT field circles with $r = (9.11\times10^{-31})(10^6)/(1.6\times10^{-19} \times 10^{-3}) = 5.7$ mm, at $f_c = 28$ MHz.

Applications: mass spectrometers (radius sorts ions by mass), particle accelerators, the magnetrons in microwave ovens, and the auroras (charged particles spiraling along Earth's field lines).

## Force on a current-carrying wire

A current is many moving charges, so a wire of length $L$ carrying current $I$ in a field $B$ feels

$$
\mathbf F = I\mathbf L\times\mathbf B, \qquad F = BIL\sin\theta
$$

> A 10 cm wire carrying 5 A across a 0.2 T field: $F = 0.2 \times 5 \times 0.1 = 0.1$ N.

## Torque on a loop: the motor principle

A rectangular loop of $N$ turns and area $A$ carrying current $I$ in a field $B$ feels forces on opposite sides that form a couple. The torque is

$$
\tau = NIAB\sin\theta
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the loop's normal and the field. Equivalently, the loop is a **magnetic dipole** with moment $\mu = NIA$, and $\boldsymbol\tau = \boldsymbol\mu\times\mathbf B$. Every DC motor, galvanometer and loudspeaker uses this (Electric Machines, Lesson 2.1).

## The Hall effect

Current flowing through a conductor in a perpendicular magnetic field gets pushed to one side, building up a transverse **Hall voltage**:

$$
V_H = \frac{IB}{nqt}
$$

($t$ = thickness in the field direction). Its sign reveals whether the carriers are positive or negative, which is how holes were shown to be real. Hall sensors in semiconductors (small $n$, so large $V_H$) measure current without contact and detect position in brushless motors, phones and car engines.

## Key takeaways

- $\mathbf F = q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$: perpendicular to motion, so no work; circular motion with $r = mv/qB$, $f_c = qB/2\pi m$.
- Wires: $F = BIL\sin\theta$. Loops: $\tau = NIAB\sin\theta$, the motor principle.
- Hall effect: $V_H = IB/nqt$, used in contactless current and position sensors.
