## Action at a distance, made local

Coulomb's law (Lesson 1.1) gives the force between two charges, but it hides a question: how does one charge "know" the other is there? The answer, due to Faraday, is the **electric field**: every charge fills the space around it with a field, and other charges respond to the field where they are.

$$
\mathbf E = \frac{\mathbf F}{q_0}, \qquad \mathbf F = q\mathbf E
$$

The field is the force per unit charge on a small test charge, in N/C (equivalently V/m). For a point charge $Q$:

$$
E = k\frac{|Q|}{r^2}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^9\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2
$$

pointing away from a positive charge and toward a negative one.

> A 5 nC charge produces $E = (8.99\times10^9)(5\times10^{-9})/0.1^2 = 4500$ V/m at 10 cm.

## Superposition

The fields of several charges add as **vectors**. That's all of electrostatics: break any charge distribution into pieces, add up their fields.

## Field lines

Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative ones; they point along $\mathbf E$, crowd together where the field is strong, and never cross. A uniform field (between parallel plates) has evenly spaced, parallel lines.

## Continuous distributions

For a smooth distribution, add up (integrate) the contributions of tiny pieces $dq$:

$$
\mathbf E = k\int\frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf r}
$$

Two results to know:

- **Infinite line** of charge density $\lambda$ (C/m): $E = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r} = \dfrac{2k\lambda}{r}$, falling as $1/r$.
- **Infinite plane** of surface density $\sigma$ (C/m²): $E = \dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$, uniform at any distance (Lesson 1.3 derives both quickly).

> A long wire charged to 2 nC/m: at 5 cm, $E = 2(8.99\times10^9)(2\times10^{-9})/0.05 = 719$ V/m.

## Dipoles

Equal and opposite charges $\pm q$ separated by $d$ form a **dipole** with dipole moment $p = qd$. Far away, its field falls as $1/r^3$ (the two charges' fields nearly cancel). In a uniform field a dipole feels no net force but a torque $\tau = pE\sin\theta$ that aligns it with the field. Water molecules are dipoles, which is why water's permittivity is high; polarized molecules in insulators are the origin of the dielectric constant (Lesson 2.1).

## Charges moving in fields

A charge in a field accelerates: $a = qE/m$. Electrons are so light that modest fields give them enormous accelerations.

> In a 1000 V/m field, an electron accelerates at $(1.6\times10^{-19})(1000)/(9.11\times10^{-31}) = 1.8\times10^{14}$ m/s², which is how cathode-ray tubes and electron microscopes steer beams.

## Key takeaways

- $\mathbf E = \mathbf F/q$; a point charge gives $E = kQ/r^2$; fields superpose as vectors.
- Line charge: $2k\lambda/r$; plane: $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ (uniform).
- Dipoles: $p = qd$, torque $pE\sin\theta$, far field $\propto 1/r^3$.
- Charges accelerate at $qE/m$.
