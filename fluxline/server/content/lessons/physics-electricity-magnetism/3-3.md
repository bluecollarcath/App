## Changing magnetism makes electricity

In 1831 Faraday found the reverse of Ørsted's discovery: a **changing** magnetic field induces a voltage. This is how nearly all the world's electricity is generated, and how transformers, inductors, wireless chargers, induction cooktops and guitar pickups work.

## Magnetic flux

The magnetic flux through a loop of area $A$ in a uniform field is

$$
\Phi_B = BA\cos\theta
$$

(in webers, T·m²), where $\theta$ is the angle between the field and the loop's normal.

## Faraday's law

The emf induced in a circuit equals the rate of change of the flux through it (times $N$ for $N$ turns):

$$
\mathcal E = -N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}
$$

The flux can change in three ways: the **field** changes (transformers), the **area** changes, or the **orientation** changes (generators).

> A 200-turn coil of area 10 cm² sits in a field that rises from 0 to 0.5 T in 0.1 s: $\mathcal E = 200 \times (0.5 \times 10^{-3})/0.1 = 1$ V.

## Lenz's law

The minus sign: the induced current flows in the direction that **opposes the change** in flux that caused it. Push a magnet toward a coil, and the induced current makes the coil repel it; pull it away, and the coil attracts it. Lenz's law is energy conservation: you must do work to generate electricity. It's also why a falling magnet drops slowly through a copper pipe (eddy currents brake it), how induction cooktops heat pans, and how magnetic brakes on trains and roller coasters work.

## Motional emf

A conductor of length $L$ moving at speed $v$ perpendicular to a field $B$ has an emf along it:

$$
\mathcal E = BLv
$$

It comes from the magnetic force on the conductor's free charges, $qvB$. A rod sliding on rails in a field is a simple generator; the current it drives produces a force $BIL$ opposing the motion (Lenz again).

> A 1.5 m car axle moving at 30 m/s through Earth's 50 µT vertical field: $\mathcal E = (5\times10^{-5})(1.5)(30) = 2.25$ mV.

## The AC generator

A coil of $N$ turns and area $A$ rotating at angular speed $\omega$ in a field $B$ has $\Phi = BA\cos\omega t$, so

$$
\mathcal E = NBA\omega\sin\omega t
$$

a **sinusoidal** voltage. That's why grid power is AC.

> 100 turns, 0.01 m², 0.5 T, spinning at 60 rev/s ($\omega = 377$ rad/s): peak emf $= 100 \times 0.5 \times 0.01 \times 377 = 189$ V.

Real generators put the field on the rotor (an electromagnet) and the windings on the stator (Electric Machines, Lesson 2.3).

## Induced electric fields

When the flux through a loop changes, the induced emf means there's an electric field circulating around the loop, even in empty space with no wire: $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = -d\Phi_B/dt$. Unlike electrostatic fields, induced fields are **not** conservative, so "voltage" between two points depends on the path. That's why the ground lead of an oscilloscope probe picks up noise from nearby switching circuits, and it's one of Maxwell's equations (Electromagnetics, Lesson 3.1).

## Key takeaways

- $\Phi_B = BA\cos\theta$; $\mathcal E = -N\,d\Phi_B/dt$: change the field, area or orientation.
- Lenz's law: induced currents oppose the change (energy conservation; eddy-current braking).
- Motional emf $BLv$; generators produce $NBA\omega\sin\omega t$.
- Induced electric fields aren't conservative: voltage becomes path-dependent.
