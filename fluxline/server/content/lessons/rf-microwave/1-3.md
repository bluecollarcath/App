## Splitting and combining power

RF systems constantly split signals (to feed several antennas, to sample a transmitter's output) and combine them (in power amplifiers, in balanced mixers). Because RF ports must stay matched, a simple "T" junction won't do: it can't be matched at all three ports while remaining lossless. Practical components use transmission-line sections, often a quarter-wave long, and their behavior is described by S-parameters (Lesson 1.1).

## The Wilkinson power divider

The Wilkinson divider splits power equally between two outputs, in phase, with all ports matched and the outputs **isolated** from each other. For a $Z_0$ system:

- two quarter-wave arms of impedance $\sqrt2Z_0$ (70.7 Ω for 50 Ω);
- a resistor of $2Z_0$ (100 Ω) between the two outputs.

When the outputs see equal, in-phase signals (normal splitting), no current flows in the resistor, and each output gets half the power, $-3.01$ dB. A signal reflected back into one output splits between the resistor and the other arm in such a way that it cancels at the other output: isolation is typically 20–30 dB. The ideal S-matrix:

$$
S = \frac{-j}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Used in reverse, it **combines** two equal, in-phase signals with no loss, the basis of combining several power amplifiers.

## Hybrids and directional couplers

A **branch-line (90°) hybrid** has four ports, built from quarter-wave lines of $Z_0/\sqrt2$ (35.4 Ω) and $Z_0$. Power into port 1 splits equally to ports 2 and 3 with a **90° phase difference**, and nothing reaches port 4. Hybrids are the core of balanced amplifiers, I/Q modulators and image-reject mixers.

A **directional coupler** samples a small, known fraction of a wave traveling in **one** direction. For power into port 1:

- **coupling** $C = -10\log_{10}(P_3/P_1)$ dB: how much reaches the coupled port;
- **isolation** $I = -10\log_{10}(P_4/P_1)$: how much leaks to the isolated port;
- **directivity** $D = I - C$: how well the coupler tells forward from reflected waves.

> A 10 dB coupler sends 10% of the power to the coupled port and 90% through, an **insertion loss** of $-10\log_{10}0.9 = 0.46$ dB. A 20 dB coupler takes only 1% (0.04 dB loss).

Couplers measure forward and reflected power in transmitters and are the heart of network analyzers, which separate incident and reflected waves to measure S-parameters.

## Microwave filters

At microwave frequencies, lumped L and C values become impractically small, so filters are built from **transmission-line sections**:

- A short high-impedance line section acts like a series **inductor**, and a short low-impedance section like a shunt **capacitor**. Alternating them gives a **stepped-impedance lowpass filter**, directly from a lumped prototype (a Butterworth or Chebyshev ladder, DSP Lesson 3.2).
- **Richards' transformation** maps lumped elements to stubs, and **Kuroda's identities** convert series stubs into realizable shunt stubs.
- **Coupled-line** and **resonator** filters (quarter- or half-wave resonators coupled through gaps, cavity filters, ceramic and SAW/BAW acoustic filters) give bandpass responses. Every phone contains dozens of BAW filters to separate its bands.

A filter's selectivity is limited by its resonators' **unloaded Q**: narrow bandwidths with low loss need high-Q resonators (cavities, ceramics, acoustic resonators).

## Key takeaways

- Wilkinson divider: $\sqrt2Z_0$ quarter-wave arms and a $2Z_0$ resistor; equal in-phase split with isolated outputs.
- Branch-line hybrids split with 90° phase difference; directional couplers sample one direction (coupling, isolation, directivity).
- Microwave filters use stepped-impedance lines, stubs (Richards, Kuroda) and coupled resonators; resonator Q limits selectivity.
