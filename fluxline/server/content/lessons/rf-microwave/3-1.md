## Will the link close?

A **link budget** adds up every gain and loss between a transmitter and a receiver, in decibels, to predict the received signal and compare it with what the receiver needs. It's the first calculation in designing any radio system, from a Bluetooth earbud to a deep-space probe.

$$
P_r = P_t + G_t + G_r - L_{path} - L_{other} \quad\text{(dB, dBm, dBi)}
$$

$$
\text{margin} = P_r - P_{sensitivity}
$$

- $L_{path}$: free-space path loss $20\log_{10}(4\pi d/\lambda)$ (Electromagnetics, Lesson 4.2), plus extra loss from terrain, buildings, foliage and rain in real environments.
- $L_{other}$: cables, connectors, filters, polarization mismatch, pointing errors.
- **Fade margin**: extra headroom (often 10–30 dB) for multipath fading and weather.

> **Example: a 5.8 GHz point-to-point link over 10 km.** $P_t = 20$ dBm, 24 dBi antennas at both ends, 2 dB of cable loss at each end.
>
> $\lambda = 5.17$ cm; path loss $= 20\log_{10}(4\pi \times 10^4/0.0517) = 127.7$ dB.
>
> $P_r = 20 + 24 + 24 - 4 - 127.7 = -63.7$ dBm.
>
> With a receiver sensitivity of −80 dBm (Lesson 2.2 sets it): margin $= 16.3$ dB. Enough for clear-weather multipath, thin for heavy rain at this frequency.

## Receiver architecture

The architecture decides which impairments the budget must cover (Communication Systems, Lesson 1.3):

- **Superheterodyne:** one or two IF stages; excellent selectivity; needs image filtering.
- **Direct conversion (zero-IF):** mixes straight to baseband I/Q; no image problem, highly integrated (every phone), but suffers DC offsets, LO leakage and 1/f noise.
- **Low-IF:** a compromise used in Bluetooth and similar radios.
- **Direct RF sampling:** fast ADCs digitize the RF band directly (software-defined radio), moving filtering and mixing into DSP.

## Linearity: compression and intermodulation

Strong signals reveal an amplifier's nonlinearity. Expanding the output as $v_o = a_1v_i + a_2v_i^2 + a_3v_i^3 + \cdots$:

- **Gain compression.** As the input grows, the cubic term reduces the gain. The **1 dB compression point** (P1dB) is where gain has dropped 1 dB; it sets the maximum usable signal.
- **Third-order intermodulation.** Two strong tones at $f_1$ and $f_2$ create products at $2f_1 - f_2$ and $2f_2 - f_1$, which land **right next to** the tones, often inside the receiver's band, where no filter can remove them. IM3 products grow **3 dB for every 1 dB** of input increase.

Extrapolating the fundamental (slope 1) and IM3 (slope 3) lines, they intersect at the **third-order intercept point** (IIP3 at the input, OIP3 at the output). For a cubic nonlinearity, P1dB ≈ IIP3 − 9.6 dB. The IM3 level (input-referred) for two tones of power $P_{in}$ each is

$$
P_{IM3} = 3P_{in} - 2\,\text{IIP3}
$$

> IIP3 = −10 dBm, with two −40 dBm tones: $P_{IM3} = -120 + 20 = -100$ dBm, 60 dB below the tones. Raise the tones 10 dB and the IM3 rises 30 dB, to only 40 dB below.

## Dynamic range

The receiver's **spur-free dynamic range** is the range of input levels between the noise floor and the level where IM3 products rise to the noise floor:

$$
\text{SFDR} = \frac23\left(\text{IIP3} - N_{floor}\right) \quad\text{(dB)}
$$

> With IIP3 = −10 dBm and a −100 dBm noise floor: SFDR $= \tfrac23(90) = 60$ dB.

Cascade IIP3 behaves like the Friis formula in reverse: **later** stages dominate linearity, because they see the signal after all the gain. Hence the classic receiver design tension: enough early gain for a low noise figure, but not so much that the mixer and later stages are overdriven by strong signals.

## Key takeaways

- Link budget: $P_r = P_t + G_t + G_r - L_{path} - L_{other}$; compare with sensitivity for the margin.
- Architectures: superhet, zero-IF, low-IF, direct sampling, each with its own impairments.
- Nonlinearity: P1dB compression; IM3 at $2f_1 - f_2$ rising 3:1; P1dB ≈ IIP3 − 9.6 dB.
- SFDR $= \tfrac23(\text{IIP3} - N_{floor})$; early gain helps noise, later stages limit linearity.
