## Why match?

Every RF interface has a characteristic impedance, almost always 50 Ω. Antennas, transistors, filters and mixers rarely present exactly 50 Ω on their own, and any mismatch reflects power (Lesson 1.1). **Matching networks** transform one impedance into another with lossless reactive components, so that maximum power transfers (conjugate matching) or so that noise, linearity or efficiency is optimized.

The Electromagnetics course covered the **quarter-wave transformer** and **single-stub matching** with transmission lines. At lower RF frequencies (below a few GHz) and on chips and small boards, lumped inductors and capacitors are more compact, so the workhorse is the **L-network**.

## The L-network

Two reactances, one in series and one in shunt, can match any two resistances. The shunt element goes across the **larger** resistance. The design starts from the network's **Q**:

$$
Q = \sqrt{\frac{R_{high}}{R_{low}} - 1}, \qquad X_{series} = QR_{low}, \qquad X_{shunt} = \frac{R_{high}}{Q}
$$

with opposite signs: if the series element is an inductor, the shunt is a capacitor (a **lowpass** match, which also attenuates harmonics), or vice versa (a **highpass** match, which passes DC blocking).

> **Example.** Match a 200 Ω load to a 50 Ω source at 100 MHz.
>
> $Q = \sqrt{200/50 - 1} = 1.73$.
>
> Series reactance $1.73 \times 50 = 86.6\ \Omega$; shunt reactance $200/1.73 = 115.5\ \Omega$.
>
> Lowpass version: series $L = 86.6/(2\pi \times 10^8) = 138$ nH, shunt $C = 1/(2\pi \times 10^8 \times 115.5) = 13.8$ pF, placed across the 200 Ω load.

**Checking it.** Looking from the source: the shunt capacitor and 200 Ω load in parallel give $50 - j86.6\ \Omega$ (an exact parallel-to-series conversion), and the series inductor's $+j86.6\ \Omega$ cancels the reactive part, leaving exactly 50 Ω.

## Bandwidth and Q

A matching network is a resonant circuit, so it matches perfectly at only one frequency. The larger the transformation ratio, the higher the Q, and the **narrower** the bandwidth (roughly $f_0/Q_L$, with the loaded $Q_L \approx Q/2$ for a matched L-network). To match over a wider band:

- use **several L-sections** in cascade, each with a small ratio (e.g. 200 → 100 → 50 Ω): lower Q per section, wider bandwidth;
- use **multi-section quarter-wave transformers** or tapered lines;
- accept a slightly imperfect match (VSWR < 2) across the band, which the **Bode–Fano limit** says is the best any passive network can do for a given load Q.

To get a **higher** Q than the L-network allows (for more harmonic filtering or selectivity), use a three-element **π** or **T** network.

## On the Smith chart

On the Smith chart (Electromagnetics, Lesson 1.3), a series element moves the point along a constant-resistance circle, and a shunt element along a constant-conductance circle. An L-match is two such arcs from the load to the center, and the two possible routes are the lowpass and highpass solutions. RF engineers design matches this way, interactively, in simulators.

Real components aren't ideal: inductors have finite Q (series resistance) and self-resonance, and capacitors have ESR and parasitic inductance. At GHz frequencies, layout pads and traces become part of the network.

## Key takeaways

- L-networks match any two resistances: $Q = \sqrt{R_{high}/R_{low} - 1}$, $X_s = QR_{low}$, $X_p = R_{high}/Q$; the shunt goes across the larger resistance.
- Lowpass (series L, shunt C) or highpass (series C, shunt L) versions.
- Higher transformation ratio, higher Q, narrower bandwidth; cascade sections or use π/T networks to shape it.
