## The receiver's floor

A receiver's ability to hear weak signals is limited by noise: thermal noise from the antenna and resistors (Probability, Lesson 3.3) plus noise added by every amplifier, mixer and filter. RF engineers quantify the added noise with the **noise figure**.

## Noise factor and noise figure

$$
F = \frac{\text{SNR}_{in}}{\text{SNR}_{out}} \ge 1, \qquad \text{NF} = 10\log_{10}F \ \text{dB}
$$

(measured with the input noise at the standard temperature $T_0 = 290$ K). A perfect noiseless component has $F = 1$ (0 dB). Equivalently, a component adds noise as if a resistor at an **equivalent noise temperature** $T_e$ sat at its input:

$$
T_e = (F - 1)T_0
$$

> A low-noise amplifier with NF = 0.8 dB has $F = 1.20$ and $T_e = 59$ K. Satellite and radio-astronomy receivers, where the sky behind the antenna is only a few kelvin, quote noise temperature; terrestrial systems quote noise figure.

**Passive losses add their loss directly** to the noise figure: a cable or filter with 2 dB of loss at room temperature has NF = 2 dB. Every decibel of loss before the first amplifier is a decibel of sensitivity lost, so LNAs are mounted right at the antenna.

## Cascades: the Friis formula

For stages with noise factors $F_i$ and available gains $G_i$ (linear):

$$
F_{total} = F_1 + \frac{F_2 - 1}{G_1} + \frac{F_3 - 1}{G_1G_2} + \cdots
$$

> **Example.** An LNA (NF 1 dB, gain 15 dB), a mixer (NF 8 dB, conversion loss 7 dB) and an IF amplifier (NF 3 dB):
> $$F = 1.26 + \frac{6.31 - 1}{31.6} + \frac{2.00 - 1}{31.6 \times 0.200} = 1.26 + 0.17 + 0.16 = 1.58$$
> NF = 2.0 dB. The LNA's gain kept the noisy mixer from adding much; with only 10 dB of LNA gain, the total would be 3.6 dB.

## Sensitivity

The noise power in bandwidth $B$ at room temperature is $kT_0B$, or **−174 dBm/Hz** $+ 10\log_{10}B$. The minimum detectable signal is

$$
P_{min} = -174\ \text{dBm} + 10\log_{10}B + \text{NF} + \text{SNR}_{min}
$$

> A 1 MHz-wide receiver with NF = 5 dB, needing 10 dB SNR: $-174 + 60 + 5 + 10 = -99$ dBm.

## Low-noise design

A transistor's noise figure depends on the source impedance it sees. It's minimized at an optimum $\Gamma_{opt}$, generally **not** the conjugate match for maximum gain. **Noise circles** on the Smith chart show where the NF is within a given amount of $F_{min}$; the designer picks a $\Gamma_S$ that balances noise, gain and input match. Techniques like inductive source degeneration let an LNA be noise-matched and 50 Ω matched at once.

## Key takeaways

- $F = \text{SNR}_{in}/\text{SNR}_{out}$; $T_e = (F - 1)290$ K; passive loss adds directly to NF.
- Friis: $F = F_1 + (F_2 - 1)/G_1 + \cdots$; a good first stage dominates.
- Sensitivity $= -174 + 10\log_{10}B + \text{NF} + \text{SNR}_{min}$ dBm.
- Minimum noise needs $\Gamma_{opt}$, usually not the gain match; noise circles show the trade-off.
