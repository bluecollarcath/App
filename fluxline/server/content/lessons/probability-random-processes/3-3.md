## Filtering noise

Every receiver, sensor and amplifier is a linear system with noise at its input. To design it, you need to know how much noise comes out, and how the filter shapes it. For a WSS input $X(t)$ to a stable LTI system with frequency response $H(f)$, the output $Y(t)$ is also WSS, with

$$
\mu_Y = \mu_X\,H(0), \qquad
S_Y(f) = |H(f)|^2\,S_X(f), \qquad
P_Y = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|H(f)|^2S_X(f)\,df
$$

Only the **magnitude** of $H$ matters for power; the phase rearranges the waveform but doesn't change how much power lies at each frequency.

## White noise through a filter

With white input $S_X = N_0/2$, the output power is

$$
P_Y = \frac{N_0}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|H(f)|^2\,df = N_0\,B_N\,|H(0)|^2
$$

which defines the filter's **noise-equivalent bandwidth** $B_N$: the width of an ideal brick-wall filter (with the same peak gain) that would pass the same noise power.

> **RC lowpass.** $|H(f)|^2 = 1/\big(1 + (2\pi fRC)^2\big)$ integrates to $B_N = \dfrac{1}{4RC}$, which is $\pi/2 = 1.57$ times the 3 dB bandwidth $1/(2\pi RC)$. A first-order filter lets through more noise than its 3 dB bandwidth suggests, because of its slow rolloff.
>
> With $R = 1$ kΩ and $C = 1\ \mu$F: $B_N = 250$ Hz. For input noise $N_0/2 = 10^{-12}$ V²/Hz, the output is $N_0B_N = 2\times10^{-12} \times 250 = 5\times10^{-10}$ V², or 22.4 µV RMS.

## Thermal noise

Any resistor at temperature $T$ generates a random voltage from the thermal motion of its electrons (Johnson–Nyquist noise). Its one-sided PSD is $4kTR$ V²/Hz, so in a bandwidth $B$

$$
\overline{v_n^2} = 4kTRB
$$

with $k = 1.38\times10^{-23}$ J/K. At room temperature ($T = 290$ K), $4kT = 1.6\times10^{-20}$ J.

> A 1 kΩ resistor in a 10 kHz bandwidth: $\sqrt{1.6\times10^{-20} \times 10^3 \times 10^4} = 0.40\ \mu$V RMS. A 50 Ω source in a 1 MHz bandwidth: 0.89 µV. That's the floor every receiver fights against.

**kT/C noise.** Put a resistor's thermal noise through its own RC lowpass. The output power is $4kTR \cdot \frac{1}{4RC} = kT/C$, **independent of R**: a bigger resistor makes more noise, but filters it to a proportionally narrower band. Every switched-capacitor circuit, ADC sampler and image-sensor pixel has this noise floor. A 1 pF capacitor at room temperature: $\sqrt{kT/C} = 63\ \mu$V RMS.

## Signal-to-noise ratio

The figure of merit for any measurement or link is

$$
\text{SNR} = \frac{P_{signal}}{P_{noise}}, \qquad \text{SNR}_{dB} = 10\log_{10}\text{SNR}
$$

> A 1 mV RMS signal with 10 µV RMS of noise has $\text{SNR} = (1000/10)^2 = 10^4$, or 40 dB.

Filtering improves SNR by removing noise outside the signal's band. The best possible filter for detecting a known pulse in white noise is the **matched filter**, whose impulse response is the pulse reversed in time. It maximizes the output SNR to $2E/N_0$, where $E$ is the pulse energy, and it's the foundation of digital receivers (Communication Systems).

## Key takeaways

- Through an LTI system: $\mu_Y = \mu_XH(0)$ and $S_Y = |H|^2S_X$.
- White noise output power $= N_0B_N|H(0)|^2$; an RC filter has $B_N = 1/(4RC)$.
- Thermal noise: $\overline{v_n^2} = 4kTRB$; through its own RC it gives $kT/C$.
- SNR in dB is $10\log_{10}$ of the power ratio; matched filters maximize it for known pulses.
