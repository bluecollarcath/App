## Why averages work

Measure a noisy voltage once and you get a random value. Average a thousand measurements and you get something very close to the true value. Add up many small, independent random effects and the total is always bell-shaped, no matter what each effect looks like. These two facts, the **law of large numbers** and the **central limit theorem**, are why statistics works and why noise is Gaussian.

## A bound that always holds

**Chebyshev's inequality** needs only the mean and variance:

$$
P\big(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma\big) \le \frac{1}{k^2}
$$

At most 25% of any distribution lies 2 or more standard deviations from its mean, at most 11% lies 3 or more. It's loose (for a Gaussian the true values are 4.6% and 0.27%), but it's guaranteed.

## The law of large numbers

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be independent with the same mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Their **sample mean**

$$
\bar X_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i
$$

has $E[\bar X_n] = \mu$ and, since the variances of independent terms add,

$$
\text{Var}(\bar X_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \sigma_{\bar X} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}
$$

So $\bar X_n \to \mu$ as $n$ grows (applying Chebyshev, the probability of any fixed deviation goes to zero). The **$1/\sqrt n$ rule** is how engineers trade time for accuracy: averaging 100 samples cuts the noise by 10 (20 dB); averaging 10,000 cuts it by 100. A digital oscilloscope's "average" mode and a lock-in amplifier both rely on it.

> **How many samples?** A sensor reads with noise $\sigma = 2$ mV. To estimate the true value within $\pm0.1$ mV at 95% confidence ($\pm1.96\,\sigma_{\bar X}$), you need $1.96 \times 2/\sqrt n \le 0.1$, so $n \ge 1537$ samples.

## The central limit theorem

Much more is true than "the average converges." The **distribution** of a sum of many independent, identically distributed variables approaches a Gaussian, whatever the original distribution (as long as its variance is finite):

$$
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n} \ \longrightarrow\ \mathcal N(0, 1)
$$

The convergence is fast. Sums of just three uniform variables already look Gaussian:

```plot
x: -0.2, 3.2, x
y: 0, 0.9, f(x)
f: x^2/2*(u(x) - u(x - 1)) + (-2*x^2 + 6*x - 3)/2*(u(x - 1) - u(x - 2)) + (3 - x)^2/2*(u(x - 2) - u(x - 3)) | sum of 3 uniforms
f: exp(-(x - 1.5)^2/0.5)/sqrt(0.5*pi) | Gaussian, same mean and variance | dash
caption: The PDF of the sum of three independent uniform $[0, 1]$ variables (mean 1.5, variance 0.25) compared with a Gaussian of the same mean and variance. They're already hard to tell apart.
```

That's why **thermal noise is Gaussian**: the voltage across a resistor is the sum of the effects of an enormous number of electrons moving randomly. It's also why measurement errors, manufacturing tolerances that combine many small causes, and the totals of many random loads on a power grid are all modeled as Gaussian.

> **Example: 100 dice.** One die has mean 3.5 and variance 35/12. The sum of 100 dice has mean 350 and variance $100 \times 35/12 = 291.7$ ($\sigma = 17.1$). The probability that the sum exceeds 380 is about
> $$
> Q\!\left(\frac{380 - 350}{17.1}\right) = Q(1.76) = 0.039
> $$
> No one would compute that from the exact distribution of 100 dice; the CLT makes it a one-line calculation.

## Key takeaways

- Chebyshev: $P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le 1/k^2$ for any distribution.
- The sample mean has standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt n$: averaging $n$ samples cuts noise power by $n$.
- CLT: sums of many independent variables are approximately Gaussian, which is why noise and tolerances are modeled as Gaussian.
