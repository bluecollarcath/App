## Two random quantities at once

Engineering problems rarely involve one random variable alone: the I and Q components of a received signal, the voltage and current in a noisy circuit, the temperature and failure rate of a chip. Their **joint distribution** describes how they behave together.

For discrete variables, the **joint PMF** $p_{XY}(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y)$; for continuous ones, the **joint PDF** $f_{XY}(x, y)$, with probabilities given by volumes:

$$
P\big((X, Y) \in A\big) = \iint_A f_{XY}(x, y)\,dx\,dy
$$

## Marginals

To get the distribution of one variable alone, sum or integrate the other one out:

$$
p_X(x) = \sum_y p_{XY}(x, y), \qquad f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{XY}(x, y)\,dy
$$

## Independence

$X$ and $Y$ are **independent** if the joint distribution factors into the marginals:

$$
f_{XY}(x, y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y) \quad\text{for all } x, y
$$

Then knowing $Y$ tells you nothing about $X$, and $E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]$.

## Covariance and correlation

The **covariance** measures how two variables move together:

$$
\text{Cov}(X, Y) = E\big[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\big] = E[XY] - \mu_X\mu_Y
$$

Normalized, it's the **correlation coefficient**, always between $-1$ and $+1$:

$$
\rho = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}
$$

$\rho = \pm1$ means $Y$ is an exact linear function of $X$; $\rho = 0$ means **uncorrelated**. Independent variables are always uncorrelated, but not the other way around: $Y = X^2$ with $X$ symmetric about zero is uncorrelated with $X$ yet completely determined by it. (For jointly Gaussian variables, uncorrelated **does** imply independent, one of the reasons Gaussian models are so convenient.)

The variance of a sum needs the covariance:

$$
\text{Var}(X + Y) = \text{Var}(X) + \text{Var}(Y) + 2\,\text{Cov}(X, Y)
$$

For independent (or uncorrelated) variables, **variances add**. That's why independent noise sources add in power, not in amplitude: two independent 3 µV and 4 µV RMS noise sources give $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$ µV RMS.

> **Worked example.** Two binary sensors have the joint PMF
>
> | | $Y = 0$ | $Y = 1$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | $X = 0$ | 0.4 | 0.1 |
> | $X = 1$ | 0.2 | 0.3 |
>
> Marginals: $P(X = 1) = 0.5$, $P(Y = 1) = 0.4$.
>
> Independent? $P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 0.3$, but $P(X = 1)P(Y = 1) = 0.2$. No.
>
> $E[XY] = 0.3$ (only the $(1, 1)$ cell contributes), so $\text{Cov} = 0.3 - 0.5(0.4) = 0.1$.
>
> $\text{Var}(X) = 0.25$, $\text{Var}(Y) = 0.24$, so $\rho = 0.1/\sqrt{0.06} = 0.41$: moderately positively correlated.

## Conditional distributions

Knowing $Y = y$ updates the distribution of $X$:

$$
f_{X\mid Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{f_{XY}(x, y)}{f_Y(y)}
$$

This is Bayes' rule (Lesson 1.1) for continuous variables, and it's the heart of estimation: from a noisy measurement $Y$, find the best guess of $X$.

## Key takeaways

- Joint distributions describe several variables; marginals come from summing or integrating out the others.
- Independent: the joint factors. Then $E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]$ and variances add.
- $\text{Cov} = E[XY] - \mu_X\mu_Y$, $\rho = \text{Cov}/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)$; uncorrelated is weaker than independent.
