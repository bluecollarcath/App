## Random functions of time

A **random process** $X(t)$ is a random variable at every instant of time: a random **waveform**. The noise on a radio receiver's output, the speech from a microphone, the bit stream on a network link, the daily load on a power grid. Each experiment produces one **sample function** (one waveform); the collection of all possible waveforms is the **ensemble**.

At any fixed time $t_1$, $X(t_1)$ is an ordinary random variable with its own distribution. The process is described by how those variables relate across time.

## Mean and autocorrelation

The two most useful descriptions:

$$
\mu_X(t) = E\big[X(t)\big], \qquad R_X(t_1, t_2) = E\big[X(t_1)X(t_2)\big]
$$

The **autocorrelation** $R_X$ says how similar the waveform is to itself at two different times. If it's large for widely separated times, the process changes slowly; if it drops quickly, the process is erratic.

## Wide-sense stationarity

A process is **wide-sense stationary** (WSS) if its statistics don't drift over time:

1. the mean is constant, $\mu_X(t) = \mu_X$, and
2. the autocorrelation depends only on the time **difference** $\tau = t_2 - t_1$: $R_X(t_1, t_2) = R_X(\tau)$.

Most noise sources and many signals are WSS, at least over the timescale of interest. For WSS processes, $R_X(\tau)$ has useful properties:

- $R_X(0) = E[X^2(t)]$ is the **average power**.
- $R_X(-\tau) = R_X(\tau)$: it's even.
- $|R_X(\tau)| \le R_X(0)$: nothing is more similar to the signal than itself, now.
- If $X$ has a nonzero mean and no periodic part, $R_X(\tau) \to \mu_X^2$ as $\tau \to \infty$: the waveform "forgets" itself, leaving only the DC.

> **Example: random-phase sinusoid.** $X(t) = A\cos(\omega_0t + \Theta)$ with $\Theta$ uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$. Averaging over the phase gives $\mu_X = 0$ and
> $$
> R_X(\tau) = \frac{A^2}{2}\cos\omega_0\tau
> $$
> It depends only on $\tau$, so the process is WSS, with power $R_X(0) = A^2/2$, the familiar power of a sinusoid. A periodic process has a periodic autocorrelation that never dies out.

> **Example: a "smooth" noise.** Noise from a first-order lowpass filter has $R_X(\tau) = \sigma^2e^{-|\tau|/\tau_c}$: its values are strongly correlated over times shorter than the **correlation time** $\tau_c$ and nearly independent beyond.

```

## Ergodicity

The definitions above are **ensemble** averages over all possible waveforms. In the lab you have only one waveform, and you average over **time**:

$$
\langle x(t)\rangle = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}x(t)\,dt, \qquad
\langle x(t)x(t + \tau)\rangle
$$

A process is **ergodic** if time averages of a single sample function equal the ensemble averages. Then one long recording tells you everything, and that's the (usually unstated) assumption behind every oscilloscope or spectrum-analyzer measurement of noise. The random-phase sinusoid is ergodic; a process like "a DC voltage chosen at random once and held forever" is WSS but **not** ergodic, because one sample function reveals only its own value.

+ its mean is constant and its autocorrelation depends only on the time difference

```

## Key takeaways

- A random process is a random waveform; $\mu_X(t)$ and $R_X(t_1, t_2)$ summarize it.
- WSS: constant mean, $R_X$ depends only on $\tau$; $R_X(0)$ is the power, $R_X$ is even and peaks at 0.
- Ergodic: time averages from one waveform equal ensemble averages, which justifies lab measurements.
