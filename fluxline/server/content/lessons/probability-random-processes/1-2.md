## Numbers from chance

A **random variable** assigns a number to each outcome of an experiment: the number of bit errors in a packet, the count of photons hitting a detector, the number of customers in a queue. A **discrete** random variable takes countable values $x_k$, described by its **probability mass function** (PMF):

$$
p_X(x_k) = P(X = x_k), \qquad \sum_k p_X(x_k) = 1
$$

## Mean and variance

The **expected value** (mean) is the probability-weighted average, and the **variance** measures spread around it:

$$
E[X] = \mu = \sum_k x_k\,p_X(x_k), \qquad
\text{Var}(X) = \sigma^2 = E\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] = E[X^2] - \mu^2
$$

The **standard deviation** $\sigma$ has the same units as $X$. Two rules used constantly: $E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b$ and $\text{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\text{Var}(X)$.

> **A fair die.** $E[X] = (1 + 2 + \cdots + 6)/6 = 3.5$; $E[X^2] = 91/6$; $\text{Var}(X) = 91/6 - 3.5^2 = 2.92$.

## The standard distributions

| Distribution | Models | PMF | Mean | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bernoulli**($p$) | one trial: success or failure | $P(1) = p$, $P(0) = 1 - p$ | $p$ | $p(1 - p)$ |
| **Binomial**($n, p$) | successes in $n$ independent trials | $\dbinom{n}{k}p^k(1 - p)^{n-k}$ | $np$ | $np(1 - p)$ |
| **Geometric**($p$) | trials until the first success | $(1 - p)^{k-1}p$, $k \ge 1$ | $1/p$ | $(1 - p)/p^2$ |
| **Poisson**($\lambda$) | count of rare events in an interval | $e^{-\lambda}\dfrac{\lambda^k}{k!}$ | $\lambda$ | $\lambda$ |

> **Example: bit errors.** A link has bit-error probability $p = 10^{-3}$. In a 1000-bit packet, the number of errors is binomial with $n = 1000$:
>
> $P(\text{no errors}) = (1 - 10^{-3})^{1000} = 0.368$, so almost two out of three packets contain at least one error and need error correction or a retransmission.
>
> With $n$ large and $p$ small, the binomial is close to Poisson with $\lambda = np = 1$: $P(0) = e^{-1} = 0.368$, the same to three digits, and far easier to compute.

> **Example: coin flips.** In 10 fair flips, $P(\text{exactly 5 heads}) = \binom{10}{5}/2^{10} = 252/1024 = 0.246$. Even the most likely outcome happens less than a quarter of the time.

The **Poisson** distribution appears whenever events occur independently at a constant average rate: photon arrivals in an optical receiver (shot noise), packet arrivals at a router, radioactive decays, failures in a large population of components. If events arrive at rate $r$ per second, the count in $T$ seconds is Poisson with $\lambda = rT$.

The **geometric** distribution answers "how long until it works?": if each transmission attempt succeeds with probability $p$, the expected number of attempts is $1/p$.

## Practice

```quiz
? A random variable is 1 with probability 0.2 and 0 otherwise. What is its variance?
= 0.16 | 0.001 |
> Bernoulli: $p(1 - p) = 0.2 \times 0.8$.
```

```quiz
? Five independent bits each flip with probability 0.1. What is the probability that none flip?
= 0.5905 | 0.001 |

```

```quiz
? Packets arrive at a router at an average of 2 per millisecond (Poisson). What is the probability that none arrive in a given millisecond?
= 0.1353 | 0.001 |
> $e^{-2} = 0.135$.
```

```quiz
? Each connection attempt succeeds with probability 0.25. What is the expected number of attempts until the first success?
= 4 | 0.001 |
> Geometric mean: $1/p$.
```

```quiz

= 32 | 0.001 |
> $\text{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\text{Var}(X) = 16 \times 2$.
```

```quiz

- 81

> For a Poisson distribution, the mean and variance are both $\lambda$.
```

## Key takeaways

- A PMF lists $P(X = x_k)$; $E[X] = \sum x_kp(x_k)$, $\text{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - \mu^2$.
- Bernoulli (one trial), binomial (count in $n$ trials), geometric (wait for first success), Poisson (rare events at a constant rate).
- Binomial with large $n$ and small $p$ is approximately Poisson with $\lambda = np$.
