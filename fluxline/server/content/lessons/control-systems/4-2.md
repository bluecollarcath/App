## When you can't measure every state

State feedback needs the full state $\mathbf{x}$, but sensors are expensive and some states (an internal temperature, a velocity) can't be measured at all. An **observer** (state estimator) rebuilds $\mathbf{x}$ from the input $u$ and the measured output $y$.

## The Luenberger observer

Run a copy of the model, and correct it with the output error:

$$
\dot{\hat{\mathbf{x}}} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}} + Bu + L\,(y - C\hat{\mathbf{x}})
$$

Subtracting this from the true dynamics gives the estimation error $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}$:

$$
\dot{\mathbf{e}} = (A - LC)\,\mathbf{e}
$$

The error dies out at a rate set by the eigenvalues of $A - LC$, which you choose through the gain $L$. This is the same problem as pole placement, on the **transposed** system.

## Observability

Placing every observer pole needs the output to "see" every state. The **observability matrix**

$$
\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ CA \\ \vdots \\ CA^{n-1} \end{bmatrix}
$$

must have full rank $n$. Observability and controllability are **duals**: $(A, C)$ is observable exactly when $(A^T, C^T)$ is controllable.

> **Example.** Double integrator, measuring position only: $C = [1 \ \ 0]$, $\mathcal{O} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$: observable, so velocity can be estimated. With $L = \begin{bmatrix} \ell_1 \\ \ell_2 \end{bmatrix}$:
> $$
> A - LC = \begin{bmatrix} -\ell_1 & 1 \\ -\ell_2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(sI - A + LC) = s^2 + \ell_1 s + \ell_2
> $$
> For observer poles at $-10, -10$ (five times faster than the controller poles at $-2 \pm j2$ from the last lesson): $(s + 10)^2 = s^2 + 20s + 100$, so $L = \begin{bmatrix} 20 \\ 100 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Rule of thumb:** make the observer poles 2 to 6 times faster than the controller poles. Faster estimates converge sooner but pass along more sensor noise. The **Kalman filter** picks $L$ optimally from the noise statistics, just as LQR picks $K$.

## The separation principle

Feed the estimate to the controller, $u = -K\hat{\mathbf{x}}$. The closed-loop poles are then

$$
\text{eig}(A - BK) \ \cup\ \text{eig}(A - LC)
$$

the controller poles and the observer poles, **each unaffected by the other**. So you can design $K$ and $L$ separately. Together they form a dynamic **observer-based compensator**, the state-space counterpart of the lead and PID compensators of Unit 3.

## Digital control

Real controllers run on microcontrollers that sample $y$ every $T$ seconds and hold $u$ constant between updates (a **zero-order hold**, ZOH). Two routes:

- **Emulation:** design in continuous time, then convert, for example with Tustin's (bilinear) rule $s \to \dfrac{2}{T}\dfrac{z - 1}{z + 1}$.
- **Direct digital design:** discretize the plant exactly, then design in the z-domain.

With a ZOH the exact discrete-time model is

$$
\mathbf{x}[k + 1] = \Phi\,\mathbf{x}[k] + \Gamma u[k], \qquad \Phi = e^{AT}, \quad \Gamma = \int_0^T e^{A\tau}\,d\tau\;B
$$

> **Example.** $\dot x = -2x + u$ sampled at $T = 0.1$ s:
> $$
> \Phi = e^{-0.2} = 0.8187, \qquad \Gamma = \frac{1 - e^{-0.2}}{2} = 0.0906
> $$
> so $x[k + 1] = 0.8187\,x[k] + 0.0906\,u[k]$. The continuous pole $s = -2$ maps to $z = e^{sT} = 0.8187$, inside the unit circle (Signals & Systems, Lesson 4.2): stable.

A digital PID update, with the integral as a running sum and the derivative as a backward difference:

$$
u[k] = K_p e[k] + K_iT\sum_{j \le k} e[j] + \frac{K_d}{T}\big(e[k] - e[k - 1]\big)
$$

**Choosing $T$:** sample 10 to 30 times faster than the closed-loop bandwidth, and filter the sensor against aliasing first. The ZOH acts roughly like a delay of $T/2$, which costs phase margin of $\omega_cT/2$ radians, so slow sampling erodes stability.

## Key takeaways

- An observer copies the model and corrects it with $L(y - C\hat{\mathbf{x}})$; its error decays per $\text{eig}(A - LC)$.
- Observability ($\mathcal{O}$ full rank) is the dual of controllability.
- Separation principle: design $K$ and $L$ separately; the closed-loop poles are both sets.
- Digital control: $\Phi = e^{AT}$, $s$-poles map to $z = e^{sT}$; sample 10–30× the bandwidth.
