## Designing with state feedback

Classical design adjusts a few gains and watches where the poles go. **State-space** design (Lesson 1.2) goes further: if you can measure every state, you can put **every** closed-loop pole wherever you want.

For $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} + B u$, use the control law

$$
u = -K\mathbf{x} + \bar N r
$$

where $K$ is a row of gains (one per state) and $\bar N$ scales the reference. The closed loop becomes

$$
\dot{\mathbf{x}} = (A - BK)\,\mathbf{x} + B\bar N r
$$

so its poles are the eigenvalues of $A - BK$, which you choose through $K$.

## Controllability: can you move every pole?

You can only place every pole if the input can reach every state. The test is the **controllability matrix**

$$
\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} B & AB & A^2B & \cdots & A^{n-1}B \end{bmatrix}
$$

The system is **controllable** if and only if $\mathcal{C}$ has full rank $n$ (for one input, $\det \mathcal{C} \ne 0$). An uncontrollable mode keeps its open-loop eigenvalue whatever $K$ is, so if that eigenvalue is unstable, no state feedback can fix it.

> **Uncontrollable example.** $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $\mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has rank 1. The input never reaches $x_2$, whose mode $e^{2t}$ grows no matter what.

## Pole placement by matching coefficients

1. Check controllability.
2. Pick the desired poles (from the specs: $\zeta$ and $\omega_n$ of Lesson 2.1, plus faster extra poles if needed) and expand the desired polynomial.
3. Write $\det(sI - A + BK)$ in terms of the gains and match coefficients.

> **Example: double integrator.** A frictionless mass, position $x_1$, velocity $x_2$:
> $$
> A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathcal{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \ (\text{rank } 2)
> $$
> With $K = [k_1 \ \ k_2]$:
> $$
> A - BK = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -k_1 & -k_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(sI - A + BK) = s^2 + k_2 s + k_1
> $$
> For poles at $-2 \pm j2$ ($\zeta = 0.707$, $\omega_n = 2.83$), the desired polynomial is $(s + 2)^2 + 4 = s^2 + 4s + 8$. So $K = [8 \ \ 4]$: position feedback acts as a spring, velocity feedback as a damper.

For larger systems, **Ackermann's formula** does the matching automatically:

$$
K = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\mathcal{C}^{-1}\,\alpha_d(A)
$$

where $\alpha_d(s)$ is the desired characteristic polynomial, evaluated with the matrix $A$ in place of $s$. In practice you'd call `place` or `acker` in MATLAB or Python.

## Tracking a reference

Feedback alone drives the states to zero. To make the output $y = C\mathbf{x}$ follow a constant $r$, choose $\bar N$ so the closed-loop DC gain is 1:

$$
\bar N = \frac{-1}{C(A - BK)^{-1}B}
$$

For the double integrator with $y = x_1$, the closed loop from $r$ to $y$ is $\bar N/(s^2 + 4s + 8)$, so $\bar N = 8$. (Adding an integrator state on the error is the robust alternative, because it doesn't rely on an exact model.)

## Where to put the poles? LQR

Very fast poles demand huge gains, which means large actuator effort and noise sensitivity. The **linear-quadratic regulator** (LQR) chooses $K$ to minimize

$$
J = \int_0^\infty \big(\mathbf{x}^T Q\,\mathbf{x} + R\,u^2\big)\,dt
$$

Raising $Q$ penalizes state error, which gives faster control. Raising $R$ penalizes effort, which gives gentler control. LQR always produces a stable loop with good margins (at least $60°$ phase margin for a single input), so it's the standard starting point for multi-state design.

## Key takeaways

- $u = -K\mathbf{x}$ makes the closed-loop poles the eigenvalues of $A - BK$.
- All poles can be placed if and only if $[B \ \ AB \ \cdots\ A^{n-1}B]$ has full rank.
- Match $\det(sI - A + BK)$ to the desired polynomial (or use Ackermann).
- $\bar N$ fixes the DC gain for tracking; LQR trades speed against effort.
