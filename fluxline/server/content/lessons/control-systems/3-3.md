## A different view of the same loop

The root locus works in the s-plane. **Frequency-response** methods look at the loop gain $L(j\omega) = G(j\omega)H(j\omega)$ on the $j\omega$ axis instead. They have big practical advantages:

- $L(j\omega)$ can be **measured** on real hardware with a sine sweep, even when no model exists.
- Time delays, which the root locus handles poorly, are easy: $e^{-sT}$ adds phase $-\omega T$ and changes no magnitudes.
- **Stability margins** measure how robust the loop is, not just whether it is stable.

## Gain and phase margins

The closed loop $1/(1 + L)$ blows up where $L(j\omega) = -1$, which means magnitude 1 **and** phase $-180°$ at the same frequency. The margins measure how far the loop is from that point:

- **Gain crossover** $\omega_c$: where $|L| = 1$ (0 dB). **Phase margin** $\text{PM} = 180° + \angle L(j\omega_c)$.
- **Phase crossover** $\omega_{180}$: where $\angle L = -180°$. **Gain margin** $\text{GM} = 1/|L(j\omega_{180})|$, or $-|L|_{\text{dB}}$ at that frequency.

A stable minimum-phase loop has both margins positive. Common design targets are $\text{PM} \ge 45°$ and $\text{GM} \ge 6$ dB. Phase margin also predicts damping: $\zeta \approx \text{PM}/100$ for $\text{PM}$ up to about $60°$.

> **Example.** $L(s) = \dfrac{10}{s(s + 1)(s + 10)}$.
>
> - $|L(j\omega)| = \dfrac{10}{\omega\sqrt{1 + \omega^2}\sqrt{100 + \omega^2}} = 1$ at $\omega_c = 0.784$ rad/s.
> - $\angle L = -90° - \tan^{-1}\omega - \tan^{-1}(\omega/10)$, which is $-132.6°$ at $\omega_c$, so $\text{PM} = 47.4°$.
> - The phase reaches $-180°$ where $\tan^{-1}\omega + \tan^{-1}(\omega/10) = 90°$, which means $\omega^2 = 10$: $\omega_{180} = 3.16$ rad/s.
> - There $|L| = 10/110 = 1/11$, so $\text{GM} = 11$ (20.8 dB). The gain could rise elevenfold before the loop went unstable.


## The Nyquist criterion

Bode margins can mislead for unusual loops (several crossovers, or open-loop poles in the right half-plane). The **Nyquist criterion** settles stability in every case. Plot $L(s)$ as $s$ travels up the whole $j\omega$ axis and around the right half-plane (the Nyquist contour). Then

$$
Z = N + P
$$

- $P$ = number of **open-loop** poles in the right half-plane (known from $L$),
- $N$ = number of **clockwise** encirclements of the point $-1$ by the Nyquist plot,
- $Z$ = number of **closed-loop** poles in the right half-plane. Stable if and only if $Z = 0$.

For the common case of a stable open loop ($P = 0$), the rule is simply: **the plot must not encircle $-1$**. An unstable open loop ($P > 0$) can be stabilized by feedback, but only if the plot circles $-1$ **counter-clockwise** $P$ times ($N = -P$).

The margins have a Nyquist reading too. The gain margin is how far the plot's crossing of the negative real axis is from $-1$ (here at $-1/11$). The phase margin is the angle between the plot's crossing of the unit circle and the negative real axis.

## Loop shaping in one paragraph

Good loops share a shape: **high gain at low frequency** (accurate tracking, disturbance rejection), a crossover with a slope of about $-20$ dB/decade and enough phase margin, then **low gain at high frequency** (sensor noise ignored, unmodeled dynamics left alone). Lead compensation adds phase near $\omega_c$; lag compensation adds low-frequency gain. That is the frequency-domain version of the previous lesson.

## Key takeaways

- Phase margin $= 180° + \angle L$ at $|L| = 1$; gain margin $= 1/|L|$ where $\angle L = -180°$.
- Aim for $\text{PM} \ge 45°$, $\text{GM} \ge 6$ dB; $\zeta \approx \text{PM}/100$.
- Nyquist: $Z = N + P$. For a stable open loop, don't encircle $-1$.
- Shape $|L|$: big at low frequency, crossing with good phase, small at high frequency.
