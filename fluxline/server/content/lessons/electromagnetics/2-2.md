## Fields from charges

Electrostatics is governed by two point laws. Charge is the source of the electric flux density $\mathbf D = \varepsilon\mathbf E$, and static $\mathbf E$ has no curl:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf D = \rho_v, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf E = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf E = -\nabla V
$$

In integral form, the first is **Gauss's law**, $\oint\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf s = Q_{enc}$, which gives fields in one line whenever symmetry lets you pull $D$ out of the integral (Physics: Electricity & Magnetism, Lesson 1.2).

## Poisson's and Laplace's equations

Substituting $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$ into Gauss's law, in a uniform material:

$$
\nabla^2V = -\frac{\rho_v}{\varepsilon} \quad\text{(Poisson)}, \qquad \nabla^2V = 0 \quad\text{(Laplace, charge-free regions)}
$$

Most practical problems are **boundary-value problems**: the potentials on the conductors are known, and Laplace's equation fills in the space between. The solution is unique, so any method that satisfies the equation and the boundary conditions is the answer.

> **Parallel plates.** Plates at $x = 0$ ($V = 0$) and $x = d$ ($V = V_0$). In one dimension, $d^2V/dx^2 = 0$ gives $V = V_0x/d$: a uniform field $E = V_0/d$.

> **Coaxial cable.** Inner conductor radius $a$ at $V_0$, outer radius $b$ grounded. In cylindrical coordinates, $\frac1\rho\frac{d}{d\rho}\big(\rho\frac{dV}{d\rho}\big) = 0$ gives $V = V_0\ln(b/\rho)/\ln(b/a)$, and the capacitance per unit length is
> $$C' = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon}{\ln(b/a)}$$
> With $b/a = 3.5$ and polyethylene ($\varepsilon_r = 2.25$): $C' = 99.9$ pF/m, the cable from Lesson 1.1.

For irregular shapes (a PCB trace over a ground plane), Laplace's equation is solved numerically: that's what field solvers inside PCB tools do to compute trace impedance.

## Boundary conditions

At an interface between two materials, apply the integral laws to a thin pillbox and a thin loop straddling the boundary:

| Component | Condition |
|---|---|
| tangential $\mathbf E$ | continuous: $E_{1t} = E_{2t}$ |
| normal $\mathbf D$ | jumps by the surface charge: $D_{1n} - D_{2n} = \rho_s$ |

**At a conductor surface** (where $\mathbf E = 0$ inside): $\mathbf E$ outside is purely **normal** to the surface, with $D_n = \rho_s$. Field lines always meet conductors at right angles.

> **Dielectric interface example.** In region 1 ($\varepsilon_r = 2$), $\mathbf E_1$ has normal component 3 V/m and tangential component 4 V/m. Region 2 has $\varepsilon_r = 4$, with no surface charge.
>
> $E_{2t} = 4$ V/m; $D_n$ continuous: $2(3) = 4E_{2n}$, so $E_{2n} = 1.5$ V/m.
>
> The field bends toward the surface in the higher-permittivity material ($36.9°$ from the surface becomes $20.6°$).

## The method of images

A point charge $Q$ at height $h$ above a grounded conducting plane: the plane must stay at $V = 0$. Remove the plane and place an **image charge** $-Q$ at depth $h$ below it. The pair produces $V = 0$ on the whole midplane, satisfies Laplace's equation above it, so by uniqueness it gives the **correct field above the plane**.

```circuit
LINE -2.4,0 2.4,0
TEXT 2.5,0 "V = 0 plane" anchor=start color=muted size=12
Q 0,-1.4 + Q
Q 0,1.4 − −Q
LINE 0.7,-1.4 0.7,1.4 dash
TEXT 0.85,-0.7 h anchor=start color=muted size=13
TEXT 0.85,0.7 h anchor=start color=muted size=13
caption: The method of images. Above the plane, the field of $Q$ plus its image $-Q$ is exactly the field of $Q$ plus the induced surface charge on the plane.
```

The charge is attracted to the plane with the force between $Q$ and its image:

$$
F = \frac{Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(2h)^2}
$$

Images also explain why a trace's impedance depends on its height above the ground plane, and why a wire near a plane has higher capacitance than a wire alone.

## Key takeaways

- Poisson $\nabla^2V = -\rho_v/\varepsilon$; Laplace in charge-free regions; solutions are unique.
- Coax: $C' = 2\pi\varepsilon/\ln(b/a)$.
- Tangential $E$ continuous; normal $D$ jumps by $\rho_s$; fields meet conductors at right angles.
- Images replace a grounded plane with a mirror charge of opposite sign.
