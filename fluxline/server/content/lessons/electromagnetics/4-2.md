## How a wire launches a wave

An antenna is the transition between a guided wave (on a cable) and a free wave (in space). Any **accelerating charge** radiates, so any conductor carrying a time-varying current radiates somewhat. An antenna is a conductor shaped to do it efficiently and in a chosen direction.

## The Hertzian dipole

The building block is a very short current element of length $\ell \ll \lambda$ carrying a uniform current $I_0\cos\omega t$ along $z$. Its vector potential (Lesson 2.3) points along $z$, and taking curls gives its fields. Far away ($r \gg \lambda$), only the **radiation fields** survive:

$$
E_\theta = j\eta\,\frac{kI_0\ell}{4\pi r}\,e^{-jkr}\sin\theta, \qquad H_\phi = \frac{E_\theta}{\eta}
$$

Three features are true of every antenna's far field:

- The fields fall as $1/r$, so the power density falls as $1/r^2$ and the total power through any large sphere is the same (energy really leaves).
- $\mathbf E$, $\mathbf H$ and the outward direction are mutually perpendicular, with $E/H = \eta$: locally, a plane wave (Lesson 3.2).
- The strength depends on direction through a **pattern** factor, here $\sin\theta$: maximum broadside ($\theta = 90°$), **zero along the wire's axis**.

## Radiation resistance and efficiency

The radiated power can be written as if the antenna were a resistor:

$$
P_{rad} = \tfrac12I_0^2R_{rad}, \qquad R_{rad} = 80\pi^2\left(\frac{\ell}{\lambda}\right)^2 \ \text{(Hertzian dipole)}
$$

A dipole $\lambda/50$ long has $R_{rad} = 0.32\ \Omega$. The conductor's own loss resistance competes with it, so the **radiation efficiency**

$$
e = \frac{R_{rad}}{R_{rad} + R_{loss}}
$$

is poor for electrically small antennas. That's why phone and smartwatch antennas are hard to design, and why AM broadcast towers are so tall.

## The half-wave dipole

The practical workhorse: two quarter-wave arms fed in the middle, with a standing-wave current that's largest at the feed. Its properties:

- Radiation resistance about **73 Ω**, an easy match to 50 or 75 Ω cable.
- Pattern $\dfrac{\cos\!\big(\frac{\pi}{2}\cos\theta\big)}{\sin\theta}$, a bit narrower than the Hertzian dipole's: half-power beamwidth $78°$ vs. $90°$.
- Directivity 1.64 (2.15 dBi). A quarter-wave **monopole** over a ground plane is half a dipole, with 36.5 Ω.

## Directivity and gain

**Directivity** $D$ compares the peak power density to that of an **isotropic** radiator (one that radiates equally in all directions) with the same total power. The Hertzian dipole has $D = 1.5$ (1.76 dBi). **Gain** includes efficiency: $G = eD$. High-gain antennas (dishes, Yagis, arrays) concentrate power into a narrow beam.

An antenna works identically transmitting and receiving (**reciprocity**). As a receiver, it captures power as if it had an **effective aperture**:

$$
A_e = \frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi}\,G
$$

## The Friis transmission equation

Combine the transmitter's gain, the spreading loss, and the receiver's aperture, for antennas in each other's far field:

$$
P_r = P_tG_tG_r\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi R}\right)^2
$$

In decibels, every term adds:

$$
P_r\,[\text{dBm}] = P_t\,[\text{dBm}] + G_t\,[\text{dBi}] + G_r\,[\text{dBi}] - 20\log_{10}\frac{4\pi R}{\lambda}
$$

The last term is the **free-space path loss**. It grows 6 dB for every doubling of distance.

> **Example: Wi-Fi link.** 2.4 GHz ($\lambda = 12.5$ cm), 100 mW (20 dBm), 2 dBi antennas at both ends, 100 m apart.
>
> Path loss $= 20\log_{10}(4\pi \cdot 100/0.125) = 80.0$ dB.
>
> $P_r = 20 + 2 + 2 - 80.0 = -56.0$ dBm, comfortably above a typical receiver sensitivity of $-80$ dBm. Walls and bodies eat the remaining margin indoors.

The far-field condition is roughly $R > 2D^2/\lambda$, where $D$ is the antenna's largest dimension. Closer than that, the pattern hasn't formed yet.

## Key takeaways

- Far fields fall as $1/r$, with $E/H = \eta$; the pattern gives the directional dependence.
- Hertzian dipole: $R_{rad} = 80\pi^2(\ell/\lambda)^2$, $D = 1.5$; half-wave dipole: 73 Ω, $D = 1.64$ (2.15 dBi).
- Gain $= $ efficiency $\times$ directivity; effective aperture $A_e = \lambda^2G/4\pi$.
- Friis: $P_r = P_tG_tG_r(\lambda/4\pi R)^2$; path loss rises 6 dB per doubling of distance.
