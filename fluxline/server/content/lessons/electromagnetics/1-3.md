## A calculator made of circles

Before computers, RF engineers solved $Z_{in}$ problems with a graphical calculator invented by Phillip Smith at Bell Labs in 1939. It's still on every network analyzer screen, because it shows at a glance how far a load is from a match and which component will fix it.

## The idea

Normalize impedances to the line: $z = Z/Z_0 = r + jx$. Then plot not $z$ but the reflection coefficient

$$
\Gamma = \frac{z - 1}{z + 1}
$$

in the complex plane. Every passive impedance lands inside the unit circle $|\Gamma| \le 1$. The grid lines of constant $r$ become **circles** all touching the right edge, and lines of constant $x$ become **arcs**. The center is the perfect match ($z = 1$); the left edge is a short ($z = 0$), the right edge is an open ($z = \infty$); the upper half is inductive, the lower half capacitive.

The chart's power comes from how a line transforms an impedance. Moving a distance $d$ toward the generator multiplies $\Gamma$ by $e^{-j2\beta d}$: the magnitude stays the same, only the angle changes. **Moving along a lossless line is a rotation about the center**, clockwise toward the generator:

- A full turn is $\lambda/2$ (the impedance repeats every half wavelength).
- A half turn is $\lambda/4$: a point maps to its mirror image through the center, which is exactly the quarter-wave inversion $z \to 1/z$.
- The circle you rotate along is the **constant-VSWR circle**; it crosses the right half of the real axis at $r = \text{VSWR}$.

Because of that half-turn property, the same chart reads **admittances** $y = 1/z$: rotate any impedance point by $180°$ and read the grid as $g + jb$. Since shunt elements add admittances, the admittance view is the natural one for stub matching.

## Single-stub matching

A **shunt stub** is a short length of shorted (or open) line connected in parallel with the main line. It adds a pure susceptance, so it can cancel the imaginary part of $y$, but it can't change the real part. The procedure:

1. Normalize the load and convert to admittance: $y_L = Z_0/Z_L$.
2. Rotate toward the generator along the VSWR circle until you hit the **$g = 1$ circle**, at $y = 1 + jb$. That distance is $d$.
3. Place a stub there with susceptance $-jb$. Now $y = 1$: matched.
4. For a shorted stub, $y_{stub} = -j\cot\beta\ell$, so choose $\ell$ with $\cot\beta\ell = b$.

> **Example.** Match $Z_L = 100\ \Omega$ to a 50 Ω line.
>
> $y_L = 50/100 = 0.5$, on the real axis at $\Gamma = -1/3$ (VSWR 2).
>
> Rotating toward the generator, the VSWR circle meets $g = 1$ after $d = 0.152\lambda$, at $y = 1 + j0.707$. You can check that with the $Z_{in}$ formula of the last lesson.
>
> A shorted stub must supply $-j0.707$: $\cot\beta\ell = 0.707$, so $\beta\ell = 54.7°$ and $\ell = 0.152\lambda$.
>
> There's a second solution further along the circle at $y = 1 - j0.707$ ($d = 0.348\lambda$), which would need an inductive stub instead.

Shorted stubs are preferred in coax and waveguide (a short is easy to make well; an open radiates), while microstrip often uses open stubs (no via to ground needed).

## Lumped-element matching

Below a few gigahertz, stubs get long, so an **L-network** of one series and one shunt reactance (inductors and capacitors) does the same job on the Smith chart. A series element moves the point along a constant-$r$ circle; a shunt element moves it along a constant-$g$ circle. Two moves are enough to reach the center from almost anywhere. That's the basis of every antenna and RF amplifier matching network.

## Key takeaways

- The Smith chart plots $\Gamma = (z - 1)/(z + 1)$ with $r$ and $x$ grids; the center is a match.
- Moving along a lossless line rotates the point clockwise (toward the generator); a full turn is $\lambda/2$.
- A half turn converts $z$ to $y$; use the admittance view for shunt elements.
- Single-stub match: rotate to the $g = 1$ circle, then cancel $jb$ with a stub.
