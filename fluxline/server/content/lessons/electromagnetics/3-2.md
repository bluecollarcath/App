## Waves with no wires

In a source-free region, take the curl of Faraday's law and substitute the Ampère–Maxwell law. With phasors, the result is the **Helmholtz (wave) equation**:

$$
\nabla^2\mathbf E + k^2\mathbf E = 0, \qquad k = \omega\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}
$$

The simplest solution is a **uniform plane wave**: fields that vary only along the direction of travel, and are uniform across infinite planes perpendicular to it. Far from any antenna, every wave looks like this locally.

## The lossless plane wave

For a wave traveling in $+z$ with $\mathbf E$ along $x$:

$$
\mathbf E(z, t) = E_0\cos(\omega t - kz)\,\hat{\mathbf x}, \qquad
\mathbf H(z, t) = \frac{E_0}{\eta}\cos(\omega t - kz)\,\hat{\mathbf y}
$$

Its properties:

- **Speed** $u_p = \omega/k = 1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon} = c/\sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}$; in vacuum, $c = 3\times10^8$ m/s.
- **Wavelength** $\lambda = 2\pi/k = u_p/f$.
- **Transverse:** $\mathbf E$, $\mathbf H$ and the direction of travel are mutually perpendicular ($\mathbf E\times\mathbf H$ points along the motion). It's a "TEM" wave.
- **Intrinsic impedance** $\eta = E/H = \sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}$. In free space,
$$\eta_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} \approx 120\pi \approx 377\ \Omega$$

$\eta$ plays the role $Z_0$ played on transmission lines; in fact, a TEM transmission line is a plane wave guided between two conductors.

## Power: the Poynting vector

The power flow per unit area is the **Poynting vector** $\mathbf S = \mathbf E\times\mathbf H$. Averaged over a cycle, for a plane wave of peak amplitude $E_0$:

$$
S_{avg} = \frac{E_0^2}{2\eta}
$$

> A 10 V/m peak field in free space carries $100/(2 \times 377) = 0.133$ W/m². Sunlight at noon is about 1000 W/m², a peak field of about 870 V/m.

## Lossy media

With conductivity, the wave number becomes complex, $\gamma = \alpha + j\beta$, and the wave **decays** as it travels:

$$
E(z) = E_0e^{-\alpha z}\cos(\omega t - \beta z)
$$

The fields fall by $1/e$ (8.7 dB) every $1/\alpha$ meters. In a **good conductor** ($\sigma \gg \omega\varepsilon$), $\alpha = \beta = \sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$, and the decay distance is the **skin depth**:

$$
\delta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}}
$$

| Copper ($\sigma = 5.8\times10^7$ S/m) | $\delta$ |
|---|---|
| 60 Hz | 8.5 mm |
| 1 MHz | 66 µm |
| 1 GHz | 2.1 µm |


The practical consequences are everywhere:

- At high frequency, current crowds into the outer skin of a wire, raising its resistance. PCB traces at GHz rates are effectively only a few microns thick; RF parts are silver-plated; low-frequency coils use **litz wire** made of many thin insulated strands.
- A thin metal enclosure blocks radio waves: a few skin depths of shielding attenuate by tens of decibels.
- Power-line conductors thicker than about 2 cm gain little, so large cables use aluminum strands around a steel core.

## Key takeaways

- Plane waves: $u_p = 1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}$, $\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}$ ($\eta_0 \approx 377\ \Omega$), with $\mathbf E \perp \mathbf H \perp$ direction of travel.
- Power density $S_{avg} = E_0^2/2\eta$.
- In good conductors, fields decay with skin depth $\delta = 1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$; at high frequency current flows only near the surface.
