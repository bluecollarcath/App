## The language of fields

Voltages and currents live on wires. **Fields** live everywhere in space: at every point there's an electric field vector $\mathbf E$ and a magnetic field vector $\mathbf H$. To write laws about them we need calculus on vector fields, built from one operator, **del**:

$$
\nabla = \hat{\mathbf x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat{\mathbf y}\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat{\mathbf z}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
$$

## Gradient: which way is uphill

For a scalar field $V(x, y, z)$, the **gradient** $\nabla V$ points in the direction of steepest increase, and its magnitude is the rate of increase in that direction. The electric field is the downhill direction of the potential:

$$
\mathbf E = -\nabla V
$$

> $V = 3x + 4y$ volts (with $x, y$ in meters): $\nabla V = 3\hat{\mathbf x} + 4\hat{\mathbf y}$, so $|\mathbf E| = 5$ V/m, pointing toward decreasing $V$.

## Divergence: sources and sinks

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}
$$

is the net **outflow** per unit volume at a point. Positive divergence means a source (field lines start there), negative means a sink. Electric charge is the source of $\mathbf D$: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D = \rho_v$.

> $\mathbf A = x^2y\,\hat{\mathbf x} + yz\,\hat{\mathbf y} + xz\,\hat{\mathbf z}$: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 2xy + z + x$. At $(1, 2, 3)$ that's $4 + 3 + 1 = 8$.

**Divergence theorem.** The total outflow through a closed surface equals the integrated divergence inside:

$$
\oint_S \mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf s = \int_V \nabla\cdot\mathbf A\,dv
$$

> $\mathbf A = \mathbf r = x\hat{\mathbf x} + y\hat{\mathbf y} + z\hat{\mathbf z}$ has $\nabla\cdot\mathbf r = 3$ everywhere. Through a sphere of radius $a$: volume integral $3 \cdot \frac43\pi a^3 = 4\pi a^3$. Directly: $\mathbf r\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} = a$ on the surface, times area $4\pi a^2$: the same $4\pi a^3$.

## Curl: circulation

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf A =
\begin{vmatrix}
\hat{\mathbf x} & \hat{\mathbf y} & \hat{\mathbf z} \\
\partial_x & \partial_y & \partial_z \\
A_x & A_y & A_z
\end{vmatrix}
$$

measures how much a field **swirls** around a point: put a tiny paddle wheel in a flowing fluid, and the curl is its spin axis (direction) and spin rate (magnitude). Currents are the source of magnetic circulation: $\nabla\times\mathbf H = \mathbf J$ (for static fields).

> $\mathbf A = -y\,\hat{\mathbf x} + x\,\hat{\mathbf y}$, a rigid rotation: $\nabla\times\mathbf A = 2\hat{\mathbf z}$ everywhere.

**Stokes' theorem.** The circulation around a closed loop equals the flux of the curl through any surface bounded by it:

$$
\oint_C \mathbf A\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = \int_S (\nabla\times\mathbf A)\cdot d\mathbf s
$$

> For the rotation above around a circle of radius $a$: $|\mathbf A| = a$ along the path, so the circulation is $a \cdot 2\pi a = 2\pi a^2$, matching the curl $2$ times the area $\pi a^2$.

Two identities you'll use constantly: $\nabla\times(\nabla V) = 0$ (gradient fields don't circulate, which is why static $\mathbf E$ has a potential) and $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A) = 0$ (curl fields have no sources, which is why $\mathbf B$ can be written as a curl).

## Coordinate systems

Choose coordinates that match the geometry's symmetry, and the integrals become one-dimensional.

| System | Coordinates | Natural for | Volume element $dv$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cartesian | $x, y, z$ | planes, boxes, PCB traces | $dx\,dy\,dz$ |
| Cylindrical | $\rho, \phi, z$ | wires, coax, solenoids | $\rho\,d\rho\,d\phi\,dz$ |
| Spherical | $r, \theta, \phi$ | point charges, antennas | $r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi$ |

Conversions: $\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, $\phi = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$ (in the correct quadrant); $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$, $\theta = \cos^{-1}(z/r)$.

The extra factors ($\rho$, $r^2\sin\theta$) also appear inside the divergence and curl formulas in curvilinear coordinates. For example, for a field that depends only on $r$:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\big(r^2A_r\big)
$$

Applied to a point charge's $D_r = Q/(4\pi r^2)$, the product $r^2D_r$ is constant, so the divergence is zero everywhere except at the charge itself.

## Key takeaways

- Gradient: steepest increase; $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$.
- Divergence: outflow per volume (sources); divergence theorem links it to surface flux.
- Curl: circulation per area; Stokes' theorem links it to line integrals.
- Pick Cartesian, cylindrical or spherical coordinates to match the symmetry.
