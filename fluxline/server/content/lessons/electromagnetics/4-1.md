## Pipes for microwaves

Above about 10 GHz, coaxial cable gets lossy, mostly from the skin-effect resistance of its thin center conductor. A **waveguide** removes the center conductor entirely: a hollow metal pipe, usually rectangular, that guides waves by bouncing them between its walls. It handles high power with very low loss, and it's used in radar, satellite ground stations and particle accelerators.

The catch: a hollow pipe can't carry DC or low frequencies. There's no second conductor for a return current, so no TEM wave is possible. Only **modes** with field patterns that fit inside the walls can propagate, and each has a **cutoff frequency**.

## Modes in a rectangular guide

For a guide with inside width $a$ and height $b$ ($a > b$), solving the wave equation with the boundary condition "tangential $\mathbf E = 0$ on the walls" gives two families:

- **TE$_{mn}$** (transverse electric): no $E$ component along the guide.
- **TM$_{mn}$** (transverse magnetic): no $H$ component along the guide; needs $m, n \ge 1$.

The integers $m$ and $n$ count half-wave variations across the width and the height. Each mode has a cutoff frequency

$$
f_{c,mn} = \frac{u}{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac{m}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{n}{b}\right)^2}
$$

where $u = 1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}$ (just $c$ for an air-filled guide). Below $f_c$, the mode doesn't propagate; its fields decay exponentially (it's **evanescent**).

## The dominant TE$_{10}$ mode

The lowest cutoff belongs to TE$_{10}$: $f_{c,10} = u/2a$. Physically, the guide must be at least half a wavelength wide. Its electric field points across the narrow dimension, peaking in the middle and vanishing at the side walls:

Waveguides are operated in the band where **only** TE$_{10}$ propagates, between its cutoff and the next mode's cutoff (TE$_{20}$ at $2f_{c,10}$, or TE$_{01}$ at $u/2b$ if $b > a/2$). Then the field pattern is predictable and no power leaks into other modes.

> **Example: WR-90 (X-band) guide**, $a = 22.86$ mm, $b = 10.16$ mm, air-filled.
>
> TE$_{10}$: $f_c = 3\times10^8/(2 \times 0.02286) = 6.56$ GHz. TE$_{20}$: 13.12 GHz. TE$_{01}$: 14.76 GHz.
>
> Single-mode operation from 6.56 to 13.12 GHz; the recommended band is 8.2–12.4 GHz, safely inside it.

## Propagation above cutoff

A mode above cutoff travels with the phase constant

$$
\beta = \frac{\omega}{u}\sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{f_c}{f}\right)^2}
$$

which gives a **guide wavelength** longer than in free space, and two different velocities:

$$
\lambda_g = \frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2}}, \qquad
u_p = \frac{u}{\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2}} > u, \qquad
u_g = u\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2} < u
$$

The phase velocity exceeds the speed of light, but no information travels at $u_p$: energy and signals move at the **group velocity** $u_g$, and $u_pu_g = u^2$. The picture behind this: the mode is two plane waves zigzagging between the side walls. Their crests sweep along the wall faster than light, while the energy, traveling along the zigzag, makes slower progress down the guide. Near cutoff the zigzag becomes nearly side-to-side and $u_g \to 0$.

> **WR-90 at 10 GHz:** $f_c/f = 0.656$, so $\sqrt{1 - 0.656^2} = 0.755$. Free-space $\lambda = 3$ cm; $\lambda_g = 3.98$ cm; $u_p = 3.98\times10^8$ m/s; $u_g = 2.26\times10^8$ m/s.

Because $u_g$ depends on frequency, waveguides are **dispersive**: different frequencies in a pulse travel at different speeds, so short pulses spread out over long runs.

Other guiding structures follow the same ideas: **circular** waveguides, **dielectric** guides such as optical fibers (guided by total internal reflection, Lesson 3.3), and the planar lines on circuit boards (microstrip and stripline, which are quasi-TEM).

## Key takeaways

- Hollow guides support TE and TM modes, each above its cutoff $f_c = (u/2)\sqrt{(m/a)^2 + (n/b)^2}$.
- TE$_{10}$ is dominant ($f_c = u/2a$); operate where only it propagates.
- $\lambda_g$ and $u_p$ exceed their free-space values; energy travels at $u_g < u$, with $u_pu_g = u^2$.
