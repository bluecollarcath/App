## Waves meeting a boundary

When a plane wave hits the boundary between two materials, part reflects and part transmits, exactly as on a transmission line at an impedance step. The intrinsic impedances $\eta_1$ and $\eta_2$ play the role of $Z_0$ and $Z_L$.

## Normal incidence

For a wave arriving straight on (perpendicular to the boundary), the boundary conditions (tangential $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf H$ continuous) give

$$
\Gamma = \frac{E_r}{E_i} = \frac{\eta_2 - \eta_1}{\eta_2 + \eta_1}, \qquad
\tau = \frac{E_t}{E_i} = 1 + \Gamma = \frac{2\eta_2}{\eta_2 + \eta_1}
$$

Power splits as $|\Gamma|^2$ reflected and $1 - |\Gamma|^2$ transmitted (for lossless media).

> **Example: air to glass** ($\varepsilon_r = 4$). $\eta_2 = 377/2 = 188.5\ \Omega$, so $\Gamma = (188.5 - 377)/(188.5 + 377) = -1/3$ and $\tau = 2/3$. 11% of the power reflects, which is why you see your reflection in a window, and 89% passes through. (Anti-reflection coatings are quarter-wave transformers, Lesson 1.2, made of thin films.)

**A perfect conductor** has $\eta_2 = 0$, so $\Gamma = -1$: total reflection, with a **standing wave** in front of the conductor whose electric field is zero at the surface and at every half wavelength away from it. Radar works because metal reflects so well.

## Oblique incidence

At an angle $\theta_i$ from the normal, the reflected wave leaves at $\theta_r = \theta_i$, and the transmitted wave bends according to **Snell's law**:

$$
n_1\sin\theta_i = n_2\sin\theta_t, \qquad n = \sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}
$$

> Light entering water ($n = 1.33$) at $45°$ continues at $\sin^{-1}(\sin45°/1.33) = 32.1°$ from the normal.

The reflection coefficients now depend on **polarization**:

- **TE** (perpendicular, or s-) polarization: $\mathbf E$ parallel to the boundary.
- **TM** (parallel, or p-) polarization: $\mathbf E$ in the plane of incidence.

These are the **Fresnel equations**:

$$
\Gamma_{TE} = \frac{n_1\cos\theta_i - n_2\cos\theta_t}{n_1\cos\theta_i + n_2\cos\theta_t}, \qquad
\Gamma_{TM} = \frac{n_2\cos\theta_i - n_1\cos\theta_t}{n_2\cos\theta_i + n_1\cos\theta_t}
$$

Two special angles:

- **Brewster angle** $\tan\theta_B = n_2/n_1$: TM waves transmit **completely**, with no reflection. Light reflected at that angle is purely TE-polarized, which is why polarized sunglasses (which block horizontal $\mathbf E$) cut glare from water and roads.
- **Critical angle** $\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1$, going from a denser to a less dense medium ($n_1 > n_2$): beyond it there is **total internal reflection**. Optical fibers trap light this way along kilometers of glass.

> Glass ($n = 1.5$) to air: $\theta_c = \sin^{-1}(1/1.5) = 41.8°$.

## Polarization

The **polarization** of a wave is the shape traced by the tip of $\mathbf E$ over time, seen looking along the direction of travel:

- **Linear:** $\mathbf E$ oscillates along a fixed line (the components along $x$ and $y$ are in phase). FM and TV broadcasting use linear polarization.
- **Circular:** two equal, perpendicular components $90°$ out of phase; the tip traces a circle. GPS and satellite links use it, because the orientation of the receiving antenna then doesn't matter.
- **Elliptical:** the general case, any other amplitude ratio or phase.

A linearly polarized antenna receiving a circularly polarized wave loses half the power (3 dB); a vertical antenna receiving a horizontal wave receives almost nothing.

## Key takeaways

- Normal incidence: $\Gamma = (\eta_2 - \eta_1)/(\eta_2 + \eta_1)$, $\tau = 1 + \Gamma$; a perfect conductor gives $\Gamma = -1$.
- Snell: $n_1\sin\theta_i = n_2\sin\theta_t$; Fresnel coefficients differ for TE and TM.
- Brewster ($\tan\theta_B = n_2/n_1$): no TM reflection. Critical angle ($\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1$): total internal reflection.
- Polarization is linear, circular or elliptical; mismatched polarization costs received power.
