## Fields from currents

Static charges make electric fields; steady **currents** make magnetic fields. The two point laws of magnetostatics are

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf H = \mathbf J, \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0
$$

with $\mathbf B = \mu\mathbf H$. The first says currents are the source of magnetic **circulation** (field lines wrap around currents); the second says there are no magnetic charges, so $\mathbf B$ lines always close on themselves.

## Ampère's law and Biot–Savart

In integral form, the curl law is **Ampère's circuital law**:

$$
\oint_C \mathbf H\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = I_{enc}
$$

Like Gauss's law, it gives the field in one line when symmetry lets you pull $H$ out of the integral.

> **Long straight wire.** By symmetry $\mathbf H$ circles the wire with constant magnitude on any circle of radius $\rho$: $H \cdot 2\pi\rho = I$, so
> $$
> B = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi\rho}
> $$
> 10 A at 5 cm gives $B = 40\ \mu$T, comparable to the Earth's field. That's why a compass near a power cable misreads.

For geometries without that symmetry, the **Biot–Savart law** adds up the contribution of each current element:

$$
d\mathbf H = \frac{I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\hat{\mathbf R}}{4\pi R^2}
$$

> **Solenoid.** With $n$ turns per meter, Ampère's law around a rectangle half inside gives a uniform interior field $B = \mu nI$. For 1000 turns/m carrying 2 A in air, $B = 2.51$ mT.

## The vector potential

Because $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$, $\mathbf B$ can always be written as the curl of a **magnetic vector potential**:

$$
\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A, \qquad \mathbf A = \frac{\mu}{4\pi}\int\frac{\mathbf J\,dv}{R}
$$

$\mathbf A$ points along the currents that produce it, which makes it much easier to compute than $\mathbf B$ directly. It returns in Lesson 4.2, where it's the starting point for antenna radiation.

## Inductance

**Inductance** is flux linkage per unit current:

$$
L = \frac{N\Phi}{I} = \frac{\Lambda}{I}
$$

It depends only on geometry and material, just as capacitance does.

> **Coax.** Between the conductors, $B = \mu I/(2\pi\rho)$. The flux per unit length is $\int_a^b B\,d\rho$, so
> $$
> L' = \frac{\mu}{2\pi}\ln\frac{b}{a}
> $$
> For $b/a = 3.5$: $L' = 250.6$ nH/m. Together with the $C' = 99.9$ pF/m found in the last lesson, that gives $Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'} = 50\ \Omega$. Note that $L'C' = \mu\varepsilon$ for **any** two-conductor line: the wave speed depends only on the material.

> **Core inductor.** $N$ turns on a closed core of permeability $\mu$, cross-section $A$ and mean path length $\ell$: $L = \mu N^2A/\ell$. For $N = 100$, $A = 1\ \text{cm}^2$, $\ell = 10$ cm and $\mu_r = 1000$: $L = 12.6$ mH.

The energy stored is $W = \tfrac12LI^2$, located in the field with density $\tfrac12\mu H^2$.

## Magnetic materials and boundary conditions

- **Diamagnetic and paramagnetic** materials (copper, aluminum, air) have $\mu_r \approx 1$.
- **Ferromagnetic** materials (iron, ferrite) have $\mu_r$ from hundreds to 100,000. They also **saturate**: above a few hundred mT (ferrite) to about 1.5 T (steel), $\mu_r$ collapses. And they show **hysteresis**, a loop in the B–H curve whose area is energy lost every cycle. Both shape inductor and transformer design (Power Electronics, Electric Machines).

At an interface, the pillbox and loop arguments give the magnetic counterparts of the electrostatic conditions:

| Component | Condition |
|---|---|
| normal $\mathbf B$ | continuous: $B_{1n} = B_{2n}$ |
| tangential $\mathbf H$ | jumps by the surface current: $H_{1t} - H_{2t} = J_s$ |

Because $B_n$ is continuous while $\mu$ jumps by a factor of 1000, flux entering a high-$\mu$ core is pulled almost parallel to its surface and stays inside. That's why cores guide flux the way wires guide current.

## Key takeaways

- $\nabla\times\mathbf H = \mathbf J$ (Ampère), $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$; wire field $B = \mu_0I/2\pi\rho$; solenoid $B = \mu nI$.
- $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$, with $\mathbf A$ parallel to the currents.
- $L = N\Phi/I$; coax $L' = (\mu/2\pi)\ln(b/a)$; core $L = \mu N^2A/\ell$; $L'C' = \mu\varepsilon$.
- $B_n$ continuous, $H_t$ jumps by $J_s$; ferromagnets saturate and show hysteresis.
