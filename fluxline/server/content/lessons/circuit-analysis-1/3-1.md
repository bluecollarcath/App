## The big idea

Any linear two-terminal network — no matter how many sources and resistors are inside — behaves, at its terminals, **exactly** like:

- **Thévenin:** a single voltage source $V_{th}$ in series with a resistance $R_{th}$, or
- **Norton:** a single current source $I_N$ in parallel with a resistance $R_N = R_{th}$.

This is the most practically useful theorem in circuit analysis. It lets you analyze a complicated source network once, then quickly evaluate many different loads. It is also how engineers think about every real source: a battery, a sensor, an amplifier output, a power outlet all have a Thévenin equivalent.

## Finding the equivalent

**Open-circuit voltage.** Remove the load and compute the voltage across the open terminals:

$$
V_{th} = v_{oc}
$$

**Short-circuit current.** Short the terminals and compute the current through the short:

$$
I_N = i_{sc}
$$

**Equivalent resistance.** Any two of these determine the third:

$$
R_{th} = \frac{v_{oc}}{i_{sc}}, \qquad V_{th} = I_N R_{th}
$$

**Shortcut for $R_{th}$ (independent sources only):** deactivate all independent sources — voltage sources become **shorts**, current sources become **opens** — and find the resistance seen from the terminals.

**With dependent sources:** you may *not* deactivate them. Either use $v_{oc}/i_{sc}$, or deactivate independent sources, apply a test source $v_T$ at the terminals, compute the resulting $i_T$, and take $R_{th} = v_T / i_T$.

## Worked example

> A 12 V source in series with $R_1 = 6\ \Omega$ feeds a node that has $R_2 = 3\ \Omega$ to ground. From that node, $R_3 = 2\ \Omega$ leads to terminal $a$; terminal $b$ is ground.
>
```circuit
V 0,0 0,2 "12 V" side=-1
R 0,0 2,0 "R_1 = 6 Ω"
R 2,0 2,2 "R_2 = 3 Ω"
R 2,0 4,0 "R_3 = 2 Ω"
T 4,0 a
W 0,2 2,2
W 2,2 4,2
T 4,2 b
caption: The original network, seen from terminals $a$–$b$.
```

```circuit
V 0,0 0,2 "V_{th} = 4 V" side=-1
R 0,0 2.2,0 "R_{th} = 4 Ω"
T 2.2,0 a
W 0,2 2.2,2
T 2.2,2 b
caption: Its Thévenin equivalent. Any load connected to $a$–$b$ behaves identically with either circuit.
```

> **$V_{th}$:** with $a$–$b$ open, no current flows in $R_3$, so $v_{ab}$ equals the divider voltage: $12 \cdot \frac{3}{6+3} = 4$ V.
>
> **$R_{th}$:** short the 12 V source. Looking in from $a$–$b$: $R_3 + (R_1 \parallel R_2) = 2 + 2 = 4\ \Omega$.
>
> **Norton:** $I_N = 4/4 = 1$ A.
>
> Now any load is trivial. For $R_L = 12\ \Omega$: $i_L = \frac{4}{4+12} = 0.25$ A.

## Source transformation

The Thévenin and Norton forms are interchangeable anywhere in a circuit: a voltage source $v_s$ in series with $R$ is equivalent to a current source $v_s/R$ in parallel with $R$. Repeated source transformations can collapse a ladder network without writing a single equation.

## Maximum power transfer (preview)

The power delivered to a load $R_L$ from a Thévenin source is

$$
p_L = \left(\frac{V_{th}}{R_{th} + R_L}\right)^2 R_L
$$

Setting $dp_L/dR_L = 0$ gives $R_L = R_{th}$, and

$$
p_{L,\max} = \frac{V_{th}^2}{4R_{th}}
$$

At this point efficiency is only 50% — fine for signals and antennas, but power systems deliberately operate with $R_L \gg R_{th}$.

## Practice

```quiz

> $V_{th}^2 / (4R_{th}) = 16/16 = 1$ W, at $R_L = 4\ \Omega$.
```

> $R_{th} = v_{oc}/i_{sc} = 5 / 0.002 = 2500\ \Omega$.
```

```

## Key takeaways

- Every linear two-terminal network ≡ $V_{th}$ + series $R_{th}$ ≡ $I_N$ ∥ $R_N$.
- $V_{th} = v_{oc}$, $I_N = i_{sc}$, $R_{th} = v_{oc}/i_{sc}$.
- With dependent sources, use a test source — never zero them out.
- Max power transfer at $R_L = R_{th}$, with only 50% efficiency.
