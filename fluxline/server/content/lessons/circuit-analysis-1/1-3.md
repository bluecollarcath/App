## Series resistors

Elements are **in series** if they carry exactly the same current — they share a node with nothing else attached. For $N$ series resistors, KVL gives

$$
R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2 + \cdots + R_N
$$

## Parallel resistors

Elements are **in parallel** if they share the same two nodes, and therefore the same voltage. KCL gives

$$
\frac{1}{R_{eq}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \cdots + \frac{1}{R_N}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
G_{eq} = G_1 + G_2 + \cdots + G_N
$$

For exactly two resistors this becomes the "product over sum" rule:

$$
R_1 \parallel R_2 = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}
$$

Useful facts: $R \parallel R = R/2$; the parallel combination is always *smaller* than the smallest resistor; the series combination is always *larger* than the largest.

## The voltage divider

For series resistors driven by a voltage $v_s$, the voltage across $R_k$ is

$$
v_k = v_s \frac{R_k}{R_1 + R_2 + \cdots + R_N}
$$

For two resistors with output taken across $R_2$:

$$
v_{out} = v_s \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}
$$

**Loading.** If a load $R_L$ is connected across $R_2$, the output becomes $v_s \frac{R_2\parallel R_L}{R_1 + R_2\parallel R_L}$ — always lower. A divider is only accurate when $R_L \gg R_2$. This is why voltage dividers are used for *reference* and *sensing* voltages, not to power loads.

## The current divider

For parallel resistors fed by a total current $i_s$, the current through $R_k$ is

$$
i_k = i_s \frac{G_k}{G_1 + G_2 + \cdots + G_N}
$$

For two resistors, the current takes the *other* resistor in the numerator:

$$
i_1 = i_s \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}, \qquad i_2 = i_s \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2}
$$

Current prefers the path of least resistance — more current goes through the smaller resistor.

## Reducing a ladder network

> **Worked example.** Find $R_{eq}$ seen by a source connected to: $R_1 = 4\ \Omega$ in series with the parallel combination of $R_2 = 12\ \Omega$ and ($R_3 = 2\ \Omega$ in series with $R_4 = 4\ \Omega$).
>
> Work from the far end: $R_3 + R_4 = 6\ \Omega$. Then $12 \parallel 6 = \frac{72}{18} = 4\ \Omega$. Finally $R_{eq} = 4 + 4 = 8\ \Omega$.
>
> If the source is 16 V: total current is 2 A; the voltage across the parallel block is $16\cdot\frac{4}{8} = 8$ V; current through the 12 Ω branch is $8/12 = 0.667$ A and through the 6 Ω branch is $8/6 = 1.333$ A. ✓ (They sum to 2 A.)

```quiz

> $\frac{6\cdot3}{6+3} = 2$ kΩ.
```

```quiz
? A divider uses $R_1 = 10$ kΩ (top) and $R_2 = 5$ kΩ (bottom) across 9 V. What is the unloaded output across $R_2$?
```

```quiz
? The same divider (9 V, 10 kΩ top, 5 kΩ bottom) now drives a 5 kΩ load across $R_2$. What is the new output voltage?
= 1.8 | 0.01 | V
> $R_2 \parallel R_L = 2.5$ kΩ; $v = 9\cdot\frac{2.5}{12.5} = 1.8$ V — a 40% drop due to loading.
```

```quiz
= 24 | 0.01 | mA
> $i_{20} = 30 \cdot \frac{80}{20+80} = 24$ mA.
```


## Key takeaways

- Series: add resistances. Parallel: add conductances.
- Voltage divides in proportion to resistance; current divides in proportion to conductance.
- Loading reduces a divider's output — always check $R_L \gg R_2$.
