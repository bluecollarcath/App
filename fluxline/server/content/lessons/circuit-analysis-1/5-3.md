## A recipe for any first-order switching problem

Lesson 5.2 gave the general solution for a single capacitor or inductor driven by DC sources:

$$
x(t) = x(\infty) + \big[x(t_0^+) - x(\infty)\big]\,e^{-(t-t_0)/\tau}
$$

This lesson turns it into a reliable procedure and applies it to harder cases: switches that remove sources, inductive kickback, and switching more than once.

### The procedure

1. **Before switching ($t_0^-$):** assume the circuit has been in that state for a long time. Treat C as open and L as short. Find $v_C(t_0^-)$ or $i_L(t_0^-)$.
2. **Continuity:** $v_C(t_0^+) = v_C(t_0^-)$, $i_L(t_0^+) = i_L(t_0^-)$. If you need some *other* quantity at $t_0^+$, replace the capacitor with a voltage source of value $v_C(t_0^+)$ (or the inductor with a current source $i_L(t_0^+)$) and solve that resistive circuit.
3. **Final value:** after switching, let $t \to \infty$ with C open / L short.
4. **Time constant:** $\tau = R_{th}C$ or $L/R_{th}$, where $R_{th}$ is the resistance seen by the storage element **in the post-switching circuit**, with independent sources deactivated.
5. **Write the answer**, valid for $t \ge t_0$.

## Example 1: inductive kick

```circuit
V 0,0 0,2 "24 V" side=-1
R 0,0 1.8,0 "4 Ω"
SW 1.8,0 3.4,0 "opens at t = 0" closed
L 3.4,0 3.4,2 "0.5 H" i=i_L
W 3.4,0 5,0
R 5,0 5,2 "12 Ω"
W 0,2 3.4,2
W 3.4,2 5,2
caption: The switch has been closed for a long time and opens at $t = 0$.
```

> **Before:** the inductor is a short, so it carries all the current and the 12 Ω carries none: $i_L(0^-) = 24/4 = 6$ A.
>
> **After:** the source is disconnected. The inductor and the 12 Ω form a source-free loop: $i_L(\infty) = 0$, $\tau = L/R = 0.5/12 = 41.7$ ms.
>
> $$i_L(t) = 6\,e^{-24t}\ \text{A}, \quad t \ge 0$$
>
> The 6 A must now flow up through the 12 Ω resistor, so the voltage across the inductor jumps from 0 to $-72$ V at $t = 0^+$, three times the supply voltage. That's an **inductive kick**. With no resistor at all, the voltage would be large enough to arc across the switch contacts. This is why relay and motor drivers include flyback diodes, and how a car's ignition coil makes a spark from 12 V.

## Example 2: switching twice

> A 10 V source charges an uncharged 1 mF capacitor through 1 kΩ starting at $t = 0$ ($\tau_1 = 1$ s). At $t = 1$ s, a two-position switch disconnects the source and connects the capacitor to a 2 kΩ resistor instead ($\tau_2 = 2$ s).
>
> **Interval 1** ($0 \le t \le 1$ s): $v_C = 10(1 - e^{-t})$. At $t = 1$ s, $v_C = 6.32$ V.
>
> **Interval 2** ($t \ge 1$ s): initial value 6.32 V, final 0, $\tau_2 = 2$ s:
> $$v_C(t) = 6.32\,e^{-(t-1)/2}\ \text{V}$$
> At $t = 3$ s: $v_C = 6.32\,e^{-1} = 2.33$ V.
>
> ```plot
> x: 0, 8, t (s)
> y: 0, 10.5, v_C (V)
> f: (1 - u(x - 1))*10*(1 - exp(-x)) + u(x - 1)*6.3212*exp(-(x - 1)/2) | v_C(t)
> vline: 1, switch moves
> mark: 1, 6.32, 6.32 V
> mark: 3, 2.33, 2.33 V
> caption: Each interval is its own first-order response. The end of one interval sets the start of the next.
> ```

The key idea: **each interval is a separate first-order problem**, and continuity of $v_C$ (or $i_L$) links them. Note that the time shift $t - t_0$ appears in the exponent.

## Example 3: Thévenin first

> A 2 A current source in parallel with 6 Ω connects through 3 Ω to an uncharged 0.1 F capacitor when a switch closes at $t = 0$.
>
> Source transformation: 2 A ∥ 6 Ω becomes 12 V in series with 6 Ω. The capacitor sees $V_{th} = 12$ V and $R_{th} = 6 + 3 = 9\ \Omega$, so $\tau = 0.9$ s and
> $$v_C(t) = 12\big(1 - e^{-t/0.9}\big)\ \text{V}$$

## Key takeaways

- Before: C open, L short. Continuity carries $v_C$ and $i_L$ across the switch.
- $\tau$ uses the post-switching Thévenin resistance.
- Opening an inductor's current path produces a voltage spike.
- For multiple switchings, solve interval by interval with shifted time.
