## Every microamp counts

A wireless sensor on a coin cell, a fitness tracker, a smart meter that must run ten years on one battery: in these products, firmware and hardware design are dominated by **energy**. The good news is that microcontrollers can draw less than a microamp while asleep. The challenge is spending as much time as possible asleep.

## Where the power goes

A CMOS chip's power has two parts:

$$
P = \underbrace{\alpha CV^2f}_{\text{dynamic}} + \underbrace{V\,I_{leak}}_{\text{static}}
$$

- **Dynamic power** comes from charging and discharging capacitances at each switching event ($\alpha$ is the fraction of the capacitance switching each cycle). It scales with $V^2$, so lowering the supply from 3.3 V to 1.8 V cuts it by 70%.
- **Static power** is leakage, flowing even when nothing switches (Semiconductor Devices, Lesson 3.3). It dominates in deep sleep.

## Sleep modes

Microcontrollers offer a ladder of modes, each turning off more:

| Mode | What's off | Typical current | Wake-up time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Run | nothing | 50–200 µA/MHz | — |
| Sleep | CPU clock | 30–50% of run | µs |
| Stop / deep sleep | most clocks; RAM kept | 1–10 µA | 5–50 µs |
| Standby / shutdown | almost everything; RAM lost | 0.1–1 µA | ms (reboot) |

A low-power **real-time clock** or wake-up timer, running from a 32.768 kHz crystal, keeps time and wakes the system on schedule.

## The energy budget

Battery life is set by the **average** current. For a device that wakes briefly and sleeps otherwise:

$$
I_{avg} = \sum_i I_iD_i
$$

where $D_i$ is the fraction of time spent in state $i$.

> **Example.** A sensor node wakes once per second, runs at 10 mA for 10 ms (measure, compute, transmit), and sleeps at 2 µA otherwise:
> $$
> I_{avg} = 10\ \text{mA}(0.01) + 2\ \mu\text{A}(0.99) = 100 + 2 = 102\ \mu\text{A}
> $$
> A CR2032 coin cell (about 220 mAh) lasts $220/0.102 = 2160$ hours, about **90 days**. The active burst dominates: halving it nearly doubles the life, while halving the sleep current barely matters. Waking once every 10 seconds instead cuts the average to 12 µA, about 2 years.

## Design techniques

- **Race to sleep.** Run fast, finish, and go back to sleep. A higher clock often uses **less** energy per task, because fixed overheads (leakage, regulators, peripherals) run for a shorter time.
- **Duty-cycle the radio.** Radios draw the most current (5–20 mA); batch data and transmit rarely. Protocols like Bluetooth Low Energy are built around this.
- **Use peripherals and DMA autonomously.** Many MCUs let a timer trigger the ADC, and DMA store the results, while the CPU stays asleep.
- **Lower the voltage.** Use the lowest supply the parts allow; a switching regulator (Power Electronics) wastes far less than a linear one when the battery voltage is well above the load's.
- **Hunt down leakage.** Disable unused peripherals and clocks, avoid floating inputs (they cause shoot-through current), check pull-ups (a pressed button with a 10 kΩ pull-up draws 330 µA, a hundred times the sleep budget), and power-gate sensors that are idle.
- **Measure.** A current probe or power analyzer showing the real profile always reveals surprises.

## Key takeaways

- $P = \alpha CV^2f + VI_{leak}$: dynamic power scales with $V^2$; leakage dominates in sleep.
- Battery life follows the average current $\sum I_iD_i$; short active bursts usually dominate.
- Sleep as deeply and as long as possible; race to sleep; duty-cycle radios; use autonomous peripherals and DMA.
- Measure real current profiles to find leakage and surprises.
