## Crossing between analog and digital

Sensors produce voltages; actuators need them. Every microcontroller has an **ADC** (analog-to-digital converter) and many have a **DAC**. Using them well takes a little circuit knowledge and a little signal processing.

## Resolution and scaling

An $N$-bit converter with reference voltage $V_{ref}$ divides the range into $2^N$ steps:

$$
\text{LSB} = \frac{V_{ref}}{2^N}, \qquad \text{code} = \left\lfloor\frac{V_{in}}{V_{ref}}\,2^N\right\rfloor, \qquad V \approx \text{code}\times\text{LSB}
$$

> **Example.** A 12-bit ADC with $V_{ref} = 3.3$ V has $\text{LSB} = 0.806$ mV. A reading of 2048 means about 1.65 V; a temperature sensor with 10 mV/°C resolves about 0.08 °C per code.

The reference **is** the measurement standard: if $V_{ref}$ is the noisy 3.3 V supply, every reading inherits that noise and error. Precision designs use a dedicated reference IC, or measure ratiometrically (e.g. a potentiometer or bridge powered from the same reference, so supply variations cancel).

## How MCU ADCs work: successive approximation

Most microcontroller ADCs are **SAR** (successive-approximation register) converters. A sample-and-hold capacitor captures the input; then the converter does a binary search: it tries the MSB using an internal DAC and a comparator, keeps or clears it, then tries the next bit, and so on. $N$ bits take $N$ comparison cycles. Typical speeds: 12 bits at 1–5 MS/s.

**Sampling time.** During acquisition, the internal sampling capacitor (a few pF) must charge through the source's resistance. To settle within half an LSB of $N$ bits takes about $\ln(2^{N+1})$ time constants: $9\tau$ for 12 bits. A high-impedance source (a 100 kΩ divider, say) needs a long sampling time or a buffer op-amp; ignoring this gives readings that depend on the **previous** channel measured.

## Sampling theory still applies

Everything from DSP holds: sample at more than twice the highest frequency present, and put an analog **anti-aliasing** RC filter in front of the ADC, or high-frequency noise folds down into your band. Averaging $n$ readings cuts random noise by $\sqrt n$ (Probability, Lesson 2.3), a cheap way to gain resolution if the signal changes slowly.

**Real converters fall short of their bit count.** Noise and distortion are summarized by the **effective number of bits**:

$$
\text{ENOB} = \frac{\text{SINAD} - 1.76}{6.02}
$$

A "12-bit" ADC with 68 dB SINAD has an ENOB of 11.0 bits.

## DACs

A **DAC** turns a code back into a voltage, $V_{out} = \text{code} \times V_{ref}/2^N$. Common types:

- **Resistor string** or **R-2R ladder**: the standard on-chip DAC;
- **PWM plus an RC filter** (Lesson 2.3): cheap, with no dedicated hardware, but slow and with residual ripple;
- **Sigma-delta**: high resolution for audio.

A DAC's output is a staircase; a **reconstruction filter** smooths it, the mirror image of the anti-aliasing filter at the input.

## Key takeaways

- LSB $= V_{ref}/2^N$; the reference's accuracy and noise set the measurement's.
- SAR ADCs binary-search one bit per cycle; allow enough sampling time ($\sim9\tau$ for 12 bits) or buffer the source.
- Anti-alias before the ADC, reconstruct after the DAC; ENOB $= (\text{SINAD} - 1.76)/6.02$.
- DACs: R-2R or string on chip, PWM + RC as a cheap option, sigma-delta for audio.
