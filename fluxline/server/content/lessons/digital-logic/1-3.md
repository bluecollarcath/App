## Every function has a standard form

Any truth table can be written directly as an expression, without any cleverness.

A **minterm** is an AND of all $n$ variables, each true or complemented, that is 1 for exactly one row. For three variables, row 5 ($ABC = 101$) has minterm $m_5 = A\overline{B}C$.

The **sum of products (SOP)** form ORs together the minterms of every row where $F = 1$:

$$
F(A,B,C) = \sum m(1, 3, 5, 6, 7) = \overline{A}\,\overline{B}C + \overline{A}BC + A\overline{B}C + AB\overline{C} + ABC
$$

The dual is the **product of sums (POS)**: AND together the **maxterms** (OR terms) of every row where $F = 0$, written $\prod M(0, 2, 4)$.

Canonical forms are correct but large: five 3-input ANDs and a 5-input OR for a function that turns out to need far less.

## Karnaugh maps

A **K-map** rearranges the truth table into a grid where **neighboring cells differ in exactly one variable**. That ordering, 00, 01, 11, 10, is a **Gray code**. Because $XY + X\overline{Y} = X$, any two adjacent 1s merge into a simpler term, any four into a simpler one still.

Here is $F = \sum m(1, 3, 5, 6, 7)$:

| $A \backslash BC$ | 00 | 01 | 11 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | 0 | **1** | **1** | 0 |
| **1** | 0 | **1** | **1** | **1** |

**Rules for grouping:**
1. Circle groups of 1s whose sizes are powers of two: 1, 2, 4, 8, …
2. Groups must be rectangles, and **wrap around** the edges: the left and right columns are neighbors, as are the top and bottom rows.
3. Make each group as large as possible, and use as few groups as possible. Overlapping is allowed.
4. Each group becomes one product term containing only the variables that stay **constant** across it.

Here: the four 1s in columns 01 and 11 share $C = 1$, giving the term $C$. The pair in row 1, columns 11 and 10, shares $A = 1, B = 1$, giving $AB$.

$$
F = C + AB
$$

Five 3-literal terms became two terms with three literals in total.

## Four variables

> **Example.** $F(A, B, C, D) = \sum m(0, 2, 5, 7, 8, 10, 13, 15)$
>
> | $AB \backslash CD$ | 00 | 01 | 11 | 10 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | **00** | **1** | 0 | 0 | **1** |
> | **01** | 0 | **1** | **1** | 0 |
> | **11** | 0 | **1** | **1** | 0 |
> | **10** | **1** | 0 | 0 | **1** |
>
> The four **corners** form one group (they're all neighbors once you wrap both ways): $B = 0$, $D = 0$ throughout, so $\overline{B}\,\overline{D}$.
>
> The center square: $B = 1$, $D = 1$, so $BD$.
>
> $$
> F = \overline{B}\,\overline{D} + BD = \overline{B \oplus D}
> $$
>
> Eight minterms collapse to a single XNOR gate.

## Don't-care conditions

Sometimes certain input combinations can never happen. For example, a BCD digit is never 1010 to 1111. Mark those cells **X** ("don't care") and treat each one as a 1 *or* a 0, whichever makes bigger groups. Don't-cares often shrink a design dramatically.

```quiz
- Because 11 is larger than 10

```

## Key takeaways

- SOP from minterms (rows where $F = 1$); POS from maxterms (rows where $F = 0$).
- K-maps use Gray-code order; group 1s in powers of two, with wraparound.
- Each group keeps only the variables that don't change. Don't-cares help groups grow.
