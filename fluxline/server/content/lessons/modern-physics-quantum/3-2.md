## From orbits to orbitals

The Bohr model of Lesson 1.3 got hydrogen's energies right with a picture of tiny planetary orbits. Real electrons don't orbit. Solving the Schrödinger equation for an electron attracted to a proton gives three-dimensional standing waves called **orbitals**, and it explains far more than Bohr could: the shapes of atoms, how they bond, and the structure of the whole periodic table.

You won't solve the 3D equation here (that takes a full upper-level course), but you'll learn to read and use its results, which is what engineers and chemists actually do.

## Three quantum numbers

In the box, one number $n$ labeled each state because the box was one-dimensional. An atom is three-dimensional, so each state needs **three** labels. They come out of the boundary conditions, just as $n$ did in the box: the wave must fit smoothly around the atom in every direction.

| Symbol | Name | Allowed values | What it controls |
|---|---|---|---|
| $n$ | principal | $1, 2, 3, \ldots$ | energy and overall size |
| $l$ | orbital (angular momentum) | $0, 1, \ldots, n-1$ | shape of the orbital |
| $m_l$ | magnetic | $-l, \ldots, 0, \ldots, +l$ | orientation in space |

For historical reasons (from the look of spectral lines), $l$ values are named by letters:

| $l$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| letter | s | p | d | f |
| shape | sphere | dumbbell | cloverleaf | more complex |

So a "2p" orbital has $n = 2$ and $l = 1$, and a "3d" orbital has $n = 3$ and $l = 2$.

> **Example 1.** List every allowed $(l, m_l)$ combination for $n = 2$.
>
> $l$ can be 0 or 1.
> - $l = 0$ (2s): $m_l = 0$. One state.
> - $l = 1$ (2p): $m_l = -1, 0, +1$. Three states.
>
> Four states in total.

**Counting states.** For each $l$ there are $2l + 1$ values of $m_l$. Adding them up for $l = 0$ to $n - 1$ gives exactly $n^2$ orbitals in level $n$: 1, 4, 9, 16, and so on. (Next lesson doubles this with electron spin.)

## Energies: Bohr was right, for hydrogen

The full solution gives exactly the same energies as Bohr's model:

$$
E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}
$$

In hydrogen, the energy depends **only on $n$**. The 2s and all three 2p orbitals have the same energy, $-3.40$ eV. States that share an energy are called **degenerate**. (In atoms with more than one electron, the electrons repel each other and this degeneracy breaks: 2s ends up slightly lower than 2p. That ordering is what builds the periodic table.)

## Where is the electron?

The orbital gives $|\psi|^2$, the probability density in 3D. A useful way to read it is the **radial probability**: the chance of finding the electron at a distance $r$ from the nucleus, regardless of direction.

```plot
x: 0, 12, distance from nucleus (units of a₀ = 0.0529 nm)
y: 0, 0.6, radial probability
f: 4*x^2*exp(-2*x) | 1s
f: 0.125*x^2*(2-x)^2*exp(-x) | 2s | dash
vline: 1, a₀
caption: Radial probability for hydrogen's 1s and 2s orbitals. The 1s electron is most likely found at exactly the Bohr radius, $a_0 = 0.0529$ nm. The 2s orbital is bigger and has a node: a shell where the electron is never found.
```

- The 1s electron is most likely found at $r = a_0 = 0.0529$ nm, the **Bohr radius**. Bohr's orbit survives as the *most probable* distance, but the electron is a cloud, not a dot.
- Higher-$n$ orbitals are bigger; the size grows roughly as $n^2$.
- The 2s orbital has a **node**, a spherical shell where $|\psi|^2 = 0$, just like the node in the second state of the box.

## Orbital angular momentum

The quantum number $l$ also sets how much angular momentum the electron has as it "circulates":

$$
L = \sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\hbar
$$

An s electron ($l = 0$) has **zero** angular momentum, something impossible in Bohr's orbit picture. And $m_l$ gives the component along a chosen axis (say, the direction of a magnetic field): $L_z = m_l \hbar$.

Because an electron with angular momentum is a circulating charge, it acts like a tiny current loop, a little magnet. In a magnetic field, the energy depends slightly on $m_l$, so spectral lines split into several closely spaced lines. This is the **Zeeman effect**, and it's how astronomers measure magnetic fields on the Sun.

## Why engineers care

- **Chemical bonds and crystals.** Silicon bonds to four neighbors because its outer electrons occupy one s and three p orbitals that mix into four directional bonds. That tetrahedral bonding sets the diamond crystal structure of silicon, which in turn shapes its energy bands.
- **Material properties** like color, magnetism and conductivity come from which orbitals the outer electrons occupy.
- **Spectroscopy** identifies materials (and contamination in chip fabs) by their spectral lines.

### Common mistakes

- **Letting $l$ reach $n$.** $l$ goes from $0$ to $n - 1$. There's no "2d" orbital.
- **Forgetting negative $m_l$.** For $l = 2$, $m_l = -2, -1, 0, 1, 2$: five values.
- **Thinking 2s and 2p have different energies in hydrogen.** They don't; only $n$ matters for hydrogen's energy.
- **Picturing a planet-like orbit.** The electron is a probability cloud; $a_0$ is the most likely distance, not a track.

## Key takeaways

- Atomic states are labeled by $n$ (energy, size), $l = 0 \ldots n-1$ (shape: s, p, d, f) and $m_l = -l \ldots +l$ (orientation).
- Level $n$ has $n^2$ orbitals; for hydrogen all have energy $-13.6/n^2$ eV.
- Orbitals are 3D probability clouds; the 1s electron is most likely at $a_0 = 0.0529$ nm.
- Orbital angular momentum is $L = \sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\hbar$, with component $m_l\hbar$ along an axis; s electrons have none.
- Orbitals determine bonding, crystal structure (like silicon's four bonds) and material properties.
