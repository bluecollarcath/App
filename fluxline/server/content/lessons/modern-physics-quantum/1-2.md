## If light is a particle, is matter a wave?

Last lesson, light, which everyone agreed was a wave, turned out to arrive in particle-like packets. In 1924 a French graduate student, Louis de Broglie, asked the reverse question in his PhD thesis: if waves can act like particles, can particles act like waves?

His committee wasn't sure whether it was genius or nonsense. Three years later, experiments showed electrons diffracting exactly like waves, and de Broglie got the Nobel Prize. This idea is the foundation of everything else in the course: **electrons in atoms and crystals behave as waves**, and that is why energy levels and energy bands exist.

## Momentum of a photon

A photon has no mass, but it does carry momentum. (That's how solar sails work: light pushes on them.) From relativity, a photon's momentum is its energy divided by $c$:

$$
p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{hf}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda}
$$

Flip that around and you get the wavelength from the momentum: $\lambda = h/p$.

## The de Broglie wavelength

De Broglie proposed that the same relation holds for **every** particle. A particle with momentum $p = mv$ has a wavelength

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}
$$

Because $h$ is so tiny ($6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s), the wavelength is only noticeable when the momentum is tiny too: very small, light particles.

> **Example 1 (a baseball).** A 0.145 kg baseball thrown at 40 m/s:
>
> $\lambda = \dfrac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{0.145 \times 40} = 1.1 \times 10^{-34}$ m.
>
> That's about $10^{-19}$ times smaller than a proton. No experiment could ever detect it, which is why baseballs never diffract around a bat.

> **Example 2 (an electron).** An electron ($m = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg) moving at $1.0 \times 10^6$ m/s:
>
> $\lambda = \dfrac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{(9.109 \times 10^{-31})(1.0 \times 10^6)} = 7.3 \times 10^{-10}$ m $= 0.73$ nm.
>
> That's a few atoms wide. Inside a crystal, where atoms are spaced about 0.2 to 0.5 nm apart, electron waves absolutely matter.

## Electrons accelerated through a voltage

In electronics, electrons usually get their speed from a voltage. An electron accelerated from rest through $V$ volts gains kinetic energy $eV$ (that's $V$ electron-volts). Since $KE = \dfrac{p^2}{2m}$, its momentum is $p = \sqrt{2meV}$, and

$$
\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2 m e V}} \approx \frac{1.226\ \text{nm}}{\sqrt{V}}
$$

with $V$ in volts. (This shortcut is for electrons only, at speeds well below the speed of light.)

| Accelerating voltage | Electron wavelength |
|---|---|
| 1 V | 1.23 nm |
| 54 V | 0.167 nm |
| 100 V | 0.123 nm |
| 10 kV | 0.0123 nm |
| 50 kV | 0.0055 nm |

> **Example 3.** What wavelength do electrons have after crossing 150 V?
>
> $\lambda = \dfrac{1.226}{\sqrt{150}} = \dfrac{1.226}{12.25} \approx 0.100$ nm, about the spacing between atoms in a crystal.

## The proof: electron diffraction

In 1927, Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer at Bell Labs fired 54 V electrons at a nickel crystal. The electrons bounced off strongly at certain angles and hardly at all at others, exactly the pattern X-rays make when they diffract from a crystal. The angle matched a wavelength of 0.167 nm, just as de Broglie's formula predicts.

That was the start of a whole technology. **Electron microscopes** use electrons accelerated through tens of kilovolts, with wavelengths thousands of times shorter than visible light. Since a microscope can't resolve details much smaller than its wavelength, electron microscopes can image individual atoms, while light microscopes stop at about 200 nm. Chip fabs use electron microscopes every day to inspect transistors only a few nanometers wide.

## The double-slit experiment

The cleanest demonstration of wave behavior is the double slit. Send waves at a barrier with two narrow slits, and the waves from the two slits overlap on a screen behind. Where crests meet crests, they add (**constructive interference**, a bright fringe); where crests meet troughs, they cancel (**destructive interference**, a dark fringe).

For small angles, the bright fringes are evenly spaced by

$$
\Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d}
$$

where $L$ is the distance to the screen and $d$ is the distance between the slits.

> **Example 4 (light).** A red laser (633 nm) shines on two slits 0.25 mm apart, with a screen 1.5 m away:
>
> $\Delta y = \dfrac{(633 \times 10^{-9})(1.5)}{0.25 \times 10^{-3}} = 3.8 \times 10^{-3}$ m $= 3.8$ mm between bright fringes.

Now do it with electrons, **one at a time**. Each electron lands at a single spot on the detector, like a particle. But after thousands of electrons, the spots build up the same striped interference pattern, as if each electron went through **both** slits and interfered with itself.

```plot
x: -3, 3, position on screen (fringe spacings)
y: 0, 1.15, how often electrons land
f: cos(pi*x)^2 * exp(-x^2/6) | both slits open: interference fringes
f: 0.55*(exp(-(x-1.2)^2/0.7) + exp(-(x+1.2)^2/0.7)) | what particles would do: two smooth humps | dash
caption: Electrons sent one at a time through two slits. If they were tiny bullets, the hits would form two broad humps (dashed). Instead they build up bright and dark fringes (solid), the signature of a wave interfering with itself.
```

It gets stranger. Put a detector at the slits to see which one each electron goes through, and the fringes **disappear**: you get the two humps. Measuring which path the electron took destroys the interference. This is a real, repeatable result, and it tells us something deep: until it's measured, the electron isn't at one place; it's described by a wave spread over both paths. The next unit makes that precise with the **wavefunction**.

## What this means for engineering

- **Electrons in crystals are waves.** Their wavelength is comparable to the atomic spacing, so they interfere with the regular rows of atoms. That interference is what creates energy bands and bandgaps (Unit 4).
- **Confinement makes energy levels.** A wave trapped in a small space can only have certain wavelengths, like a guitar string can only play certain notes. That's why electrons in atoms have discrete energies (next lesson).
- **Electron microscopes** and electron-beam lithography rely on short electron wavelengths.

### Common mistakes

- **Using $\lambda = 1240/E$ for electrons.** That formula is for photons only. For electrons use $\lambda = h/p$, or $1.226/\sqrt{V}$ nm.
- **Forgetting to square-root the voltage.** Doubling the voltage doesn't halve the wavelength; you need four times the voltage to halve it.
- **Thinking big objects "aren't waves".** They are, but their wavelengths are far too small to ever observe.

## Key takeaways

- Every particle has a de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p = h/(mv)$; photons obey the same relation.
- Electrons accelerated through $V$ volts: $\lambda \approx 1.226/\sqrt{V}$ nm.
- Big objects have unmeasurably small wavelengths; electrons have wavelengths about the size of atoms.
- Electron diffraction and the one-at-a-time double slit prove that electrons behave as waves; measuring the path destroys the interference.
- Electron waves in crystals and atoms are the origin of energy levels and energy bands.
