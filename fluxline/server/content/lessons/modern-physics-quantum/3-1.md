## Walking through walls

Roll a ball at a hill without enough energy to get over it, and it rolls back. Always. Classically, a barrier higher than your energy is a perfect wall.

Quantum mechanically, it isn't. In Lesson 2.4 you saw that a wavefunction doesn't stop at a barrier; it decays exponentially inside it. If the barrier is thin enough, the wave hasn't died out completely when it reaches the far side, and it continues on as a smaller wave. The particle has a small but real chance of appearing on the other side, without ever having had enough energy to climb over. This is **quantum tunneling**.

Tunneling isn't a curiosity. It's how flash memory stores your photos, why transistors leak power, how scanning tunneling microscopes image single atoms, and a reason the Sun shines.


Three regions:
1. **Before the barrier:** the incoming wave, plus most of it reflecting back.
2. **Inside the barrier:** an evanescent wave decaying as $e^{-\kappa x}$, with $\kappa = \dfrac{\sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}}{\hbar}$.
3. **After the barrier:** a transmitted wave with the same wavelength as before (same energy), but a much smaller amplitude.

## The tunneling probability

The amplitude shrinks by about $e^{-\kappa L}$ across a barrier of thickness $L$. Probability goes as amplitude squared, so the **transmission probability** is approximately

$$
T \approx e^{-2\kappa L}
$$

This approximation holds when the barrier is "thick," meaning $\kappa L$ is bigger than about 1, which covers almost every engineering case. (The exact formula has a prefactor of order 1 in front, which we'll ignore.)

For an electron, use $\kappa = \sqrt{\dfrac{V_0 - E}{0.0381}}$ nm⁻¹ with energies in eV.

> **Example 1.** An electron meets a barrier 1.0 eV higher than its energy and 1.0 nm thick.
>
> $\kappa = \sqrt{\dfrac{1.0}{0.0381}} = 5.12$ nm⁻¹, so $2\kappa L = 10.25$, and $T \approx e^{-10.25} = 3.5 \times 10^{-5}$.
>
> About one electron in 28,000 gets through. That sounds tiny, but a current of 1 µA is $6 \times 10^{12}$ electrons per second, so tunneling currents are easily measurable.

## The exponential is everything

Because thickness sits in the exponent, small changes in the barrier make enormous changes in the current.

| Barrier thickness (1 eV barrier) | Tunneling probability |
|---|---|
| 0.5 nm | $6.0 \times 10^{-3}$ |
| 1.0 nm | $3.5 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| 2.0 nm | $1.3 \times 10^{-9}$ |

**Doubling the thickness doesn't halve the probability; it squares it.** Going from 1 nm to 2 nm cut tunneling by a factor of about 28,000. Raising the barrier height also helps, but more gently, because $V_0 - E$ sits under a square root.

## Tunneling in technology

**Scanning tunneling microscope (STM).** A metal needle sharpened to a single atom at its tip is held about half a nanometer above a surface, with a small voltage applied. Electrons tunnel across the gap. With a typical barrier of about 4.5 eV (the work function), $\kappa \approx 10.9$ nm⁻¹, so moving the tip just 0.1 nm closer multiplies the current by $e^{2 \times 10.9 \times 0.1} \approx 9$. That extreme sensitivity lets an STM map surfaces atom by atom. Its inventors won the 1986 Nobel Prize.

**Flash memory.** Every SSD, USB stick and phone stores data as electric charge on a tiny conductor called a **floating gate** (or, in newer chips, a charge-trap layer), completely surrounded by insulating oxide. To write a bit, the chip applies a high voltage (around 15 to 20 V) that tilts the energy barrier, making it effectively thin enough for electrons to tunnel *onto* the floating gate. Remove the voltage, and the barrier is back to full thickness: the electrons are trapped for 10 years or more. Every write slightly damages the oxide, which is why flash cells wear out after a few thousand to a hundred thousand writes.

**Transistor gate leakage.** A MOSFET's gate is insulated from its channel by a thin oxide layer. Around 2005, silicon dioxide gates had shrunk to about 1.2 nm, only five atoms thick, and electrons began tunneling straight through, wasting power even when the transistor was off. The industry's fix was to switch to **high-κ dielectrics** like hafnium oxide, which control the channel just as well while being physically thicker. Thicker barrier, exponentially less tunneling.

**Tunnel diodes and resonant-tunneling diodes** use tunneling through ultra-thin junctions for very fast switching and oscillators at hundreds of gigahertz.

**Nature uses it too.** Alpha particles escape uranium nuclei by tunneling, which sets radioactive half-lives. And protons in the Sun's core don't have enough energy to fuse classically; they tunnel through their mutual electric repulsion. Without tunneling, the Sun wouldn't shine.

> **Example 2 (gate oxide).** Electrons see about a 3.1 eV barrier at a silicon-to-SiO₂ interface. Compare the tunneling probability through 1.2 nm and 2.0 nm of oxide.
>
> $\kappa = \sqrt{\dfrac{3.1}{0.0381}} = 9.02$ nm⁻¹.
>
> 1.2 nm: $T \approx e^{-2(9.02)(1.2)} = e^{-21.6} \approx 4 \times 10^{-10}$.
>
> 2.0 nm: $T \approx e^{-36.1} \approx 2 \times 10^{-16}$.
>
> The thinner oxide leaks about two million times more. (Real numbers also depend on the electron's effective mass in the oxide, but the exponential trend is the point.)

### Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the factor of 2.** Amplitude decays as $e^{-\kappa L}$; probability as $e^{-2\kappa L}$.
- **Using the electron's full energy instead of $V_0 - E$.** $\kappa$ depends on how far *below the top* of the barrier the electron is.
- **Thinking tunneling loses energy.** The transmitted electron has the same energy (same wavelength) it started with; only the probability is reduced.
- **Scaling linearly.** Twice as thick doesn't mean half the current; it means the probability gets squared.

## Key takeaways

- A wave meeting a barrier higher than its energy decays inside but can emerge on the far side: tunneling.
- Transmission probability: $T \approx e^{-2\kappa L}$, with $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar$; for electrons $\kappa = \sqrt{(V_0 - E)/0.0381}$ nm⁻¹.
- Thickness is in the exponent: doubling $L$ squares $T$. Tiny changes in thickness change currents enormously.
- Tunneling powers flash memory and STMs, causes gate leakage in tiny transistors, and is why high-κ dielectrics exist.
- A tunneling particle keeps its energy; only its probability of arrival is reduced.
