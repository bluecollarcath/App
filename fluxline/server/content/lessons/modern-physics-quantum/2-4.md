## Real walls aren't infinite

The particle in a box had walls that could never be crossed. Real barriers have a finite height: an electron in a thin layer of GaAs sandwiched between AlGaAs, for example, is held in by an energy step of only a few tenths of an eV. That changes the physics in two important ways, and both matter for devices: the wavefunction **leaks into the walls**, and there are only **a limited number of trapped states**.

This lesson also solves the second most important problem in quantum mechanics, the harmonic oscillator, which describes vibrating molecules, vibrating crystal atoms, and the modes of light itself.

## The finite square well

Picture a well of width $L$ and depth $V_0$: the potential is $0$ inside and $V_0$ outside. Take an electron with energy $E$ less than $V_0$. Classically it's trapped, bouncing back and forth, never entering the walls.

**Inside the well**, the solution is still sines and cosines, as before.

**Inside the walls**, where $V_0 > E$, the Schrödinger equation becomes

$$
\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = \kappa^2 \psi \qquad \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}}{\hbar}
$$

Notice the sign: $+\kappa^2$, not $-k^2$. The solutions aren't waves; they're exponentials. The one that makes physical sense (doesn't blow up far away) **decays**:

$$
\psi(x) \propto e^{-\kappa x}
$$

This is called an **evanescent** wave. The electron has a real, nonzero probability of being found inside the wall, a region where classically it doesn't have enough energy to be. The wavefunction falls by a factor of $e$ over the **penetration depth**

$$
\delta = \frac{1}{\kappa}
$$

For an electron, $\kappa = \sqrt{\dfrac{V_0 - E}{0.0381\ \text{eV·nm}^2}}$, so for a barrier 1 eV above the electron's energy, $\kappa = 5.1$ nm⁻¹ and $\delta \approx 0.2$ nm.

**Consequences of leaking:**
- The wave has a little more room than $L$, so its wavelength is a bit longer and its energies are a bit **lower** than in an infinite well of the same width.
- Only a **finite number** of states fit below the top of the well. States with $E > V_0$ aren't trapped at all.
- A shallow, narrow well might hold just **one** bound state, but (in one dimension) it always holds at least one.
- If a barrier is thin enough, the decaying tail reaches the other side, and the particle can escape. That's **quantum tunneling**, the subject of Lesson 3.1.

> **Example 1.** An electron in a well sits 0.1 eV below the top of the barrier. How far does its wavefunction reach into the wall?
>
> $\kappa = \sqrt{\dfrac{0.1}{0.0381}} = 1.62$ nm⁻¹, so $\delta = \dfrac{1}{1.62} = 0.62$ nm. Electrons near the top of a shallow well spread well into the barrier.

**Where finite wells are engineered:** quantum-well lasers (in every fiber-optic transmitter and Blu-ray player), high-electron-mobility transistors (HEMTs, used in 5G and satellite receivers), and infrared detectors. Engineers choose the layer thicknesses and materials to place the energy levels exactly where they want them.

## The quantum harmonic oscillator

A mass on a spring feels a restoring force $F = -kx$ and a potential energy $V = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$: a parabola. Classically, it oscillates at angular frequency $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ with any energy you like.

Atoms in a molecule or a crystal behave like tiny masses on springs, held near their resting positions by chemical bonds. Solving the Schrödinger equation with $V = \tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2$ gives allowed energies

$$
E_n = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
$$

Compare with the box:
- The levels are **evenly spaced**, all $\hbar\omega$ apart (not growing as $n^2$).
- The ground state ($n = 0$) has energy $\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$: the **zero-point energy** again. A quantum oscillator can never be completely still, even at absolute zero.

```plot

y: 0, 5, energy (units of ħω)

hline: 0.5, n = 0: ½ ħω
hline: 1.5, n = 1: 3/2 ħω
hline: 2.5, n = 2: 5/2 ħω
hline: 3.5, n = 3: 7/2 ħω
caption: Energy levels of the quantum harmonic oscillator: evenly spaced by $\hbar\omega$, starting at $\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$ above the bottom of the parabola.
```

Since $\hbar\omega = hf$, the spacing is just Planck's constant times the classical oscillation frequency: a jump of one level absorbs or emits one photon of exactly that frequency.

> **Example 2 (molecular vibration).** The bond in a hydrogen chloride (HCl) molecule vibrates at $f = 8.66 \times 10^{13}$ Hz. Find the level spacing and the wavelength of light that excites it.
>
> $\hbar\omega = hf = (4.136 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{eV·s})(8.66 \times 10^{13}) = 0.358$ eV.
>
> $\lambda = \dfrac{1240}{0.358} = 3460$ nm $= 3.46$ µm, in the infrared.

This is how **infrared gas sensors** work: each molecule absorbs at its own vibration frequencies. A CO₂ sensor in a smart thermostat measures how much 4.3 µm infrared light gets absorbed, which tells it the CO₂ concentration. It's also why CO₂ traps heat in the atmosphere: its vibrations absorb the infrared that Earth radiates.

> **Example 3 (why you never notice it).** A 0.1 kg mass on a spring with $k = 100$ N/m has $\omega = \sqrt{100/0.1} = 31.6$ rad/s. The level spacing is $\hbar\omega = 3.3 \times 10^{-33}$ J $= 2 \times 10^{-14}$ eV, trillions of times too small to notice. Quantization only shows up for atomic-scale masses and springs.

**The same math describes light.** Each mode of the electromagnetic field (each frequency in a cavity or a waveguide) is mathematically a harmonic oscillator. Its energy steps of $\hbar\omega = hf$ are photons. That's the deep connection between Lesson 1.1 and this one.

### Common mistakes

- **Using $n^2$ for the oscillator.** Box: $E \propto n^2$. Oscillator: $E = (n + \tfrac{1}{2})\hbar\omega$, evenly spaced.
- **Starting the oscillator at $n = 1$.** It starts at $n = 0$, with energy $\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$.
- **Thinking the wavefunction stops at the wall.** In a finite well it decays exponentially inside the barrier.

## Key takeaways

- In a finite well, the wavefunction decays into the walls as $e^{-\kappa x}$, with $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar$; for electrons $\kappa = \sqrt{(V_0 - E)/0.0381}$ nm⁻¹.
- Finite wells have slightly lower energies than infinite ones and only a limited number of bound states.
- Harmonic oscillator: $E_n = (n + \tfrac{1}{2})\hbar\omega$, evenly spaced by $\hbar\omega = hf$, with zero-point energy $\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$.
- Molecules absorb infrared at their vibration frequencies, the basis of IR gas sensors.
- Engineered quantum wells power laser diodes, HEMT transistors and infrared detectors.
