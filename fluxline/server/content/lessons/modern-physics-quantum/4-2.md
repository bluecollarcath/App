## Who fills the bands?

Last lesson showed that a semiconductor has a full valence band, an empty conduction band, and a gap of about 1 eV between them. Now the practical questions: at a given temperature, how many electrons make it into the conduction band? What carries the current? And how does adding one phosphorus atom per five million silicon atoms change the conductivity by a factor of a million? This lesson is the bridge into Semiconductor Devices.

## The Fermi-Dirac distribution

Electrons are fermions: each state holds at most one (Pauli). At temperature $T$, the probability that a state at energy $E$ is occupied is the **Fermi-Dirac distribution**:

$$
f(E) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(E - E_F)/kT}}
$$

$E_F$ is the **Fermi level**: the energy where the occupation probability is exactly $\tfrac{1}{2}$. And $k = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/K is Boltzmann's constant, so $kT \approx 0.0259$ eV at room temperature (300 K).

```plot

y: 0, 1.1, occupation probability f(E)
f: 1 - u(x) | T = 0 K: sharp step | dash
f: 1/(1 + exp(x/0.02585)) | T = 300 K
f: 1/(1 + exp(x/0.0517)) | T = 600 K | dot
mark: 0, 0.5, f = ½ at the Fermi level
caption: The Fermi-Dirac distribution. At absolute zero, every state below $E_F$ is full and every state above is empty. At higher temperatures the edge softens over a few $kT$: some states just below are emptied and some just above are filled.
```

What to notice:
- **At 0 K** it's a perfect step: full below $E_F$, empty above.
- **At room temperature** the edge blurs over only about $\pm 3kT \approx \pm 0.08$ eV. Far above $E_F$, the occupation falls off as $e^{-(E - E_F)/kT}$: every extra 0.06 eV cuts it by about 10×.
- **Raising the temperature** widens the blur, pushing more electrons to higher energies.

In a pure (intrinsic) semiconductor, $E_F$ sits near the middle of the gap. The conduction band is about $E_g/2 \approx 0.56$ eV above it in silicon, so its states are occupied with probability around $e^{-0.56/0.0259} \approx 4 \times 10^{-10}$. That's where the $e^{-E_g/2kT}$ from last lesson comes from.

## Holes: the missing electrons

When an electron jumps into the conduction band, it leaves an empty state behind in the valence band. That empty state is called a **hole**, and it turns out to be extremely useful to treat it as a particle in its own right:

- A neighboring valence electron can hop into the hole, which moves the hole the other way. Many electrons each shifting one step behaves exactly like **one positive charge** moving in the opposite direction.
- So a hole acts like a particle with charge $+e$, drifting **with** the electric field (toward the negative terminal).

Picture a row of cars bumper to bumper with one empty space. As each car moves forward into the gap, the gap moves backward. Tracking the one gap is far easier than tracking every car.

In a semiconductor, current is carried by both conduction-band **electrons** and valence-band **holes**. This two-carrier picture is the basis of every diode and transistor.

## Effective mass

An electron in a crystal isn't moving through empty space; it's a wave interacting with the periodic atoms. The net result is that it responds to forces as if it had a different mass, its **effective mass** $m^*$. In silicon, conduction electrons behave as if $m^* \approx 0.26\,m_e$; in GaAs, $m^* \approx 0.067\,m_e$. A lighter effective mass means electrons accelerate more easily and move faster for a given field, which is a big reason GaAs and related materials are used in high-frequency transistors.

## Intrinsic carriers

In pure silicon at 300 K, thermal excitation produces about

$$
n_i \approx 1 \times 10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}
$$

electrons in the conduction band, and the same number of holes. That sounds like a lot, but silicon has $5 \times 10^{22}$ atoms per cm³: only one atom in five trillion contributes a carrier. Pure silicon is a poor conductor. And since $n_i$ grows exponentially with temperature, it would be an unreliable one too.

## Doping: engineering the carriers

The solution is to add a tiny, carefully controlled amount of impurity, called **doping**. From Lesson 3.3:

**n-type: add phosphorus (or arsenic).** Phosphorus has 5 valence electrons. Four form bonds with neighboring silicon atoms; the fifth is barely held. Its binding energy is only about 0.045 eV, less than $2kT$ at room temperature, so essentially every donor gives its extra electron to the conduction band. Each phosphorus atom is a **donor**.

**Why so weakly bound? Hydrogen again.** The extra electron orbits the phosphorus ion like the electron in a hydrogen atom, but inside silicon. Two things weaken the pull: the surrounding silicon screens the charge (dielectric constant $\varepsilon_r = 11.7$), and the electron's effective mass is smaller. Scaling hydrogen's 13.6 eV by $\dfrac{m^*/m_e}{\varepsilon_r^2} = \dfrac{0.26}{11.7^2}$ gives about 0.026 eV, the right size. (The measured 0.045 eV differs because the simple model is rough near the ion, but it explains why donors ionize so easily.)

**p-type: add boron.** Boron has 3 valence electrons, so one bond is short an electron. A neighboring valence electron easily hops in (about 0.045 eV again), which creates a hole that can move freely. Each boron atom is an **acceptor**.

### The mass-action law

Doping adds one kind of carrier, and it also suppresses the other kind. At equilibrium, electrons and holes keep recombining, and the product of their concentrations stays fixed:

$$
n \cdot p = n_i^2 = 10^{20}\ \text{cm}^{-6} \quad (\text{silicon at 300 K})
$$

> **Example 1 (n-type silicon).** Silicon is doped with $N_D = 10^{16}$ phosphorus atoms per cm³ (0.2 parts per million).
>
> Electrons: $n \approx N_D = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³, a million times more than pure silicon.
>
> Holes: $p = \dfrac{n_i^2}{n} = \dfrac{10^{20}}{10^{16}} = 10^4$ cm⁻³, a million times fewer.
>
> Electrons are the **majority carriers** and holes the **minority carriers**. The conductivity is now set by the doping, not by temperature, which is what makes reliable devices possible.

### Where the Fermi level goes

Adding donors fills more conduction states, so the Fermi level moves **up**, toward the conduction band. Measured from the intrinsic level $E_i$ (near midgap):

$$
E_F - E_i = kT\ln\frac{n}{n_i}
$$

For $n = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³: $E_F - E_i = 0.0259 \ln(10^6) = 0.358$ eV above midgap. In p-type silicon the Fermi level moves **down** toward the valence band by $kT\ln(p/n_i)$.

When n-type and p-type silicon are joined, the Fermi level has to be the same everywhere in equilibrium. Lining them up bends the bands and creates a built-in voltage across the junction: the starting point of the diode, the transistor and the solar cell in Semiconductor Devices.

### Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the minority carriers.** Doping with $10^{16}$ donors doesn't leave $10^{10}$ holes; it drops them to $10^4$ via $np = n_i^2$.
- **Thinking holes are positrons.** A hole is just a missing electron in the valence band that behaves like a positive charge.
- **Mixing up donors and acceptors.** Donors (column 5: P, As) give electrons, making n-type; acceptors (column 3: B) make holes, p-type.

## Key takeaways

- Fermi-Dirac: $f(E) = 1/(1 + e^{(E - E_F)/kT})$; $f = \tfrac{1}{2}$ at the Fermi level, and the edge blurs over a few $kT$ ($kT = 0.0259$ eV at 300 K).
- Holes are missing valence electrons that act as positive carriers; current flows by both electrons and holes.
- Effective mass $m^*$ captures how electrons respond inside a crystal ($0.26\,m_e$ in Si, $0.067\,m_e$ in GaAs).
- Pure Si has $n_i \approx 10^{10}$ cm⁻³. Donors (P, As) make n-type; acceptors (B) make p-type; always $np = n_i^2$.
- Doping shifts the Fermi level by $kT\ln(n/n_i)$, up for n-type and down for p-type.
