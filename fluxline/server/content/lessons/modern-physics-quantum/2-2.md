## A limit built into nature

In classical physics, nothing stops you from knowing exactly where a particle is and exactly how fast it's moving, at the same time. Better instruments just give you better numbers. In 1927 Werner Heisenberg showed that quantum mechanics forbids this. It's not a flaw in our instruments; it's a property of waves.

## Why waves can't have a sharp position and a sharp wavelength

A wave with a single, perfectly defined wavelength goes on forever: a pure sine wave has no beginning or end, so it has no location. To make a wave that's bunched up in one place, a **wave packet**, you have to add together many sine waves with different wavelengths, so that they reinforce in one spot and cancel everywhere else.

```plot
x: -6, 6, position
y: -1.2, 1.2
f: exp(-x^2/8)*cos(4*x) | wide packet: wavelength well defined, position fuzzy
f: exp(-x^2/0.5)*cos(4*x) | narrow packet: position sharp, needs many wavelengths | dash
hline: 0
caption: Two wave packets. The wide one (solid) has many clean oscillations, so its wavelength (and momentum) is well defined, but it's spread out in space. The narrow one (dashed) is well located, but squeezing it requires mixing in a wide range of wavelengths.
```

You've already met this idea if you've studied signals: **a short pulse needs a wide bandwidth.** A 1 ns pulse contains frequencies spread over about 1 GHz; a pure tone has zero bandwidth but lasts forever. Quantum mechanics is the same math, with position in place of time and momentum ($p = h/\lambda$) in place of frequency.

## The uncertainty principle

Let $\Delta x$ be the spread (standard deviation) in a particle's position and $\Delta p$ the spread in its momentum. Then

$$
\Delta x\, \Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}
$$

where $\hbar$ ("h-bar") is Planck's constant divided by $2\pi$:

$$
\hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi} = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}
$$

Squeeze the position spread smaller, and the momentum spread must get bigger, and vice versa. Their product can never go below $\hbar/2$.

> **Example 1 (an electron in an atom).** Atoms are about 0.1 nm across. Confine an electron to $\Delta x = 0.1$ nm and its momentum spread is at least
>
> $\Delta p \ge \dfrac{\hbar}{2\Delta x} = \dfrac{1.055 \times 10^{-34}}{2 \times 10^{-10}} = 5.3 \times 10^{-25}$ kg·m/s.
>
> Divide by the electron's mass: $\Delta v \ge \dfrac{5.3 \times 10^{-25}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}} \approx 5.8 \times 10^5$ m/s. An electron inside an atom is necessarily zipping around at hundreds of kilometers per second.

> **Example 2 (a baseball).** Locate a 0.145 kg baseball to within a micrometer ($\Delta x = 10^{-6}$ m):
>
> $\Delta v \ge \dfrac{1.055 \times 10^{-34}}{2 \times 10^{-6} \times 0.145} \approx 3.6 \times 10^{-28}$ m/s.
>
> Utterly unmeasurable. The uncertainty principle matters only for very small, light things.

## Confinement costs energy

Example 1 points to one of the most important consequences in all of physics: **you can't confine a particle without giving it kinetic energy.** If the momentum is spread by at least $\Delta p$, the particle's average kinetic energy is at least roughly

$$
KE \sim \frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m} \sim \frac{\hbar^2}{2m (\Delta x)^2}
$$

The smaller the box, the larger this **confinement energy**, growing as $1/(\Delta x)^2$. For an electron, $\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m} = 0.0381$ eV·nm², a handy number to remember.

This is why:
- **Atoms don't collapse.** (See below.)
- **Quantum dots change color with size.** Smaller dots squeeze electrons harder, so their energies go up and they emit bluer light (Lesson 2.3).
- **Transistors get harder to shrink.** Electrons confined in a few-nanometer channel pick up extra energy that shifts the transistor's threshold voltage. Chip designers model this.

## Why atoms don't collapse

The electron in hydrogen is attracted to the proton, so its potential energy $-\dfrac{1.44\ \text{eV·nm}}{r}$ drops lower the closer it gets. Classically, it should fall in. But squeezing it into a radius $r$ costs confinement energy of about $\dfrac{0.0381\ \text{eV·nm}^2}{r^2}$. Add the two together:

$$
E(r) \approx \frac{0.0381}{r^2} - \frac{1.44}{r} \qquad (E \text{ in eV}, r \text{ in nm})
$$

```plot
x: 0.015, 0.2, radius r (nm)
y: -20, 25, energy (eV)
f: 0.0381/x^2 - 1.44/x | total energy
f: 0.0381/x^2 | confinement (kinetic) energy | dash
f: -1.44/x | electrical (potential) energy | dot
hline: 0
mark: 0.0529, -13.6
vline: 0.0529, r = 0.053 nm
hline: -13.6, E = −13.6 eV
caption: Squeezing the electron closer lowers its potential energy but raises its confinement energy even faster. The total has a minimum at 0.053 nm and −13.6 eV, exactly the size and energy of a real hydrogen atom.
```

The total energy has a minimum at $r = \dfrac{2 \times 0.0381}{1.44} = 0.053$ nm, where $E = -13.6$ eV. That's precisely hydrogen's measured size and ground-state energy. The uncertainty principle is what holds atoms open, and therefore what gives all matter its size.

## Energy and time

There's a second uncertainty relation, between energy and time:

$$
\Delta E\, \Delta t \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}
$$

A state that lasts only a short time $\Delta t$ can't have a perfectly sharp energy. Excited states of atoms live for a few nanoseconds before emitting a photon, so their energies, and the colors of the light they emit, are slightly blurred. This sets the minimum **linewidth** of spectral lines and lasers.

> **Example 3.** An excited atomic state lasts about 10 ns. What's its minimum energy spread?
>
> $\Delta E \ge \dfrac{\hbar}{2\Delta t} = \dfrac{1.055 \times 10^{-34}}{2 \times 10^{-8}} = 5.3 \times 10^{-27}$ J $= 3.3 \times 10^{-8}$ eV. Tiny, which is why atomic lines are sharp enough to run atomic clocks.

> **Example 4 (ultrafast lasers).** A 1 ps laser pulse has an energy spread of at least $\dfrac{\hbar}{2 \times 10^{-12}} = 5.3 \times 10^{-23}$ J $= 0.33$ meV. Ultrashort pulses can't be a single pure color, exactly like short pulses in signals need bandwidth.

## What it doesn't mean

- **It's not about clumsy measurement.** It's often explained as "measuring disturbs the particle." That's part of it, but the deeper truth is that a particle simply doesn't *have* a perfectly sharp position and momentum at the same time.
- **It doesn't limit one quantity alone.** You can measure position as precisely as you like; you just give up knowledge of momentum.
- **It's not vague.** $\hbar/2$ is an exact, testable bound.

### Common mistakes

- **Mixing up $h$ and $\hbar$.** The uncertainty principle uses $\hbar = h/2\pi = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s.
- **Forgetting it's an inequality.** $\Delta p \ge \hbar/(2\Delta x)$ gives the *minimum* spread.
- **Getting $\Delta v$ wrong.** Divide $\Delta p$ by the mass to get the velocity spread.

## Key takeaways

- $\Delta x\, \Delta p \ge \hbar/2$, with $\hbar = h/2\pi = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s. A localized wave needs a spread of wavelengths, like a short pulse needs bandwidth.
- Confinement costs energy: $KE \sim \hbar^2/(2m\Delta x^2)$, and for electrons $\hbar^2/2m = 0.0381$ eV·nm².
- The balance between confinement energy and electrical attraction sets the size of atoms (0.053 nm) and hydrogen's −13.6 eV.
- $\Delta E\, \Delta t \ge \hbar/2$: short-lived states and short pulses have spread-out energies.
- The effects are only noticeable for very light, very confined particles like electrons.
