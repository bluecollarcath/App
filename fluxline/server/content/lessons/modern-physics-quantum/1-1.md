## Why this course exists

Every transistor, LED, solar cell, laser diode and flash-memory chip works because of quantum mechanics. Classical physics, everything from Newton to Maxwell, predicts that a silicon crystal should either conduct like copper or insulate like glass. It does neither, and the reason is quantum. This course builds that understanding from the ground up, so that when Semiconductor Devices talks about energy bands and holes, you'll know where they come from.

Quantum mechanics has a reputation for being weird. It is, but it's also precise: its predictions match experiments to ten decimal places. We'll focus on the ideas you can calculate with, and on where they show up in real hardware.

## Light was "solved"... almost

By 1900, physicists were confident that light is a wave: an oscillating electric and magnetic field, exactly as you studied in E&M. Waves explained reflection, refraction, interference and diffraction. Two experiments refused to fit.

**1. Glowing hot objects (blackbody radiation).** Heat a piece of metal and it glows red, then orange, then white. Classical wave theory predicted that a hot object should radiate *infinite* energy at short wavelengths, the so-called ultraviolet catastrophe. In 1900 Max Planck fixed the math with a strange assumption: the oscillating charges in the hot object can only give off energy in chunks of size

$$
E = hf
$$

where $f$ is the frequency and $h$ is a new constant of nature, **Planck's constant**:

$$
h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}
$$

**Wien's law.** One useful result from Planck's theory: the wavelength where a hot object glows brightest is $\lambda_{\text{peak}} = \dfrac{2.898 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m·K}}{T}$. The Sun's surface (about 5800 K) peaks near 500 nm, green-yellow light. Your body (310 K) peaks near 9.3 µm, in the infrared, which is why thermal cameras work at those wavelengths.

**2. The photoelectric effect.** This is the experiment that forced the quantum idea on everyone, and it's the one we'll study closely.

## The photoelectric effect

Shine light on a clean metal surface, and electrons can be knocked out of it. Collect them with a nearby electrode and you get a current: this is how old light sensors and photomultiplier tubes work.

```circuit

caption: Light hits a metal surface and ejects electrons, which cross the gap to the collector and flow back through the ammeter (A) as a current. What matters is how the electrons' energy depends on the light.
```

Wave theory made clear predictions about this experiment. Every one of them failed:

| Wave theory predicted | What experiments showed |
|---|---|
| Brighter light gives each electron more energy. | Brighter light gives **more electrons**, but each one has the **same** maximum energy. |
| Any color works if it's bright enough. | Below a certain **threshold frequency**, no electrons come out at all, no matter how bright. |
| Dim light needs time to build up enough energy. | Electrons come out **instantly**, even in very dim light. |
| Frequency shouldn't matter much. | The electrons' maximum energy rises in a **straight line** with frequency. |

## Einstein's answer: photons

In 1905 Albert Einstein took Planck's idea seriously: light itself comes in packets, now called **photons**. Each photon carries energy

$$
E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
$$

where $c = 3.00 \times 10^8$ m/s is the speed of light and $\lambda$ is the wavelength. A photon hits one electron and gives it all its energy, or none. The electron needs a minimum energy $W$, the **work function**, just to escape the metal. Whatever is left becomes kinetic energy:

$$
KE_{\max} = hf - W
$$

This explains everything in the table:
- **Brighter light** means more photons, so more electrons, but each photon still has the same energy $hf$.
- **Below threshold** ($hf < W$), one photon can't free an electron, and electrons almost never absorb two at once.
- **Instant emission**: one photon is enough; nothing needs to build up.
- **The straight line**: $KE_{\max}$ rises with $f$ with slope $h$.

Einstein won the Nobel Prize for this, not for relativity.

## The electron-volt

Joules are awkward for single particles: a photon of red light carries about $3 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Physicists and engineers use the **electron-volt** (eV) instead: the energy one electron gains when it moves through a potential difference of 1 volt.

$$
1\ \text{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}
$$

It's a natural unit for electronics. An electron crossing a 3 V battery gains 3 eV. A silicon bandgap is 1.12 eV. A visible photon is about 1.8 to 3.1 eV.

**The shortcut every engineer uses.** Combining the constants, $hc = 1240$ eV·nm, so

$$
E\ (\text{eV}) = \frac{1240}{\lambda\ (\text{nm})}
$$

| Light | Wavelength | Photon energy |
|---|---|---|
| Fiber-optic telecom (infrared) | 1550 nm | 0.80 eV |
| Red | 700 nm | 1.77 eV |
| Green | 550 nm | 2.25 eV |
| Blue | 450 nm | 2.76 eV |
| Violet | 400 nm | 3.10 eV |
| UV-C germicidal lamp | 254 nm | 4.88 eV |

> **Example 1.** A red laser pointer emits 650 nm light. Find the energy of one photon, in eV and in joules.
>
> $E = \dfrac{1240}{650} = 1.91$ eV. In joules: $1.91 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} = 3.06 \times 10^{-19}$ J.

> **Example 2.** That laser puts out 1 mW (0.001 J each second). How many photons is that per second?
>
> $\dfrac{0.001\ \text{J/s}}{3.06 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{15}$ photons per second. That's why light looks smooth and continuous: the packets are tiny and incredibly numerous.

> **Example 3 (photoelectric).** Potassium has a work function of 2.30 eV. Violet light at 400 nm shines on it. What is the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electrons?
>
> Photon energy: $\dfrac{1240}{400} = 3.10$ eV. Then $KE_{\max} = 3.10 - 2.30 = 0.80$ eV.
>
> What's the longest wavelength that ejects any electrons? Set $hf = W$: $\lambda = \dfrac{1240}{2.30} \approx 539$ nm (green). Red light, however bright, does nothing.

```plot
x: 0, 15, frequency f (× 10¹⁴ Hz)
y: -0.5, 4, max. electron energy (eV)
f: max(0.41357*x - 2.3, 0) | potassium, W = 2.30 eV

vline: 5.56, threshold for K
mark: 7.49, 0.8, 400 nm violet: 0.80 eV
caption: Maximum electron energy versus light frequency. Both metals give lines with the same slope, Planck's constant $h$; they differ only in where they start (the work function). Below the threshold, no electrons come out.
```

**Measuring it in volts.** In the lab, you find $KE_{\max}$ by applying a reverse voltage that pushes electrons back. The voltage that just stops the fastest electrons is the **stopping voltage**, and in eV it equals $KE_{\max}$ directly: a 0.80 eV electron is stopped by 0.80 V. That's the beauty of the electron-volt.

## So is light a wave or a particle?

Both, depending on the experiment. Light travels and interferes like a wave, and it is absorbed and emitted in whole photons. This **wave-particle duality** is the first big idea of quantum mechanics, and next lesson you'll see that electrons have it too.

**Where you'll see photons in engineering:** photodiodes and camera sensors (each photon frees one electron), solar cells, fiber-optic receivers, LEDs and lasers (which emit one photon per electron), and UV lamps that break chemical bonds that visible light can't.

### Common mistakes

- **Using nm directly in $E = hc/\lambda$ with SI constants.** Either use the 1240 eV·nm shortcut with nm, or convert to meters with $h$ and $c$ in SI units.
- **Thinking brightness raises electron energy.** Brightness is the number of photons; energy per photon depends only on frequency.
- **Forgetting the work function.** The electron's kinetic energy is what's *left* after escaping: $hf - W$, not $hf$.

## Key takeaways

- Light comes in photons with energy $E = hf = hc/\lambda$; $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s.
- Engineer's shortcut: $E\ (\text{eV}) = 1240 / \lambda\ (\text{nm})$; 1 eV $= 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ J.
- Photoelectric effect: $KE_{\max} = hf - W$. Brightness changes the number of electrons, not their energy; below threshold, nothing happens.
- The stopping voltage in volts equals $KE_{\max}$ in eV.
- Light behaves as a wave when it travels and as particles when it's absorbed or emitted: wave-particle duality.
