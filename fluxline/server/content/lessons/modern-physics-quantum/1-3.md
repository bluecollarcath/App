## The fingerprint of an element

Pass the light from a neon sign or a sodium street lamp through a prism, and you don't get a smooth rainbow. You get a handful of sharp, bright lines at very specific colors. Every element has its own pattern of lines, its **spectrum**, as unique as a fingerprint. Astronomers use these lines to tell what distant stars are made of.

Classical physics couldn't explain the lines at all. In fact, it predicted that atoms shouldn't exist: an electron orbiting a nucleus is an accelerating charge, and accelerating charges radiate energy (that's how antennas work). The electron should spiral into the nucleus in about $10^{-11}$ seconds. Quantum ideas solve both problems.

## The Bohr model

In 1913 Niels Bohr proposed a model of hydrogen (one proton, one electron) with a radical rule: the electron can only occupy certain **allowed orbits**, each with a definite energy, and while it's in one of them it doesn't radiate.

De Broglie later explained why only certain orbits are allowed: the electron is a wave, and a wave wrapped around a circle must fit a whole number of wavelengths, or it cancels itself out. It's like a guitar string, which can only vibrate at notes where a whole number of half-waves fit between its ends.


The allowed energies of hydrogen turn out to be

$$
E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2} \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
$$

The whole number $n$ is the **principal quantum number**. (Quantum means "a definite amount": the energy can only take these values, nothing in between.)

| Level | Energy |
|---|---|
| $n = 1$ (ground state) | $-13.6$ eV |
| $n = 2$ | $-3.40$ eV |
| $n = 3$ | $-1.51$ eV |
| $n = 4$ | $-0.85$ eV |
| $n = \infty$ (electron freed) | $0$ eV |

**Why negative?** Zero energy means the electron is free, far from the proton and at rest. A bound electron has *less* energy than that, so its energy is negative. The lowest level, $n = 1$, is the **ground state**; the others are **excited states**.

**Ionization energy.** To pull the electron completely free from the ground state takes $0 - (-13.6) = 13.6$ eV. From $n = 2$, it only takes 3.40 eV.

## Jumping between levels makes light

An electron can drop from a higher level to a lower one by emitting a photon. Energy is conserved, so the photon carries away exactly the difference:

$$
E_{\text{photon}} = E_{\text{upper}} - E_{\text{lower}} \qquad \lambda = \frac{1240\ \text{eV·nm}}{E_{\text{photon}}}
$$

The reverse also happens: an atom can **absorb** a photon, but only one with exactly the right energy to lift the electron to a higher level. That's why a cool gas in front of a bright source makes dark lines at the same wavelengths a hot gas makes bright ones.

> **Example 1 (hydrogen's red line).** An electron drops from $n = 3$ to $n = 2$.
>
> $E_{\text{photon}} = -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89$ eV, so $\lambda = \dfrac{1240}{1.89} \approx 656$ nm.
>
> This is the deep red line that makes glowing hydrogen clouds in space look pink-red.

> **Example 2.** From $n = 2$ down to $n = 1$:
>
> $E_{\text{photon}} = -3.40 - (-13.6) = 10.2$ eV, so $\lambda = \dfrac{1240}{10.2} \approx 122$ nm, in the ultraviolet.

The visible lines of hydrogen all come from drops ending at $n = 2$ (656, 486, 434 and 410 nm): the **Balmer series**. Drops ending at $n = 1$ are all ultraviolet, and drops ending at $n = 3$ are all infrared.

## Beyond hydrogen

The formula $-13.6/n^2$ only works for hydrogen. Atoms with more electrons have more complicated level patterns (Unit 3 explains how they're organized). But the central idea is universal: **atoms have discrete energy levels, and light is emitted or absorbed in jumps between them.**

That one idea runs a lot of technology:
- **Sodium street lamps** glow yellow-orange at 589 nm (2.10 eV), a sodium transition.
- **Fluorescent tubes** contain mercury, whose strongest line is 254 nm UV. A phosphor coating on the glass absorbs the UV and re-emits visible light.
- **Atomic clocks**, which keep GPS and cellular networks in sync, count the frequency of a transition in cesium or rubidium atoms.
- **Lasers** depend on electrons dropping between specific levels (Unit 4).

**Where the Bohr model falls short.** Electrons don't really travel in neat circular orbits; they're spread out in "clouds" described by the wavefunction. The Bohr model gets hydrogen's energies right but little else. Quantum mechanics (Unit 2) replaces it, and when we solve the hydrogen atom properly in Unit 3, the same $-13.6/n^2$ energies come out.

### Common mistakes

- **Using one level's energy instead of the difference.** The photon energy is $E_{\text{upper}} - E_{\text{lower}}$, not $E_{\text{upper}}$.
- **Sign errors.** Subtract the more negative number: $-1.51 - (-3.40) = +1.89$ eV.
- **Forgetting the square.** $E_3 = -13.6/9$, not $-13.6/3$.

## Key takeaways

- Atoms have discrete energy levels; for hydrogen, $E_n = -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$.
- A level is allowed when the electron wave fits a whole number of wavelengths: a standing wave.
- Jumps between levels emit or absorb a photon with $E = E_{\text{upper}} - E_{\text{lower}}$, so $\lambda = 1240/\Delta E$ nm.
- Each element has its own line spectrum; hydrogen's visible lines end on $n = 2$.
- Ionization energy is the energy to lift the electron to $0$: 13.6 eV from hydrogen's ground state.
