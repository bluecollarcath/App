## Waves of what?

Unit 1 showed that electrons behave like waves. But a water wave is water moving, and a radio wave is electric and magnetic fields oscillating. What exactly is waving in an electron wave?

The answer, worked out by Max Born in 1926, is the central idea of quantum mechanics: the wave describes **probability**. The electron wave tells you where you're likely to find the electron if you look.

## The wavefunction

A particle in quantum mechanics is described by a **wavefunction**, written $\psi(x)$ (the Greek letter psi, pronounced "sigh"). It's a function of position, a bit like the voltage along a transmission line is a function of position. On its own, $\psi$ isn't something you can measure directly; it can even be negative, or in general a complex number.

What you can measure comes from its square:

$$
\text{probability density} = |\psi(x)|^2
$$

**Born's rule.** The probability of finding the particle between $x = a$ and $x = b$ is the area under $|\psi|^2$ over that range:

$$
P(a \le x \le b) = \int_a^b |\psi(x)|^2\, dx
$$

For a small range $\Delta x$ around a point, that's approximately $|\psi(x)|^2 \Delta x$: height times width.

Think of $|\psi|^2$ like a histogram of where the particle turns up. Prepare a thousand identical electrons in the same state, measure each one's position once, and plot the results: you get the shape of $|\psi|^2$.

## Normalization

The particle has to be *somewhere*, so the total probability over all space must be 1:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x)|^2\, dx = 1
$$

A wavefunction that satisfies this is **normalized**. If you find a wavefunction that has the right shape but the wrong size, you multiply it by a constant to fix the total area.

> **Example 1 (normalizing a flat wavefunction).** A particle is equally likely to be anywhere between $x = 0$ and $x = L$, and never outside. So $\psi(x) = A$ inside, and $0$ outside. Find $A$.
>
> $\displaystyle\int_0^L A^2\, dx = A^2 L = 1$, so $A = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{L}}$.
>
> For $L = 4$ nm, $A = 0.5$ nm$^{-1/2}$, and the probability density is $A^2 = 0.25$ per nm. The chance of finding it in any 1 nm stretch is $0.25 \times 1 = 0.25$, which makes sense: one quarter of the box.

## A real example: an electron in a box

In Lesson 2.3 you'll derive the wavefunctions of an electron trapped in a box of width $L$. Here are the answers now, so you can practice reading wavefunctions. The lowest-energy state is

$$
\psi_1(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\, \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) \qquad 0 \le x \le L
$$

and the next one up is $\psi_2(x) = \sqrt{\dfrac{2}{L}}\, \sin\!\left(\dfrac{2\pi x}{L}\right)$.

```plot
f: 2*sin(2*pi*x)^2 | ψ₂ squared: second state | dot | 4
hline: 0
caption: Wavefunctions in a box of width $L$ (heights in units of $1/\sqrt{L}$ and $1/L$). In the ground state the electron is most likely near the middle. In the second state $|\psi_2|^2$ has two peaks and is exactly zero at the center: the electron is never found there.
```

Notice three things:
- **$|\psi_1|^2$ peaks in the middle.** In the ground state, the electron is most likely near the center and unlikely near the walls.
- **$|\psi_2|^2$ is zero at the center.** In the second state there's a point, called a **node**, where the electron is *never* found, even though it spends equal time on both sides. Classical particles can't do that.
- **Both are normalized.** The area under each $|\psi|^2$ curve is exactly 1.

> **Example 2.** An electron is in the ground state of a box with $L = 1$ nm. What's the probability of finding it within 0.01 nm of the center?
>
> At the center, $|\psi_1|^2 = \dfrac{2}{L}\sin^2\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = 2$ per nm. The range is 0.02 nm wide (0.01 on each side), so $P \approx 2 \times 0.02 = 0.04$: a 4% chance.

> **Example 3 (using the integral).** In the ground state, what's the probability the electron is in the middle third of the box, from $L/3$ to $2L/3$?
>
> $\displaystyle P = \frac{2}{L}\int_{L/3}^{2L/3} \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right) dx = \left[\frac{x}{L} - \frac{1}{2\pi}\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi x}{L}\right)\right]_{L/3}^{2L/3}$
>
> $= \dfrac{1}{3} - \dfrac{1}{2\pi}\left(\sin\dfrac{4\pi}{3} - \sin\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \dfrac{1}{3} + \dfrac{1}{2\pi}(1.732) \approx 0.609$.
>
> About 61%, compared with 33% for a classical particle bouncing back and forth at constant speed.

## Expectation values: the average result

If you measure the position of many identically prepared particles, the average result is the **expectation value**:

$$
\langle x \rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\, |\psi(x)|^2\, dx
$$

It's a weighted average: each position counts in proportion to how likely it is. For the box states above, $|\psi|^2$ is symmetric about the middle, so $\langle x \rangle = L/2$ for every state, even $\psi_2$, where the electron is never actually found at $L/2$. The average of many measurements isn't always a possible result of one measurement, just like the average family has 2.5 children.

## Measurement and collapse

Here's the part that bothered Einstein. Before you measure, the electron in state $\psi_1$ has no definite position; it has a spread of possible positions given by $|\psi|^2$. When you measure, you get one definite answer, randomly, with those probabilities. Measure again right away and you get the same answer: the wavefunction has **collapsed** to a narrow spike at the place you found it.

The randomness is not a lack of knowledge about some hidden position. Experiments (which won the 2022 Nobel Prize) have ruled that out: nature is genuinely probabilistic at this level. But the probabilities themselves are predicted exactly, and with $10^{20}$ electrons in a chip, those probabilities become perfectly reliable currents. That's why quantum devices behave predictably.

## Superposition

Because the Schrödinger equation (coming soon) is linear, if $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ are both allowed states, so is any combination like $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\psi_1 + \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\psi_2$. This is **superposition**, the same principle you use in circuit analysis when you add up the effects of each source. In quantum mechanics, a particle in a superposition is genuinely in both states until measured; measuring its energy gives $E_1$ or $E_2$, each with probability $\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Superposition is the key resource in quantum computing (Lesson 4.4).

### Common mistakes

- **Treating $\psi$ as a probability.** The probability density is $|\psi|^2$. $\psi$ itself can be negative.
- **Forgetting the width.** $|\psi|^2$ is a density (probability per unit length). You must multiply by a length, or integrate, to get a probability.
- **Expecting $\langle x \rangle$ to be a likely result.** It's an average, and it can fall where the particle is never found.

## Key takeaways

- A particle is described by a wavefunction $\psi(x)$; $|\psi(x)|^2$ is the probability density.
- Probability of finding it in a range = area under $|\psi|^2$; for a narrow range, $|\psi|^2 \Delta x$.
- Normalization: total area under $|\psi|^2$ is 1.
- $\langle x \rangle = \int x |\psi|^2 dx$ is the average of many measurements.
- Measurement gives one random result with those probabilities and collapses the wavefunction; superposition lets a particle be in several states at once.
