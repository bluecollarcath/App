## The question classical physics couldn't answer

Copper conducts electricity about $10^{20}$ times better than glass. Silicon sits in between, and its conductivity can be changed a millionfold by adding a few parts per million of phosphorus or boron. Classical physics had no explanation for any of this. Everything in this unit, and the entire semiconductor industry, rests on the answer: **in a solid, the energy levels of atoms spread into bands, separated by gaps where no electron states exist.**

## Two atoms: one level becomes two

Start with two identical atoms far apart, each with an electron in the same level. Bring them closer. The electron waves begin to overlap, and just like two coupled LC circuits or two pendulums joined by a spring, the single shared frequency splits into two:

- a **bonding** combination, where the waves add between the atoms, slightly **lower** in energy;
- an **antibonding** combination, where they cancel between the atoms, slightly **higher**.

This is exactly how a chemical bond forms: two electrons (one spin up, one down) settle into the lower, bonding level, and the molecule has less energy than two separate atoms.

## Many atoms: levels become bands

With $N$ atoms, each original level splits into $N$ levels. A grain of silicon has around $10^{22}$ atoms, so each atomic level splits into $10^{22}$ levels packed into a range of a few eV. They're so close together that, for all practical purposes, they form a continuous **energy band**.

Each atomic level produces its own band, and between bands there can be **forbidden gaps**: ranges of energy where the crystal simply has no electron states. An electron can't sit in the gap any more than a guitar string can play a note between its harmonics.

## Why gaps appear: waves in a periodic crystal

There's a second, wave-based way to see the gaps, and it's the one semiconductor engineers use. An electron moving through a crystal is a wave passing a perfectly regular row of atoms, spaced a distance $a$ apart. When its wavelength fits the spacing just right ($\lambda = 2a$), the small reflections from every atom add up in step and the wave is completely reflected, like light from a multilayer mirror or a signal from a periodic grating. At exactly that wavelength, the electron can't travel as a single wave; it forms two standing waves, one with its peaks on the atoms (lower energy) and one with its peaks between them (higher energy). The energy difference between those two standing waves is the **bandgap**.

Engineers draw the result as a **band diagram**: just the two bands that matter, with energy upward.

## Filling the bands: conductor, insulator, or semiconductor

Pauli exclusion fills the band states from the bottom, two electrons per state, until all the electrons are placed. What happens at the top of the filling decides everything.

**Conductors (metals).** The highest occupied band is only partly full. There are empty states just above the filled ones, so an applied voltage can nudge electrons into slightly faster-moving states: current flows freely. Copper, aluminum and gold are like this.

**Insulators.** The highest occupied band, the **valence band**, is completely full, and the next band up, the **conduction band**, is completely empty, with a large gap between them. A full band can't carry current: for every electron moving right there's one moving left, and there are no empty states to shift into. To conduct, an electron must jump the whole gap, and at room temperature essentially none can.

**Semiconductors.** The same structure as an insulator, but with a **small** gap, around 1 eV. A few electrons get enough thermal energy to jump into the conduction band, leaving behind empty states in the valence band (holes). Both can carry current. The conductivity is modest, strongly temperature-dependent, and, crucially, **controllable** by doping (next lesson).

| Material | Bandgap | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Germanium (Ge) | 0.66 eV | semiconductor |
| Silicon (Si) | 1.12 eV | semiconductor |
| Gallium arsenide (GaAs) | 1.42 eV | semiconductor |
| Silicon carbide (4H-SiC) | 3.26 eV | wide-gap semiconductor |
| Gallium nitride (GaN) | 3.4 eV | wide-gap semiconductor |
| Diamond (C) | 5.5 eV | insulator |
| Silicon dioxide (SiO₂) | about 9 eV | insulator |

**Why the gap is so decisive: temperature.** Thermal energy at room temperature is about $kT = 0.0259$ eV (with $k = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/K). The chance of an electron being thermally excited across a gap scales roughly as $e^{-E_g/2kT}$. For silicon, that's $e^{-0.56/0.0259} \approx 4 \times 10^{-10}$, small but enough to give about $10^{10}$ free electrons per cm³. For diamond it's about $10^{-46}$: none at all. An exponential again turns a factor of 5 in gap into a factor of $10^{36}$ in carriers.

**Wide-bandgap semiconductors** like SiC and GaN can handle higher voltages and temperatures without thermally generated carriers swamping the device. They're behind modern EV inverters, fast chargers and 5G power amplifiers; you'll meet them in Power Electronics.

## The gap and light

A photon can lift an electron from the valence band to the conduction band only if its energy is at least the bandgap: $hf \ge E_g$, or

$$
\lambda \le \frac{1240}{E_g}\ \text{nm}
$$

- **Silicon** ($E_g = 1.12$ eV) absorbs all light shorter than 1107 nm, so it's opaque to visible light and works as a solar cell and camera sensor. It's transparent to longer infrared, which is why silicon photonics chips use 1310 and 1550 nm light.
- **Glass** ($E_g \approx 9$ eV) can't absorb visible photons at all, which is why it's transparent.
- **Germanium** ($E_g = 0.66$ eV) absorbs out to 1880 nm, so it makes good infrared detectors.

### Common mistakes

- **Thinking a full band conducts best.** A completely full band carries no net current; there are no empty states for electrons to move into.
- **Calling semiconductors "bad conductors."** Their defining feature is a small gap that makes their conductivity controllable, not just a medium value.
- **Mixing up bands and atomic levels.** A band is a huge number of closely spaced levels formed when many atoms' levels split.

## Key takeaways

- When atoms join, each level splits; with $10^{22}$ atoms, levels merge into bands separated by forbidden gaps.
- Gaps also follow from waves in a periodic crystal: at $\lambda = 2a$ the electron forms standing waves with two different energies.
- Partly filled band: metal. Full valence band and empty conduction band: insulator (big gap) or semiconductor (gap around 1 eV).
- Room-temperature $kT \approx 0.026$ eV; thermal excitation across a gap goes roughly as $e^{-E_g/2kT}$.
- A material absorbs photons with $\lambda \le 1240/E_g$ nm; silicon absorbs below 1107 nm.
