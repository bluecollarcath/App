## Electrons and photons trading places

Lesson 1.3 showed atoms emitting light when electrons drop between levels. A semiconductor does the same thing between its bands: an electron in the conduction band falls into a hole in the valence band and gives off a photon with about the bandgap's energy. Run it the other way, and a photon lifts an electron across the gap and creates a free electron-hole pair. Those two processes, **emission** and **absorption** across the gap, are the physics of every LED, laser diode, solar cell, camera sensor and fiber-optic receiver.

## The rule of thumb: color equals bandgap

Electrons and holes quickly settle near the edges of their bands, so the photons they produce when they recombine have energy close to $E_g$:

$$
\lambda \approx \frac{1240}{E_g\ (\text{eV})}\ \text{nm}
$$

| Material | Bandgap | Light |
|---|---|---|
| InGaN (indium gallium nitride) | about 2.7 eV | blue, 460 nm |
| AlGaInP | about 1.9 eV | red, 650 nm |
| GaAs | 1.42 eV | near infrared, 873 nm (remote controls) |
| InGaAsP | about 0.8 eV | 1550 nm (fiber-optic lasers) |
| Silicon | 1.12 eV | barely emits (see below) |

By mixing elements (like adjusting the indium in InGaN), engineers tune the bandgap and choose the color. Finding a material for efficient **blue** LEDs took decades; it was solved with gallium nitride in the 1990s and won the 2014 Nobel Prize, because blue made white LED lighting possible.

**White LEDs** are blue LEDs coated with a yellow phosphor. Some blue light passes through; the rest is absorbed and re-emitted as yellow; together they look white.

**Why silicon doesn't make LEDs.** In silicon, the lowest point of the conduction band and the highest point of the valence band occur at different electron momenta: it has an **indirect gap**. A photon carries almost no momentum, so an electron can't drop straight down while emitting one; it needs a crystal vibration to take up the difference, which is unlikely. The electron usually loses its energy as heat instead. GaAs, InGaN and the other LED materials have a **direct gap**, where the transition happens easily. (Silicon still *absorbs* light well enough for solar cells and cameras, especially with a thick enough layer.)

**LED forward voltage.** To create a photon of energy $E_g$, each electron must be given at least $E_g$ of energy, which means the LED needs a forward voltage of at least about $E_g/e$. That's why red LEDs need about 1.8 to 2 V and blue LEDs about 3 V, a fact you'll use every time you pick a current-limiting resistor.

> **Example 1.** An LED is made from a material with $E_g = 2.25$ eV. What color is it, and roughly what forward voltage does it need?
>
> $\lambda = 1240/2.25 \approx 551$ nm: green. Forward voltage at least about 2.25 V (in practice a bit more).

## Lasers: stimulated emission

In 1917, Einstein realized that a photon passing an excited atom (or an electron sitting in a conduction band) can **stimulate** it to emit a second photon that is an exact copy: same energy, same direction, same phase. One photon becomes two, two become four, and so on. That's **L**ight **A**mplification by **S**timulated **E**mission of **R**adiation.

Two conditions make a laser:
1. **Population inversion.** Normally, absorption beats stimulated emission because most electrons are in the lower state. A laser diode is pumped with enough current that, at the lasing energy, more electrons are waiting to drop down than there are empty states waiting to absorb. Then emission wins. Below a **threshold current** it glows like an LED; above threshold, it lases.
2. **An optical cavity.** Mirrors at each end (in a laser diode, simply the cleaved ends of the crystal) bounce the light back and forth through the gain region so it's amplified many times. One mirror lets a little light out: the beam.

```plot

y: 0, 14, light output (mW)
f: 0.02*x + u(x-20)*(x-20)*0.6 | laser diode
vline: 20, threshold current
caption: Light output of a laser diode. Below threshold it emits weakly and in all directions, like an LED. Above threshold, stimulated emission takes over and the output climbs steeply as a coherent beam.
```

The result is light that's single-color, tightly directional and coherent (all in step). Laser diodes, usually built on finite quantum wells (Lesson 2.4) for efficiency, drive fiber-optic internet links, barcode scanners, LiDAR and laser printers.

## Solar cells and photodiodes: running it backward

A photon with $E \ge E_g$ is absorbed and frees an electron-hole pair. A p-n junction's built-in electric field (from Lesson 4.2's Fermi-level alignment) sweeps the electron one way and the hole the other, producing current: that's a **photodiode**, and with a large area in sunlight, a **solar cell**.

**Two ways energy is lost in a solar cell:**
- **Photons below the gap** pass straight through. Silicon wastes all sunlight beyond 1107 nm.
- **Photons above the gap** create a pair, but the excess energy $E_{\text{photon}} - E_g$ is lost as heat within picoseconds. A blue photon in silicon wastes more than half its energy.

> **Example 2.** A 500 nm photon is absorbed in silicon. What fraction of its energy is lost as heat?
>
> $E = 1240/500 = 2.48$ eV. Excess: $2.48 - 1.12 = 1.36$ eV, which is $1.36/2.48 = 55\%$.

Balancing these two losses, a single-junction cell has a theoretical maximum efficiency of about 33%, best at a bandgap near 1.3 to 1.4 eV (the **Shockley-Queisser limit**). Silicon's 1.12 eV is close to ideal, which, along with its abundance, is why it dominates: commercial panels reach about 20 to 24%. **Multi-junction** cells stack materials with different gaps, each harvesting its slice of the spectrum, and exceed 40% (used on satellites).

**Photodiode responsivity.** Each absorbed photon gives at most one electron of current. If a fraction $\eta$ (the **quantum efficiency**) of photons produce an electron, the current per watt of light is

$$
R = \frac{\eta\, \lambda\ (\text{nm})}{1240}\ \text{A/W}
$$

> **Example 3.** A silicon photodiode with $\eta = 0.8$ receives 850 nm light. $R = \dfrac{0.8 \times 850}{1240} = 0.55$ A/W. One microwatt of light gives 0.55 µA of current.

Longer wavelengths give more current per watt because each photon carries less energy, so a watt contains more photons, up to the point where the photon energy drops below the gap and the response falls to zero.

### Common mistakes

- **Expecting silicon LEDs.** Silicon's indirect gap makes it a very poor light emitter.
- **Assuming all photon energy becomes electricity.** Only about $E_g$ per photon is useful; the excess is heat.
- **Forgetting the threshold.** A laser diode below its threshold current is just a dim LED.

## Key takeaways

- Recombination across the gap emits $\lambda \approx 1240/E_g$ nm; absorption needs $\lambda \le 1240/E_g$.
- Direct-gap materials (GaAs, InGaN, AlGaInP) emit light efficiently; indirect-gap silicon doesn't. LED forward voltage is at least about $E_g/e$.
- Lasers use stimulated emission, population inversion above a threshold current, and a mirror cavity.
- Solar cells lose sub-gap photons and the excess energy of high-energy photons; single-junction limit about 33%, best near 1.3 to 1.4 eV.
- Photodiode responsivity: $R = \eta\lambda/1240$ A/W.
