## The equation that runs the quantum world

Newton's $F = ma$ tells you how a classical particle moves. Its quantum replacement, written down by Erwin Schrödinger in 1926, tells you what the wavefunction $\psi$ is. Solve it, and you get the allowed energies of an electron in an atom, a molecule, a quantum dot or a crystal. Every band diagram in your semiconductor course comes from solving this equation.

## The time-independent Schrödinger equation

For a particle of mass $m$ moving along $x$ with potential energy $V(x)$, the states with a definite energy $E$ satisfy

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x)\,\psi = E\,\psi
$$

Read it as an energy balance:
- $-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}$ is the **kinetic energy** term. The second derivative measures how sharply $\psi$ curves; a wavefunction that wiggles fast (short wavelength, high momentum) has high kinetic energy.
- $V(x)\psi$ is the **potential energy** term, set by the situation: the pull of a nucleus, an electric field, the edges of a material.
- $E$ is the total energy, and the equation says kinetic plus potential equals total.

It's a second-order linear differential equation, exactly the kind you solved in Differential Equations. The physics is in the boundary conditions.

## The particle in a box

The simplest useful problem: an electron trapped between two impenetrable walls, a distance $L$ apart. Inside, it moves freely ($V = 0$). The walls are infinitely high, so the electron can never be found outside, and $\psi = 0$ there.

This "infinite square well" is a surprisingly good model for an electron in a thin semiconductor layer, a nanowire or a quantum dot.

**Step 1: inside the box.** With $V = 0$:

$$
\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = -k^2 \psi \qquad \text{where } k = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}
$$

That's the equation of simple harmonic motion (the same one as an LC circuit). Its general solution is

$$
\psi(x) = A\sin(kx) + B\cos(kx)
$$

**Step 2: the left wall.** $\psi$ must be continuous, and it's zero outside, so $\psi(0) = 0$. Since $\cos(0) = 1$, that forces $B = 0$, leaving $\psi = A\sin(kx)$.

**Step 3: the right wall.** $\psi(L) = 0$ means $\sin(kL) = 0$. Sine is zero only at whole multiples of $\pi$:

$$
kL = n\pi \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
$$

($n = 0$ would make $\psi = 0$ everywhere: no particle at all.)

**Step 4: the energies.** Solve $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ for $E$ and substitute $k = n\pi/L$:

$$
E_n = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m} = \frac{n^2 \pi^2 \hbar^2}{2 m L^2} = \frac{n^2 h^2}{8 m L^2}
$$

**Step 5: normalize.** $\displaystyle\int_0^L A^2\sin^2\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx = A^2 \frac{L}{2} = 1$ gives $A = \sqrt{2/L}$, the wavefunctions you used in Lesson 2.1:

$$
\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\,\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)
$$

The boundary conditions did all the work. Requiring the wave to vanish at both walls allows only whole numbers of half-wavelengths to fit, exactly like the notes of a guitar string, or the resonant modes of a shorted transmission line. **Confinement plus wave behavior equals quantized energy.**

## Working with the energies

For an electron, the combination $\dfrac{h^2}{8m}$ is $0.376$ eV·nm², so

$$
E_n = 0.376\ \text{eV} \times \frac{n^2}{L^2} \qquad (L \text{ in nm})
$$

| Box width | $E_1$ | $E_2$ | $E_3$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 nm | 0.094 eV | 0.376 eV | 0.846 eV |
| 1 nm | 0.376 eV | 1.50 eV | 3.38 eV |
| 0.5 nm | 1.50 eV | 6.02 eV | 13.5 eV |

Three patterns to notice:
- **Energies grow as $n^2$.** The levels get farther apart as you go up.
- **Smaller boxes mean higher energies,** growing as $1/L^2$. Halve the box and every energy quadruples. That's the confinement energy from Lesson 2.2, now exact.
- **The lowest energy isn't zero.** Even in its ground state, a confined particle can't sit still. $E_1$ is called the **zero-point energy**.

> **Example 1.** An electron is trapped in a 1 nm box. It drops from $n = 2$ to $n = 1$. What photon is emitted?
>
> $E_2 - E_1 = 0.376(4 - 1) = 1.13$ eV, so $\lambda = \dfrac{1240}{1.13} \approx 1100$ nm, near infrared.

> **Example 2.** How wide must the box be for the ground state to be 1.0 eV?
>
> $1.0 = \dfrac{0.376}{L^2}$, so $L = \sqrt{0.376} = 0.61$ nm.

## Quantum dots: tuning color with size

A **quantum dot** is a semiconductor crystal only a few nanometers across. Its electrons are confined in all three directions, so their energy levels behave like a particle in a box. The light a dot emits depends on its size: **smaller dots emit bluer light** (higher energy, since $E \propto 1/L^2$), and larger dots emit redder light.

Quantum dots made from the same material can therefore glow any color, just by controlling their size during manufacturing. That's the technology inside "QLED" and quantum-dot TVs, where a blue backlight excites red and green dots for very pure colors. (In real dots the numbers also depend on the material's bandgap and the electron's effective mass in the crystal, Lesson 4.2, but the $1/L^2$ trend holds.)

The same physics shows up in modern transistors. A FinFET's channel is only a few nanometers thick, so electrons in it have quantized energies that shift the threshold voltage. Device engineers have to include this in their models.

## The time-dependent equation (briefly)

The full Schrödinger equation also says how $\psi$ changes in time. For a state with definite energy, the answer is simple: $\psi$ just oscillates in phase at frequency $E/\hbar$, while $|\psi|^2$ stays constant. That's why these are called **stationary states**: an electron in one of them doesn't radiate and stays put forever, unless something disturbs it. That's the precise reason atoms are stable.

### Common mistakes

- **Letting $n = 0$.** It gives $\psi = 0$ everywhere, which means no particle. The ground state is $n = 1$.
- **Forgetting the square on $n$ or on $L$.** $E \propto n^2/L^2$.
- **Using $0.376$ eV·nm² for something other than a free electron's mass.** It's $h^2/8m$ for an electron; other particles have other masses.

## Key takeaways

- The time-independent Schrödinger equation: $-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + V\psi = E\psi$ (kinetic + potential = total).
- In a box of width $L$: $\psi_n = \sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)$ and $E_n = \dfrac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}$; for electrons, $E_n = 0.376\, n^2/L^2$ eV with $L$ in nm.
- Quantization comes from boundary conditions: only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit.
- Energies grow as $n^2$ and as $1/L^2$; the lowest energy (zero-point energy) is never zero.
- Quantum dots use size to tune color: smaller dots emit bluer light.
