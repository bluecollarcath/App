## One more quantum number

In 1922, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach fired a beam of silver atoms through a non-uniform magnetic field. A tiny magnet passing through such a field gets pushed up or down depending on how it's oriented. Classically, randomly oriented magnets should spread into a continuous smear. Instead the beam split cleanly into **exactly two** spots.

The explanation, found a few years later, is that the electron has its own built-in angular momentum and magnetic moment, called **spin**, and it can only point two ways: "up" or "down" along any axis you measure.

## Electron spin

Spin is a fourth quantum number, $m_s$, with only two possible values:

$$
m_s = +\tfrac{1}{2}\ (\text{spin up}) \quad \text{or} \quad m_s = -\tfrac{1}{2}\ (\text{spin down})
$$

The spin angular momentum along the measured axis is $S_z = m_s\hbar = \pm\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar$.

It's tempting to picture the electron as a tiny spinning ball, and that picture helps with intuition, but it's not literally true: the electron has no measurable size, and a ball would have to spin faster than light to produce that much angular momentum. Spin is an intrinsic property, like charge or mass.

**Spin makes every electron a magnet.** Its magnetic moment is about one **Bohr magneton**:

$$
\mu_B = 9.274 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{J/T} = 5.788 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{eV/T}
$$

In a magnetic field $B$, spin-up and spin-down electrons have slightly different energies, separated by

$$
\Delta E = 2\mu_B B
$$

> **Example 1.** In a 1.0 T field (a strong permanent magnet), the spin splitting is $\Delta E = 2(5.788 \times 10^{-5})(1.0) = 1.16 \times 10^{-4}$ eV. A photon with exactly that energy has frequency $f = \Delta E / h \approx 28$ GHz, a microwave. Flipping electron spins with microwaves is called **electron spin resonance**, used to study materials and defects in semiconductors.

## The complete address of an electron

Every electron in an atom now has four quantum numbers: $n$, $l$, $m_l$ and $m_s$. Each orbital (each $n, l, m_l$ combination) can hold one spin-up and one spin-down electron, so level $n$ holds

$$
2n^2 \text{ electrons}: \quad 2, 8, 18, 32, \ldots
$$

## The Pauli exclusion principle

In 1925 Wolfgang Pauli stated the rule that structures all of matter:

> **No two electrons in the same system can have the same four quantum numbers.**

Each "address" holds at most one electron, which means each orbital holds at most two (one up, one down). Electrons (and protons and neutrons, all particles with half-integer spin) obey this rule; they're called **fermions**.

Without Pauli exclusion, every electron in every atom would pile into the lowest 1s orbital. All atoms would be tiny and nearly identical; chemistry wouldn't exist. With it, electrons fill orbitals one by one, from the lowest energy up, and the outermost electrons determine how each element behaves.

The superscripts count electrons in each subshell. Elements in the same column of the periodic table have the same number of valence electrons and similar chemistry.

**This table is the key to semiconductor doping.** Silicon and germanium have four valence electrons, so each atom bonds to four neighbors in a perfect crystal with no electrons to spare. Replace one silicon atom with phosphorus (five valence electrons), and there's one electron left over, free to carry current: that's **n-type** silicon. Replace one with boron (three), and one bond is missing an electron, leaving a **hole**: that's **p-type**. Lesson 4.2 picks this up.

## Spin in technology

- **MRI.** Protons have spin too. In a magnetic field, their spins precess at a frequency proportional to the field: 42.58 MHz per tesla. A 1.5 T hospital scanner works at about 64 MHz, a 3 T scanner at about 128 MHz, right in the radio band. The scanner sends RF pulses to flip spins and listens to the signal as they relax; how fast they relax depends on the tissue.
- **Hard-drive read heads** use **giant magnetoresistance**: a layered metal film whose resistance depends on whether the spins in adjacent magnetic layers line up. That made today's high-density drives possible (2007 Nobel Prize).
- **MRAM**, a nonvolatile memory built into some microcontrollers, stores bits in the spin orientation of magnetic layers and reads them through a tunnel barrier: spin and tunneling together.
- **Magnets.** Iron is magnetic because the spins of many of its electrons line up, and Pauli exclusion is part of why they prefer to.

### Common mistakes

- **Giving each orbital one electron.** Each orbital holds two: one spin up, one spin down.
- **Using $n^2$ for the capacity of a level.** With spin it's $2n^2$.
- **Imagining spin as literal rotation.** It's intrinsic angular momentum with only two possible measured values.

## Key takeaways

- Electrons have spin: intrinsic angular momentum with two values, $m_s = \pm\tfrac{1}{2}$, and a magnetic moment of about $\mu_B = 5.788 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/T.
- In a field $B$, spin up and down differ by $\Delta E = 2\mu_B B$ (about 28 GHz per tesla).
- Pauli exclusion: no two electrons share all four quantum numbers, so each orbital holds two electrons and level $n$ holds $2n^2$.
- Filling orbitals in energy order builds the periodic table; valence electrons set chemistry.
- Silicon and germanium have 4 valence electrons; phosphorus (5) makes n-type silicon, boron (3) makes p-type.
