## From explaining nature to engineering it

Everything so far in this course has used quantum mechanics to explain how materials and devices behave. Quantum computing tries something more ambitious: building machines that *process information* using superposition and entanglement directly. It's one of the most hyped technologies of the decade, so this lesson aims for an honest picture: what a qubit is, what quantum computers could genuinely do better, what they can't, and why they're so hard to build. It's also a field that hires a lot of electrical engineers, because the hardest problems are microwave design, cryogenics and control electronics.

## Bits and qubits

A classical bit is either 0 or 1: a transistor is off or on.

A **qubit** is a quantum system with two states, labeled $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. (The angle brackets, called "kets," are just notation for "the state named 0.") Like the electron in Lesson 2.1, it can be in a **superposition**:

$$
|\psi\rangle = \alpha\,|0\rangle + \beta\,|1\rangle
$$

where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are amplitudes (in general, complex numbers). When you **measure** the qubit, you get 0 with probability $|\alpha|^2$ or 1 with probability $|\beta|^2$, and the superposition collapses to whichever you got. The probabilities must add to one:

$$
|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
$$

> **Example 1.** A qubit is in the state $0.6\,|0\rangle + 0.8\,|1\rangle$. Check that it's valid and find the probability of measuring 1.
>
> $0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1$. Valid. $P(1) = 0.8^2 = 0.64$.

A qubit isn't "secretly 0 or 1 and we don't know which." Before measurement, the amplitudes can **interfere**, like the electron waves in the double slit, and that interference is what quantum algorithms exploit.

## Many qubits: the exponential state space

Two classical bits hold one of four values: 00, 01, 10 or 11. Two qubits can be in a superposition of all four at once, described by **four** amplitudes. In general, $n$ qubits need $2^n$ amplitudes:

| Qubits | Amplitudes to describe the state |
|---|---|
| 10 | 1,024 |
| 50 | about $10^{15}$ (roughly 18 petabytes to store on a classical computer) |
| 300 | about $10^{90}$, more than the number of atoms in the observable universe |

This is why quantum systems are hard to simulate classically, and why a quantum computer might be good at simulating them. But there's a catch: **a measurement only returns $n$ ordinary bits.** You can't read out all $2^n$ amplitudes. A useful quantum algorithm must arrange interference so that wrong answers cancel and the right answer is likely when you measure.

## Entanglement

Two qubits can be **entangled**: in a joint state that can't be described as two separate qubits. The standard example is

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\right)
$$

Measure either qubit and you get 0 or 1 at random, 50/50. But the two results always **agree**, however far apart the qubits are. This correlation is stronger than anything classical physics allows (that's what the 2022 Nobel Prize experiments proved). It can't be used to send messages faster than light, because each individual result is random. It's a resource that quantum algorithms, quantum cryptography and quantum error correction rely on.

## Quantum gates

Quantum programs are sequences of **gates** that rotate qubit states, much as logic gates transform bits:
- **X gate** (the quantum NOT): swaps $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$.
- **Hadamard (H) gate**: turns $|0\rangle$ into the equal superposition $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$, a 50/50 coin.
- **CNOT** (controlled-NOT): flips the second qubit if the first is 1. An H followed by a CNOT creates the entangled state above.

In hardware, gates are usually precisely shaped **microwave pulses**. A pulse at the qubit's resonant frequency rotates its state steadily from $|0\rangle$ toward $|1\rangle$ and back, an effect called a **Rabi oscillation**. Choosing the pulse length chooses the gate.

## What quantum computers are (and aren't) good for

**Genuinely promising:**
- **Simulating molecules and materials.** Designing catalysts, batteries and drugs requires solving quantum mechanics for many interacting electrons, exponentially hard classically. This was Richard Feynman's original motivation.
- **Factoring large numbers (Shor's algorithm).** It could break RSA and elliptic-curve encryption, which is why the world is already migrating to **post-quantum cryptography**: new encryption standards designed to resist quantum attacks.
- **Searching unstructured data (Grover's algorithm).** It finds one item among $N$ in about $\tfrac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{N}$ steps instead of about $N/2$ on average. For a million items, that's about 785 steps instead of 500,000. A real but modest (square-root) speedup.

**Not a general speedup.** Quantum computers won't run your web browser, spreadsheet or video game faster. For most everyday tasks there's no known quantum advantage at all, and classical computers will remain far cheaper and faster. Claims that quantum computers "try every answer at once" are misleading: you only get one measured answer, so the algorithm has to be cleverly designed.

## Why they're so hard to build

**Decoherence.** A qubit's superposition is destroyed by any uncontrolled interaction with its surroundings: a stray photon, a vibration, a fluctuating charge. Today's best qubits keep their coherence for microseconds to milliseconds, long enough for only thousands of gate operations. Every gate also has a small error rate (around 0.1% to 1%).

**Error correction** spreads one reliable **logical qubit** across many physical qubits, continuously checking for and fixing errors without measuring the protected information. Estimates suggest hundreds to thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit, and millions of physical qubits for something like breaking RSA. Today's machines have hundreds to a few thousand physical qubits. Early demonstrations of logical qubits that outperform their physical parts have been reported, but large, fault-tolerant machines are still years of engineering away.

**Leading hardware approaches:**
- **Superconducting circuits** (Google, IBM): tiny LC resonators made nonlinear with a Josephson junction (two superconductors separated by a thin tunnel barrier, Lesson 3.1). They operate near 5 GHz and must be cooled to about 15 millikelvin in a dilution refrigerator.
- **Trapped ions**: individual atoms held in electromagnetic traps, controlled with lasers. Very high quality, but slower.
- **Neutral atoms, photons and spin qubits in silicon** are also being pursued; silicon spin qubits could someday reuse chip-fab technology.

> **Example 2 (why so cold?).** A 5 GHz superconducting qubit's energy step is $hf = (4.136 \times 10^{-15})(5 \times 10^9) = 2.1 \times 10^{-5}$ eV. At room temperature, $kT = 0.026$ eV, about 1000 times bigger, so heat would scramble the qubit instantly. At 15 mK, $kT = 1.3 \times 10^{-6}$ eV, about 16 times *smaller* than the qubit's step, so thermal noise rarely flips it.

### Common mistakes

- **"A qubit is 0 and 1 at the same time, so it computes everything at once."** You get one measured result; usefulness comes from interference, not reading out everything.
- **Forgetting probabilities are squared amplitudes.** $P(1) = |\beta|^2$, not $\beta$.
- **Thinking entanglement sends signals.** Each measured result is random; only the correlation is special.

## Key takeaways

- A qubit's state is $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ with $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$; measurement gives 0 or 1 with those probabilities.
- $n$ qubits need $2^n$ amplitudes, but measurement returns only $n$ bits; algorithms rely on interference.
- Entangled qubits give perfectly correlated results but can't send signals.
- Real advantages: simulating molecules and materials, factoring (Shor; hence post-quantum cryptography), square-root speedup for search (Grover). No general speedup for everyday computing.
- Decoherence and gate errors are the main obstacles; error correction needs many physical qubits per logical qubit. Superconducting qubits run near 5 GHz at about 15 mK.
